First stop: a world that may be built differently from Earth at the deepest level — richer in carbon, under pressures that can favour exotic forms of carbon… including diamond-like material in models of some carbon-rich planets.
Say that slowly.
Not a jewellery advert. A planet where the chemistry of rock itself may lean toward carbon, and where extreme pressure can rearrange that carbon into structures we associate with gemstones.
On Earth, carbon mostly lives in softer forms — graphite in pencils, organic chains in life. Oxygen steals much of the bonding game in our crust. But if a planet forms with less oxygen and more carbon in its building blocks, the interior maths changes. Under enough pressure, carbon’s favourite shape can become a rigid crystal lattice — the same geometry we call diamond.
We have not walked there. We infer from star composition, planet mass, planet radius, and physics under crushing force. Some famous candidates and thought-experiments — worlds like the carbon-planet idea tied to systems such as fifty-five Cancri — made the public imagination race ahead of the photographs we do not have.
Is every glittering exoplanet a diamond beach? No. Models disagree. Some worlds may be carbon-rich but still hide their carbon in other minerals. Some may be gas-dominated giants with exotic chemistry we barely name yet.
That is fine… if we keep the honesty.
Orbit’s cream eyes drink the glitter anyway. Because even a careful model of a diamond-leaning crust is enough to rewrite what “ground” can mean.
Standing here would not be standing on granite. It would be standing on a chemistry lesson written in facets — hard, bright, and utterly alien to a world like Earth.
Alien worlds do not have to be alive to be astonishing. Sometimes the astonishment is geology wearing a tuxedo.

Next: one of the strangest weather forecasts in astronomy.
There is a famous blue-ish hot world — often discussed with HD 189733 b — where models and observations paint a brutal picture: scorching heat, winds that can scream at thousands of kilometres per hour… and silicate particles that may condense and fall as a kind of glass rain.
Not gentle drizzle.
Sideways.
On Earth, rain falls down because gravity wins against soft air. On a world like this, the atmosphere is a runaway engine. Heat from a nearby star drives furious circulation. The dayside can be hot enough that minerals behave like vapour. Cooler regions — or shocked flows in the wind — can let those minerals condense into tiny solid grains.
Those grains are not water. They can be silicates — the same family of stuff that makes sand and glass on Earth — lofted, swept, and driven nearly horizontally in the jet-stream violence of a hot atmosphere.
Picture a storm where the droplets are microscopic shards, and the wind is a highway. That is the HD 189733 b story as many papers tell it: a hot Jupiter-class world so close to its star that “weather” becomes a chemistry experiment in motion.
Is every detail certain? No. Exoplanet atmospheres are hard. Instruments argue. Models improve. Headlines sometimes run faster than papers.
But the core wonder is solid enough to teach: alien weather is not Earth weather with a filter. It can be chemistry and dynamics pushed into a regime our umbrellas never prepared for.
Orbit files a mental note: do not open a picnic blanket here.
He also files a quieter note: worlds like this are why astronomers chase spectra. A blue planet is not automatically an ocean. Sometimes blue is hot air, scattering, and exotic clouds.
