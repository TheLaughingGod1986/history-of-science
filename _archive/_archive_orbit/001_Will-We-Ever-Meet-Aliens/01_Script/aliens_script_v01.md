## Script

[ORBIT: intro_wave]  
[TITLE: Will We Ever Meet Aliens?]

Look up on a clear night and the sky feels crowded. Thousands of stars. Billions more beyond them. Whole galaxies spinning in the dark.

So… where is everybody?

If the universe is so big, and so old, why haven’t we met anyone yet?

I’m Orbit — and today we’re asking one of the biggest questions humans keep returning to: will we ever meet aliens?

Not in a movie. Not as a meme. As a real question about biology, distance, time, and what “meeting” even means.

[ORBIT: explaining]

Here’s the first hard truth. Space is not just big. It’s awkwardly, inconveniently, almost rudely big.

Our nearest star system, Alpha Centauri, is about four light-years away. That sounds almost neighbourly… until you remember light itself takes four years to cross that gap. Our fastest spacecraft would need tens of thousands of years to get there.

And that’s the *close* one.

The Milky Way alone holds hundreds of billions of stars. Many of those stars have planets. Some of those planets sit in the so-called habitable zone — not too hot, not too cold, where liquid water could exist.

On paper, that sounds promising. So promising that in the nineteen-sixties, astronomer Frank Drake tried to estimate how many communicative civilisations might exist in our galaxy. The Drake Equation isn’t a final answer. It’s a way of lining up our ignorance in a useful order: star formation, planets, life, intelligence, technology, and how long those civilisations last.

Plug in optimistic numbers and the galaxy should be buzzing. Plug in pessimistic ones and we might be alone for practical purposes.

[ORBIT: thinking]

Which brings us to the Fermi Paradox — named after physicist Enrico Fermi, who allegedly asked, during lunch, a version of: if alien civilisations are common… where are they?

No radio greetings. No city lights on exoplanets. No obvious megastructures. No visiting fleets. Just silence — or at least, silence as far as we can tell.

That silence has inspired dozens of explanations. Some are hopeful. Some are unsettling. Most are incomplete.

[ORBIT: explaining]

One popular idea is that life is common, but *intelligent* life is rare. Earth had single-celled organisms for most of its history. Complex animals are late. Humans with radios are later still. Maybe the jump from chemistry to curiosity is the hard part.

Another idea: intelligence happens, but technology doesn’t last. Civilisations might burn bright and short — collapsing, changing priorities, or destroying the tools that would make them visible across light-years.

Or maybe they’re out there… and carefully quiet. The so-called zoo hypothesis imagines advanced civilisations watching younger ones without interfering — a cosmic nature reserve with a “do not disturb” sign.

There’s also the simpler possibility: we’re early. The universe is about thirteen point eight billion years old, but rocky planets around sun-like stars may have become more common later. Perhaps the party hasn’t really started yet — and we’re among the first guests checking the door.

[ORBIT: surprised]

Then there’s distance again — not as poetry, but as logistics.

Even if a civilisation only a thousand light-years away sent a radio message today, we wouldn’t hear it for a thousand years. If they sent a ship at a fraction of light speed, the journey could outlast empires, languages, and maybe even species.

“Meeting” might not mean a handshake. It might mean detecting a spectrum, a chemical fingerprint, a patterned signal — evidence that *someone* existed, even if we never share a room.

[ORBIT: explaining]

That’s why modern searches look different from flying-saucer stories.

SETI — the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence — listens for artificial radio or optical signals. Projects scan the sky for narrow-band transmissions that nature doesn’t usually make. So far, no confirmed “hello.” We’ve had intriguing candidates, like the famous Wow! signal in nineteen seventy-seven, but nothing that has repeated cleanly enough to settle the question.

Meanwhile, exoplanet science has exploded. We now know planets are normal. Transit missions and telescopes measure sizes, orbits, and sometimes atmospheres. The next frontier isn’t just finding planets — it’s finding *biosignatures*: gases or combinations of gases that living worlds might produce.

Oxygen alone isn’t proof. Context matters. But if we found a world with a strange, hard-to-fake atmospheric cocktail — plus liquid water, plus the right temperature — the conversation on Earth would change overnight.

We may meet aliens first as data: a line on a graph, a wobble in starlight, a molecule that shouldn’t be there.

[ORBIT: thinking]

So will we ever *meet* them — face to… faceplate?

Honest answer: we don’t know. Anyone claiming certainty is selling comfort, not science.

What we can say is more careful.

If “meet” means a physical encounter with interstellar travellers in our lifetime, the odds look long. The energy costs are enormous. The timescales are brutal. The universe does not schedule appointments around human careers.

If “meet” means detecting clear evidence of life beyond Earth — microbial or technological — then this century is one of the most interesting windows we’ve ever had. Instruments are getting sharper. Catalogues of planets are growing. The question is moving from philosophy toward measurement.

[ORBIT: explaining]

There’s also a quieter lesson inside the Fermi Paradox.

The silence of the sky isn’t only about aliens. It’s about us. How long do technological civilisations last? How carefully do we listen? How honestly do we interpret ambiguous data without turning every anomaly into a headline?

Wanting company in the cosmos is deeply human. So is the fear that we might be alone. Both feelings can coexist with careful science.

[ORBIT: ending]

So — will we ever meet aliens?

Maybe as a signal.  
Maybe as a biosignature.  
Maybe never as a conversation.

But the search still matters. Because looking for life elsewhere is also a way of understanding life *here* — how rare it might be, how fragile it might be, and how precious a noisy little planet becomes when the rest of the sky stays quiet.

I’m Orbit. If you want more big questions about a bigger universe, stick around.

And next time you look up… remember: the silence isn’t empty. It’s an invitation to keep listening.
