## PART 02: The Trick That Shrank the Wire (1:15–2:35)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Trick That Shrank the Wire · 1:15 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:18 — warm 3D cartoon period drafting desk: a brass dial shows voltage climbing while a glowing current arrow shrinks; an inked diagram on cream parchment draws two wires side by side — low voltage glowing hot red, high voltage staying cool gold · DATA: inked Joule-loss bar chart — raising voltage 10x cuts current to 1/10 and cuts wire heat loss to 1/100 · SCALE: ten times the pressure, one hundredth the wasted heat · LABEL: TEN TIMES HIGHER]

[EXPLORER ACTS: once. Beside the brick transformer shed in Great Barrington, the Explorer leans close to a humming wooden pole box, holds his brass compass near the wire as the needle trembles, widens his eyes behind his round gold glasses, and steps back out of frame]
[TEACH: Raising transmission voltage cuts current and reduces resistive heat loss sharply. Because AC constantly changes direction, a transformer with no moving parts can step voltage up for travel and down for safety.]

The physics is clear. Power equals voltage multiplied by current — pressure times flow.

What heats a wire is current, and wasted heat grows with the square of the current. Raise voltage ten times, and you need one tenth of the current for the same power. Wasted heat drops a hundredfold. Ten times the pressure, one hundredth the wasted heat.

The catch was safety: you cannot feed three thousand volts straight into a house lamp. You needed a way to step voltage up for travel, and down at the front door.

In the eighteen-eighties, steady direct current had no simple box to change its voltage. Alternating current did. Because it reverses direction repeatedly, its shifting magnetic field induces a new voltage in a neighbouring coil, with no moving parts.

In May eighteen eighty-five, three engineers in Budapest unveiled a closed-iron-core device wired in parallel so lamps could switch independently. They helped name it the transformer.

George Westinghouse, rich from railway air brakes, bought early European rights and backed an engineer named William Stanley. On the twentieth of March, eighteen eighty-six, in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, Stanley flipped a switch.

His generator made five hundred volts of alternating current, stepped it up to three thousand volts, sent it four thousand feet down Main Street — nearly thirteen football pitches — and stepped it back down to a safe one hundred volts across twenty shops.

The one-mile trap was proved broken. Yet Edison still held one trump card. What couldn't alternating current do yet?

## PART 03: The Motor Without Sparks (2:35–4:00)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Motor Without Sparks · 2:35 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:38 — 3D cartoon 1880s factory floor: leather belts drive lathes from a humming DC motor while tiny amber sparks crackle at its copper brushes; beside it sits an idle AC wire powering only hanging lamps · LABEL: LIGHTS WITHOUT MOTORS]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:56 — 3D cartoon Manhattan workshop, 1884: Nikola Tesla, tall and lean in a dark coat, sketches glowing geometric coils on a drafting board by lamplight, then walks out past heavy DC dynamos into a rainy New York street · WHO: Nikola Tesla (1856–1943) — electrical engineer · WHEN: New York, June 1884]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:14 — 3D cartoon explainer in period ink-and-brass style: a copper ring of coils lights in staggered pulses — phase one, phase two — pulling an invisible luminous magnetic wave smoothly in a circle; a solid copper cylinder at the centre begins to spin swiftly with zero brushes touching it · WHEN: 1 May 1888 · DATA: animated two-phase rotating field diagram — staggered AC waves creating continuous torque with zero sliding brushes · SCALE: like pushing a child's roundabout from opposite sides in rhythm · LABEL: ROTATING FIELD]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:38 — 3D cartoon cut-out portrait transition (HOTU style) between Turin, Italy, and Pittsburgh: Galileo Ferraris lectures beside a brass model in March 1888; cut to George Westinghouse shaking hands with Tesla over patent papers in July 1888 · WHO: Galileo Ferraris (1847–1897) — physics professor · WHEN: March–July 1888 · FACT: Never patented his motor]
[TEACH: Tesla's polyphase induction motor used out-of-step alternating currents to create a rotating magnetic field, turning a brushless rotor so AC could power factories as well as lights.]

Cities didn't just want parlour lights. Factories needed motors. Direct current had working motors; early alternating current sputtered and stalled.

Enter Nikola Tesla. Born in what is now Croatia, Tesla reached New York in June eighteen eighty-four and worked briefly at the Edison Machine Works. Seeing direct current's limits first-hand, he walked away and built the alternating-current motor Edison dismissed.

Tesla's experiment was pure elegance. Instead of one alternating wave, send two or more waves out of step, like two hands pushing a roundabout from opposite sides in steady rhythm.

As coils energised in sequence around a ring, they created a smooth, rotating magnetic field. Drop a metal rotor inside, and the field dragged it round automatically. No sliding brushes. No sparks. Quiet, relentless torque.

On the first of May, eighteen eighty-eight, Tesla won his foundational American patents for this polyphase induction motor. Weeks earlier, in March eighteen eighty-eight, Italian professor Galileo Ferraris published the same discovery in Turin — yet never patented his motor, doubting its industrial strength.

Westinghouse saw the proof immediately. In July eighteen eighty-eight, he licensed Tesla's patents and took an option on Ferraris's design.

Now alternating current had both transformers for distance and a rugged factory motor. Surely Edison would switch sides? Case closed?

Wrong. Even after one of his own engineers wrote a confidential eighteen eighty-six report advising alternating current, Thomas Edison refused. He had staked his fortune and his name on direct current.

Overhead high-voltage lines truly were poorly insulated back then, and a fierce March eighteen eighty-eight blizzard tore wires down across New York. Edison's company leaned hard into that fear.

Electrical engineer Harold Brown staged grim public demonstrations arguing alternating current was deadlier than direct current. He claimed to act alone, though evidence later showed Edison's laboratory quietly supplied equipment and support.

When New York State adopted electrical execution, Brown covertly bought secondhand Westinghouse alternating-current generators for its first electric chair, hoping to tie Westinghouse to death. On the sixth of August, eighteen ninety, the execution of William Kemmler was horribly botched — and backfired in public disgust.

Meanwhile, bankers read the ledger. In eighteen ninety-two, they merged Edison's firm with rival Thomson-Houston to form General Electric, dropping Edison's name from the door.

Now Westinghouse and General Electric faced off for America's grandest showcase. Who would light the world's fair?

For the eighteen ninety-three World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, General Electric re-bid five hundred and fifty-four thousand dollars. George Westinghouse undercut them at three hundred and ninety-nine thousand dollars and won.

Then came the twist. General Electric owned Edison's bulb patent and refused to sell Westinghouse a single lamp.

Imagine standing in Westinghouse's workshop with opening day approaching and over ninety thousand lamps to build. Barred from Edison's sealed globe, Westinghouse's engineers rushed out a two-piece "stopper lamp", plugged like a glass bottle.

When the fair opened in May eighteen ninety-three, alternating current flooded the white halls with golden light, creating "the White City", while Nikola Tesla demonstrated his polyphase motors on the floor.

That triumph sealed Niagara Falls. Later in eighteen ninety-three, Westinghouse won the contract for five-thousand-horsepower alternating-current generators at the Edward Dean Adams Power Plant — and even General Electric built transmission lines and transformers.

Local Niagara power began in August eighteen ninety-five. Then at midnight on the fifteenth to sixteenth of November, eighteen ninety-six, transformers stepped the current to eleven thousand volts and sent it twenty-two miles to Buffalo, New York — over twenty times Edison's original reach.

One grid had conquered a whole region. So did direct current vanish forever?

## PART 06: Why Both Currents Won (6:55–8:20)

[CHAPTER CARD: Why Both Currents Won · 6:55 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:58 — 3D cartoon sweeping modern landscape in warm golden dusk: a distant power station steps AC up to tall steel pylons crossing hills, down through a neighbourhood transformer, and into a glowing kitchen lamp pulsing quietly at 50 or 60 cycles a second · DATA: modern grid voltage ladder — power plant → hundreds of thousands of volts on pylons → 120 V / 230 V at the wall socket (50–60 Hz) · SCALE: fifty to sixty silent flips every single second · LABEL: THE MODERN GRID]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:30 — 3D cartoon cutaway beneath a deep blue ocean: a sleek modern high-voltage direct-current (HVDC) cable glows gently along the seabed between two coastal converter stations, cutting back to a hand plugging a phone charger brick into an AC wall socket · FACT: DC came back undersea · LABEL: BOTH CURRENTS WON]
[TEACH: AC still carries power across cities and into homes because transformers make voltage changes simple, while modern solid-state electronics have brought high-voltage DC back for ultra-long undersea cables and inside every electronic device.]

Look at the wall socket beside you. Its electricity is alternating current, reversing fifty times a second in Britain and Europe, or sixty times a second in North America.

Beyond your town, transformers boost that current to hundreds of thousands of volts to cross countries with minimal loss, then step it down to the one hundred and twenty or two hundred and thirty volts inside your walls.

Yet there is a quiet twist. What would you feel if you touched your phone charger right now? Gentle warmth — because circuits inside that plug turn the wall's alternating current straight back into direct current for every microchip and battery.

And beneath our oceans, Edison's idea returned. With solid-state electronics that never existed in eighteen eighty-two, engineers can finally step direct current to huge voltages too. Today, ultra-long undersea cables run on high-voltage direct current, wasting even less energy over extreme distances.

Alternating current won the war that wired the modern world — and modern physics found room for both.

Half a century after Niagara lit Buffalo, physicists faced an even sharper calculation — one that nearly convinced them a single test in the New Mexico desert could set the sky on fire.
