Why do we use AC instead of DC — and how did Edison vs Tesla decide the electricity in your wall socket? In September 1882, Thomas Edison switched on Pearl Street Station in Lower Manhattan, lighting 400 lamps across a quarter square mile at 110 volts of direct current — but pushing low-voltage DC any further meant ruinous copper and wasted heat.
Discover how transformers, Nikola Tesla's brushless polyphase induction motor, the 1893 Chicago World's Fair stopper bulbs, and Niagara Falls settled the War of the Currents — and why direct current quietly returned inside your phone charger and beneath the ocean.

In this film:
• why Edison's 1882 Pearl Street DC station hit a copper-and-heat wall past a quarter square mile
• how raising voltage 10× cuts resistive heat loss to 1/100 — and William Stanley's 1886 Great Barrington transformer test
• how Nikola Tesla and Galileo Ferraris unlocked the rotating magnetic field and brushless AC motor
• the 1888–1892 War of the Currents, Harold Brown, and the birth of General Electric
• George Westinghouse's 1893 Chicago White City stopper lamps and the 22-mile 1896 Niagara-to-Buffalo transmission line
• why AC powers the grid while DC powers every microchip and modern undersea HVDC cables
