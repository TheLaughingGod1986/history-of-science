# What Killed the Dinosaurs Was Hidden in a Line of Clay (script master v01)

## PART 01: The Line in the Rock (0:00–1:20). No chapter card.

What killed the dinosaurs? The answer was hidden in a line of rock as thin as a pencil.

In a quiet gorge in Italy, you can put your finger on it. Below it, the world of the dinosaurs. Above it, they're gone.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:00 — a sunlit limestone cliff in a narrow Italian gorge; the camera pushes in on a thin dark band of clay between pale layers of rock; a fingertip rests on it (ANSWER IMAGE by 0:04) · LABEL: THE CLAY LINE]

So what happened in that thin layer of clay? And how could a few grams of it tell us what killed the dinosaurs?

[VISUAL MUST: 0:08 — a small glass vial of grey clay on a lab bench, turning slowly under a lamp · LABEL: ONE SMALL SAMPLE]

By the end of this film, you'll know how a geologist, his father, and a rare metal hidden in that clay solved one of the biggest mysteries on Earth.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:15 — three things appear one after another on a workbench: a geologist's hammer, a vial of clay, a printed science paper · LABEL: A HAMMER · A VIAL · A PAPER]

For more than a hundred and sixty million years, dinosaurs ruled the land. Then, about sixty-six million years ago, they disappeared, along with most of the other kinds of life on Earth. In the seas, on land, in the air. Gone.

For a long time, nobody knew why. Some scientists said the climate slowly changed. Some said volcanoes. Others said disease, or that mammals ate their eggs. There were dozens of ideas, and almost no way to test them.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:40 — a painted museum hall: a great dinosaur skeleton, with question marks drifting around it like dust · LABEL: WHAT HAPPENED?]

Picture yourself standing in that gorge, with your hand on the rock. Below your fingers, the last days of the dinosaurs. Above, a world without them. What would you look for, to find out what happened in between?

[TEACH: Dinosaurs lived for more than 160 million years and died out about 66 million years ago, along with most other kinds of life. For a long time, nobody could test why.]

In the nineteen-seventies, a young American geologist started looking very closely.

## PART 02: The Gorge at Gubbio (1:20–2:55)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Gorge at Gubbio · 1:20 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:23 — Gubbio, Italy, 1970s: Walter Alvarez (readable face, about thirty-five, short beard, open-necked shirt, field bag over his shoulder) taps the cliff with a geologist's hammer · LABEL: WALTER ALVAREZ · GUBBIO]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:45 — a magnifying lens over the pale limestone below the clay: it's crowded with tiny round fossil shells, each the size of a grain of sand · LABEL: TINY SHELLS]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:05 — the same lens moves above the clay line: the rock is almost empty, just a few very small shells · LABEL: ALMOST NONE]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:25 — Walter carefully wraps a small piece of the rock, with its thin clay band, in cloth and puts it in his bag · LABEL: A PIECE TO TAKE HOME]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once. In the gorge the Explorer peers through a hand lens at the rock below the clay, raises his eyebrows at all the tiny shells, moves the lens above the line, finds nothing, and looks back at the camera, puzzled]
[TEACH: Below the clay, the rock is full of tiny fossil shells. Above it, almost all of them are gone. Something wiped them out at the same moment the dinosaurs died.]

Walter Alvarez was studying the rocks in a gorge near the town of Gubbio. The cliffs there are made of limestone, laid down layer by layer on an ancient sea floor, over millions of years.

When he looked through a hand lens, the rock below the clay was crowded with tiny fossil shells, each about the size of a grain of sand. They came from little creatures that had floated in the sea.

But just above the clay, almost all of them were gone. Only a few very small ones were left.

Why should tiny shells in the sea matter to the story of the dinosaurs? Because they vanished at exactly the same level in the rock. Whatever killed the dinosaurs on land had struck in the sea at the very same moment. And the clay layer, about a centimetre thick, was the moment itself.

Walter wondered something simple. How long did that clay take to form? A few years would mean a sudden disaster. Thousands of years would mean a slow decline. If he could time the clay, he could tell the difference.

But how do you put a clock on a layer of mud that's sixty-six million years old?

He took a piece back home, to California. And he showed it to his father.

## PART 03: A Clock Made of Stardust (2:55–4:40)

[CHAPTER CARD: A Clock Made of Stardust · 2:55 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:58 — Berkeley, California: Luis Alvarez (readable face, late sixties, white hair, glasses, cardigan) turns the rock sample over in his hands at a kitchen table, his son beside him · LABEL: LUIS ALVAREZ · BERKELEY]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:20 — a calm teach scene: a soft, steady rain of glittering space dust falls on the Earth from all directions · LABEL: A STEADY RAIN FROM SPACE]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:40 — a cutaway Earth forming: molten iron sinks to the centre, carrying silvery iridium with it; the crust above is left almost bare · LABEL: IRIDIUM SANK TO THE CORE]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:00 — a laboratory at Berkeley, late 1970s: Frank Asaro and Helen Michel (readable faces, lab coats) load tiny powdered samples into a measuring machine · LABEL: FRANK ASARO · HELEN MICHEL]
[TEACH: Iridium is very rare in the Earth's crust, because most of it sank to the core. But it's much more common in meteorites, so space dust adds a little to the sea floor every year.]

Walter's father was Luis Alvarez, a physicist who had won a Nobel Prize. He loved a puzzle. He had once used tiny particles from space to search for hidden rooms inside an Egyptian pyramid. And now he had an idea for a clock.

Every day, a gentle rain of dust from space falls on the Earth. It's far too fine to see. But it's always there, settling on land and on the sea floor, year after year.

That space dust carries a rare metal called iridium. On Earth's surface, iridium is incredibly scarce. When our planet was young and molten, most of it sank down to the core with the iron. But in meteorites, and in the dust from space, there's much more of it.

So here was the clock. If the clay formed slowly, over thousands of years, it would have collected lots of space dust, and lots of iridium. If it formed quickly, in a few years, there would be almost none.

They needed to measure iridium in tiny amounts, a few parts in a billion. So they asked two chemists at Berkeley, Frank Asaro and Helen Michel, who had a machine sensitive enough to do it.

They expected a small number. What came back was not small at all.

## PART 04: Too Much Iridium (4:40–6:20)

[CHAPTER CARD: Too Much Iridium · 4:40 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:43 — a hand-drawn graph on a lab notebook page: a flat low line through the rock layers, then a tall sharp spike exactly at the clay · LABEL: THE IRIDIUM SPIKE]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:05 — a map of the world: a pin in Gubbio, then a second pin at a white sea cliff in Denmark, then a third in New Zealand; each glows with the same spike · LABEL: ALL AROUND THE WORLD]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:30 — a slow, calm scene with no fire shown: a dark grey veil of dust spreads high across the sky and dims the Sun over a quiet prehistoric forest · LABEL: DUST AROUND THE WORLD]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:55 — the journal Science, 6 June 1980, lies open on a desk; its title reads EXTRATERRESTRIAL CAUSE FOR THE CRETACEOUS-TERTIARY EXTINCTION · LABEL: SCIENCE · 1980]
[TEACH: The clay held far more iridium than space dust alone could explain. The team proposed that a huge asteroid had hit the Earth and spread iridium-rich dust around the world.]

In the Gubbio clay, there was about thirty times more iridium than in the rock around it. Far too much for a slow, gentle rain of space dust.

At first, they wondered whether it was a strange local accident. So they tested the same boundary in a completely different place, a white sea cliff in Denmark. The iridium spike was there too, even bigger. Later it turned up in New Zealand, and then all around the world.

Something had sprinkled iridium over the whole planet, all at once.

Luis Alvarez worked through the possibilities, one by one. What could spread a rare metal over the whole world?

First, he wondered about an exploding star nearby. A supernova should leave behind a rare kind of plutonium. So they tested the clay for it. For a few exciting days, it looked as if they'd found it. But when they checked again, it wasn't there. The supernova idea was wrong.

In the end, only one idea fitted every clue. A giant asteroid, about ten kilometres across, had struck the Earth.

An asteroid that size would hit with unimaginable force. It would throw dust high into the sky, spreading the asteroid's iridium around the globe. The dust would block out sunlight, for months or even years. Plants would stop growing. And without plants, the animals that ate them, and the animals that ate those animals, would starve.

In June nineteen eighty, the four scientists published their idea in the journal Science. It was bold. And a lot of other scientists didn't believe it.

Because if an asteroid that big had really hit the Earth, where was the hole?

## PART 05: The Crater and the Birds (6:20–8:10)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Crater and the Birds · 6:20 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:23 — a microscope view of a grain of quartz criss-crossed with fine straight cracks; then a tiny glass bead from Haiti beside a coin for scale · LABEL: SHOCKED QUARTZ · GLASS BEADS]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:40 — a gravity map of Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula: a huge buried ring appears in glowing colours beneath the coastline · LABEL: CHICXULUB CRATER]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:55 — a scale teach card: the crater's ring laid over a map of southern England for size, about 180 km across · LABEL: ABOUT 180 KM WIDE]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:10 — the Gubbio clay line again, close up; a gentle glow shows the iridium inside it · LABEL: THE SAME MOMENT]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:35 — a garden in the morning; a robin hops along a fence and turns its head to camera; a faint outline of a small feathered dinosaur skeleton fades in over it, then out · LABEL: STILL HERE]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once. In the garden the Explorer crouches, holds out a few crumbs, and a robin hops closer; he looks from the bird to the sky, and smiles]
[TEACH: The Chicxulub crater in Mexico, about 180 kilometres wide and 66 million years old, is the impact site. Birds are the dinosaurs that survived.]

For ten years, scientists searched and argued. The clues slowly piled up. In the clay, they found tiny grains of quartz, cracked by a shock far more violent than any volcano could make. Then tiny beads of glass, splashed from melted rock, turned up in Haiti. They pointed the hunt towards the Caribbean. Could the crater be somewhere nearby?

Then, in the early nineteen-nineties, researchers pieced together the answer. Buried under the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico, hidden beneath newer rock, was an enormous crater. About a hundred and eighty kilometres wide.

It's called Chicxulub. And when its rocks were dated, they were sixty-six million years old. The same moment as the thin clay line in Italy.

Not every question has been answered. Scientists still study how volcanoes in India may have added to the damage. But the main story is now clear. A rock from space hit the Earth, and the world of the dinosaurs ended.

Why was this so groundbreaking? Because it showed that the history of life on Earth isn't only slow and steady. Sometimes, everything changes at once. And it showed that a few grams of clay, measured carefully enough, can tell you about a moment sixty-six million years ago.

There's one more twist. Not all the dinosaurs died. A few small, feathered ones survived. Their descendants are all around you now. The pigeon on the pavement. The robin in the garden. Every bird you have ever seen is a living dinosaur.

Why did they survive, when the giants didn't? That's still a mystery scientists are working on. Being small, eating seeds that could last through the dark, and nesting on the ground may all have helped.

So next time you see a bird, think about that thin line of clay in Italy. Below it, a world of giants. Above it, the survivors. And one of them is singing outside your window.

A hammer, a vial and a paper. Together, they found a catastrophe hidden in a line of clay.
