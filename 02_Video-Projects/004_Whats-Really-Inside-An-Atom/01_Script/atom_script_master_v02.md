Manchester, eighteen-oh-eight. A schoolteacher named John Dalton puts the old idea to work. His **atomic theory** says every element is made of its own kind of atom, and each kind has its own weight. Hydrogen is the lightest. Oxygen is heavier. Gold is heavier still.

Why believe him? Because elements always combine in the same fixed amounts. Water is always the same mix of hydrogen and oxygen, however you make it. That only makes sense if both are made of tiny pieces with fixed weights.

Now chemists had a ruler. Compare the weights of the atoms and line them up.

Sixty years later in Saint Petersburg, Dmitri Mendeleev does exactly that. He writes each element on a card and sorts the cards by weight. The properties repeat. Soft metals that fizz in water line up in one column. Harsh, reactive chlorine, bromine and iodine line up in another. He even leaves gaps for elements nobody has found yet. Then he prints his table and mails it to chemists across Europe.

But look closer. Tellurium is heavier than iodine, so by weight tellurium should come after it. Mendeleev puts it before anyway, because that's where it behaves right. Cobalt and nickel have the same trouble.

Mendeleev guessed the weights had been measured wrong. They hadn't.

So the table works, but it breaks its own rule. If weight isn't the real order, what is?

[TEACH: Thomson showed cathode rays are made of tiny charged particles, far lighter than any atom. Atoms have parts.]

Cambridge, eighteen ninety-seven. **J.J. Thomson** is studying a strange glow. Pump the air out of a glass tube, run electricity through it, and a beam shoots from one end to the other. Scientists called them cathode rays. But what were they made of? Light, or stuff?

Thomson brings a magnet close. The beam bends. He tries two charged metal plates. It bends again. Light doesn't do that. Tiny charged particles do.

Then he measures how far they bend, and works out how heavy they must be. The answer is astonishing: far lighter than a hydrogen atom, the lightest thing anyone knew. We now know each one is about two thousand times lighter.

But here is the strange part. It doesn't matter which metal he uses. The same particles come out every time.

So the uncuttable thing can be cut. Every atom carries these tiny negative particles. Thomson called them corpuscles. Today we call them electrons.

But atoms have no overall charge, so something positive must balance them out. A few years later, in nineteen-oh-four, Thomson made his guess: a soft ball of positive stuff with electrons dotted through it, like raisins in a pudding.

It was a lovely idea. It was also wrong.

Manchester, nineteen-oh-nine. Ernest Rutherford has a new kind of bullet: alpha particles, tiny and fast, thrown out by radioactive rocks. His assistant Hans Geiger and a young student called Ernest Marsden fire them at gold beaten thinner than paper.

Picture yourself in that room. The lights are off, and you sit in the dark until your eyes adjust. You look through a microscope at a small screen. Each time a particle hits it, you see a tiny green flash, and you count it.

Now, if Thomson's pudding is right, every bullet should punch straight through. Almost all of them do.

But about one in eight thousand bounces back.

[VISUAL MUST: LABEL: "As if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you." Rutherford]

Rutherford said it was as if you fired a huge shell at a piece of tissue paper, and it came back and hit you.

Then, in nineteen-eleven, he explains it. Nearly all of an atom's mass is packed into a tiny, positive centre, which he calls the **nucleus**. The electrons sit far outside it. Everything in between is almost empty.

How empty? Imagine you are standing in the middle of a football stadium, holding a pea. If the atom were the whole stadium, the nucleus would be that pea. The electrons would be somewhere out past the back row of seats. Everything else, all around you, is nothing at all.

That raised a new question. If every element has its own nucleus, what makes one nucleus different from another? A Dutch lawyer and amateur scientist, Antonius van den Broek, had a bold guess: an element's place in the table might equal the positive charge in its nucleus.

It was a brilliant guess, but nobody could measure it. Yet.

Manchester, nineteen thirteen. Henry Moseley is twenty-five. He builds a tube with a little train inside, carrying a different metal target into the beam each time. He fires electrons at each target, and each metal gives off X-rays.

It's the same invisible light Röntgen used to see the bones in a living hand.

Moseley splits the X-rays with a crystal and measures their wavelength. Then he finds a pattern. Element by element, the X-rays step up in perfectly even jumps, like the rungs of a ladder. One rung per element, no matter how heavy it is.

Count the rungs and you get a whole number for every element: the positive charge in its nucleus. Van den Broek's guess was right.

[VISUAL MUST: LABEL: ATOMIC NUMBER = the charge in the nucleus]

Now sort the table by that number. Tellurium is fifty-two and iodine is fifty-three. Cobalt is twenty-seven and nickel is twenty-eight. The broken rule fixes itself. Mendeleev's instinct was right all along. He was just using the wrong ruler.

And the ladder had missing rungs, at forty-three, sixty-one, seventy-two and seventy-five. Those were elements nobody had found yet. Over the next thirty-odd years, every one of them was found in nature or made in a lab.

Why do the columns repeat? The charge in the nucleus decides how many electrons an atom holds, and the outer electrons decide how it behaves. Elements with the same kind of outer electrons end up in the same column. That's why the soft metals fizz together, and why chlorine and iodine react alike.

Two years later, in nineteen fifteen, Moseley was killed in the First World War at Gallipoli. He was twenty-seven. Many scientists believe he would have won a Nobel Prize.

So this is what's really inside an atom. First it was an idea. Then Dalton gave each kind a weight. Thomson found a piece inside it. Rutherford found its tiny centre. And Moseley counted it. Every element has its own number, and that number is the order of the periodic table. It isn't a list of weights. It's a count.

But the nucleus had one more secret. Some nuclei don't sit still. They break apart and throw out rays of their own. That's the next story.
