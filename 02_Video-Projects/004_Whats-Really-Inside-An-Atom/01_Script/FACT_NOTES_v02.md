# HOS 004 — teach and fact notes (script v02)

## Teach (say it out loud after the film)

1. An **atom** is the smallest piece of an element. The word means "uncuttable", but atoms have parts.
2. **J.J. Thomson** found the electron in 1897. **Rutherford** found the nucleus in 1911.
3. **Moseley** used X-rays in 1913 to count the positive charge in each nucleus. That count is the **atomic number**.
4. **Why it was groundbreaking:** the periodic table's order is counting, not weighing, and it predicted four missing elements.

## Fact notes for Ben / UAT

- Tellurium (127.6) is heavier than iodine (126.9) but comes before it. Mendeleev suspected a mismeasured weight; it wasn't. Cobalt (58.93) is slightly heavier than nickel (58.69); in 1869 both were given as about 59.
- Dalton's weights were relative (compared with hydrogen), not weighings of single atoms, hence "compare the weights" and "gave each kind a weight".
- Thomson measured the charge-to-mass ratio in 1897. His first estimate was roughly a thousand times lighter than hydrogen; the modern value is about 1/1836, so the script says "far lighter" for 1897 and "we now know about two thousand" for today.
- The plum pudding model is Thomson's 1904 paper, not 1897.
- Geiger and Marsden (1909) reported about 1 in 8,000 alpha particles deflected by more than 90°: bounced back at a steep angle, not always straight back. They counted scintillations by eye through a microscope in a darkened room. Rutherford's "15-inch shell" line comes from a later lecture and is widely quoted.
- Moseley was born in November 1887, so he was 25 in 1913 and 27 at his death at Gallipoli on 10 Aug 1915.
- The gaps at 43, 61, 72 and 75 were filled by hafnium (1923), rhenium (1925), technetium (1937, made in a lab) and promethium (1945), 32 years after 1913, hence "thirty-odd years" and "found in nature or made in a lab".
- Stadium and pea: a nucleus is roughly 1/100,000 the width of its atom. A pea (about 1 cm) in a stadium about 1 km across is the same order.
