## For the description's Sources line

- John Dalton, 1808, *A New System of Chemical Philosophy* (Part I, Manchester: Printed by S. Russell for R. Bickerstaff, London) — relative atomic weights, atomic theory, and the hydrogen-based weight scale.
- Dmitri Mendeleev, 1869, *Zeitschrift für Chemie* (vol. 12, pp. 405–406), "On the Relationship of the Properties of the Elements to their Atomic Weights" — ordering elements by atomic weight, predicting undiscovered elements, placing tellurium before iodine, and identifying mismeasured weights.
- J. J. Thomson, 1897, *Philosophical Magazine* (series 5, vol. 44, no. 269, pp. 293–316), "Cathode Rays" — magnetic and electrical deflection of cathode rays, discovery of subatomic corpuscles (electrons), and charge-to-mass ratio far lighter than hydrogen.
- H. Geiger & E. Marsden, 1909, *Proceedings of the Royal Society of London. Series A* (vol. 82, no. 557, pp. 495–500), "On a Diffuse Reflection of the α-Particles" — measurement of large-angle alpha particle scattering, showing approximately 1 in 8,000 particles deflected by more than 90 degrees.
- E. Rutherford, 1911, *Philosophical Magazine* (series 6, vol. 21, no. 125, pp. 669–688), "The Scattering of α and β Particles by Matter and the Structure of the Atom" — nuclear model of the atom, showing mass and positive charge concentrated in a tiny central nucleus with mostly empty space.
- H. G. J. Moseley, 1913, *Philosophical Magazine* (series 6, vol. 26, no. 156, pp. 1024–1034), "The High-Frequency Spectra of the Elements" — X-ray spectra stepping by regular whole-number integers, establishing atomic number as nuclear charge, resolving the Te/I and Co/Ni anomalies, and predicting missing elements at 43, 61, 72, and 75.

## Flags

- None. All 40 factual claims have been verified against live authoritative sources with exact verbatim quotations (Verdict: 40 OK, 0 FLAG, 0 NO SOURCE).
- Historical nuance note on Row 28 (Rutherford 1911): In his landmark May 1911 *Philosophical Magazine* paper, Rutherford described the core as a "central electric charge" or "central charge"; he adopted and popularized the specific term "nucleus" for this central body beginning in 1912. The nuclear model and the localization of mass at the centre were established in the 1911 paper.
- Historical nuance note on Row 23 (Thomson 1904): In his March 1904 *Philosophical Magazine* paper, Thomson formulated the model mathematically as "a number of negatively electrified corpuscles enclosed in a sphere of uniform positive electrification"; the popular metaphor "plum pudding" was coined during contemporary discussions and widely adopted in later textbooks.
