Manchester, nineteen-oh-nine. Ernest Rutherford has a new kind of bullet: alpha particles, tiny and fast, thrown out by radioactive rocks. His assistant Hans Geiger and a young student called Ernest Marsden fire them at gold beaten thinner than paper.

Picture yourself in that room. The lights are off, and you sit in the dark until your eyes adjust. You look through a microscope at a small screen. Each time a particle hits it, you see a tiny green flash, and you count it.

Now, if Thomson's pudding is right, every bullet should punch straight through. Almost all of them do.

But about one in eight thousand bounces back.

Rutherford said it was as if you fired a huge shell at a piece of tissue paper, and it came back and hit you.

Then, in nineteen-eleven, he explains it. Nearly all of an atom's mass is packed into a tiny, positive centre, which he calls the nucleus. The electrons sit far outside it. Everything in between is almost empty.

How empty? Imagine you are standing in the middle of a football stadium, holding a pea. If the atom were the whole stadium, the nucleus would be that pea. The electrons would be somewhere out past the back row of seats. Everything else, all around you, is nothing at all.

That raised a new question. If every element has its own nucleus, what makes one nucleus different from another? A Dutch lawyer and amateur scientist, Antonius van den Broek, had a bold guess: an element's place in the table might equal the positive charge in its nucleus.

It was a brilliant guess, but nobody could measure it. Yet.
