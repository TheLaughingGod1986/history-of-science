Cambridge, eighteen ninety-seven. J.J. Thomson is studying a strange glow. Pump the air out of a glass tube, run electricity through it, and a beam shoots from one end to the other. Scientists called them cathode rays. But what were they made of? Light, or stuff?

Thomson brings a magnet close. The beam bends. He tries two charged metal plates. It bends again. Light doesn't do that. Tiny charged particles do.

Then he measures how far they bend, and works out how heavy they must be. The answer is astonishing: far lighter than a hydrogen atom, the lightest thing anyone knew. We now know each one is about two thousand times lighter.

But here is the strange part. It doesn't matter which metal he uses. The same particles come out every time.

So the uncuttable thing can be cut. Every atom carries these tiny negative particles. Thomson called them corpuscles. Today we call them electrons.

But atoms have no overall charge, so something positive must balance them out. A few years later, in nineteen-oh-four, Thomson made his guess: a soft ball of positive stuff with electrons dotted through it, like raisins in a pudding.

It was a lovely idea. It was also wrong.
