Manchester 1913. Henry Moseley is 25. He builds a tube with a little train inside, carrying a different metal target into the beam each time. He fires electrons at each target, and each metal gives off X-rays. It's the same invisible light Röntgen used to see the bones in the living hand. Moseley splits the X-rays with a crystal and measures their wavelength. Then he finds a pattern. Element by element, the X-rays step up in perfectly even jumps, like the rungs of a ladder. One rung per element, no matter how heavy it is. Count the rungs, and you get a whole number for every element. The positive charge in its nucleus. Vanden Broeck's guess was right. Now sort the table by that number. Tellurium is 52, and iodine is 53. Cobalt is 27, and nickel is 28. The broken rule fixes itself. Mendeleev's instinct was right all along. He was just using the wrong ruler. And the ladder had missing rungs at 43, 61, 72 and 75. Those were elements nobody had found yet. Over the next 30-odd years, every one of them was found in nature or made in a lab. Why do the columns repeat? The charge in the nucleus decides how many electrons an atom holds, and the outer electrons decide how it behaves. Elements with the same kind of outer electrons end up in the same column. That's why the soft metals fizz together, and why chlorine and iodine react alike. Two years later, in 1915, Moseley was killed in the First World War at Gallipoli. He was 27. Many scientists believe he would have won a Nobel Prize. So this is what's really inside an atom. First it was an idea. Then Dalton gave each kind a weight. Thompson found a piece inside it. Rutherford found its tiny center, and Moseley counted it. Every element has its own number, and that number is the order of the periodic table. It isn't a list of weights, it's a count.
