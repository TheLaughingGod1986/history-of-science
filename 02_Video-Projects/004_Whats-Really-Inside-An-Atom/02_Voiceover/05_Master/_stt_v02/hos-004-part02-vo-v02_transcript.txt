Manchester 1808. A school teacher named John Dalton puts the old idea to work. His atomic theory says every element is made of its own kind of atom, and each kind has its own weight. Hydrogen is the lightest. Oxygen is heavier. Gold is heavier still. Why believe him? Because elements always combine in the same fixed amounts. Water is always the same mix of hydrogen and oxygen however you make it. That only makes sense if both are made of tiny pieces with fixed weights. Now chemists had a ruler. Compare the weights of the atoms and line them up. Sixty years later in St. Petersburg, Dmitri Mendeleev does exactly that. He writes each element on a card and sorts the cards by weight. The properties repeat. Soft metals that fizz in water line up in one column. Harsh, reactive chlorine, bromine, and iodine line up in another. He even leaves gaps for elements nobody has found yet. Then he prints his table and mails it to chemists across Europe. But look closer. Tellurium is heavier than iodine, so by weight, tellurium should come after it. Mendeleev puts it before anyway because that's where it behaves right. Cobalt and nickel have the same trouble. Mendeleev guessed the weights had been measured wrong. They hadn't. So the table works, but it breaks its own rule. If weight isn't the real order, what is?
