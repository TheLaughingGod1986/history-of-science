## 2. The film

### 2.1 Story (person, date, twist) — UNVERIFIED
- **Open (first 15 s):** a harbour where fishing boats sit flat on wet mud, then float six hours later (the Bay of Fundy's range exceeds 10 m, reaching ~16 m at Burntcoat Head — verify exact NOAA/CHS figure). Twice a day on **most coasts** (*J3214 fix: never say "everywhere", because diurnal coasts such as parts of the Gulf of Mexico have one tide a day*). What is moving the sea?
- **1609, Johannes Kepler (*Astronomia Nova*):** suggests the Moon attracts the waters of the Earth.
- **1616 and 1632, Galileo Galilei (*Discourse on the Tides*, *Dialogue* Fourth Day):** calls a pull from the Moon "occult" / "children's tales" and argues instead that the sea sloshes because the Earth spins and orbits, like water in a moving Venetian barge. It is the closing argument of his *Dialogue*. His main mechanism predicts one high tide every 24 hours, clashing with the ~12-hour cycle sailors knew.
- **1687, Isaac Newton (*Principia*, Book I & Book III):** explains the tides through differential gravity from the Moon and Sun — the Sun contributing roughly 46% of the Moon's tide-raising force, producing spring and neap tides. *J3214 physics guardrail: explain accurately via the differential horizontal (tractive) force sliding water sideways toward opposite sides of Earth, not "the Moon lifting water straight up" or a naive centrifugal fling.*
- **The midpoint twist (1775–1776, Pierre-Simon Laplace):** Newton's static equilibrium picture is not the whole answer either. Laplace treats the ocean as a fluid sheet on a rotating globe constrained by inertia, Coriolis deflection, ocean depth, and coastlines (Laplace's Tidal Equations): the real tide is a forced wave moving around ocean basins, explaining why tide times lag the Moon, why some coasts have diurnal (once-daily) tides, and why funnel-shaped resonant basins like the Bay of Fundy amplify tides dramatically.
- **Close (1872–1873 → 1944):** tide tables, Sir William Thomson's (later Lord Kelvin — *J3214 WHO-card note: created Baron Kelvin in 1892, so in an 1872 scene his WHO card must say Sir William Thomson, Glasgow physicist*) 1872–1873 mechanical brass tide-predicting machine built with Edward Roberts and Alexander Légé, and Arthur Doodson's use of tide-predicting machines at Bidston Observatory ("Position Z") to calculate the Normandy D-Day tidal window in June 1944.
- **Candidate DATA + SCALE moments (all UNVERIFIED pending `SOURCES.md`):**
  1. Two tides in a lunar day of ~24 h 50 min (~12 h 25 min apart).
  2. Bay of Fundy's ~16 m extreme range compared against a 4–5 storey house.
  3. Animated timeline `1609 → 1616/1632 → 1687 → 1775–1776 → 1872 → 1944`.
  4. Sun's tidal force (~46% of the Moon's, despite the Sun's far larger mass, because tidal force falls as $1/r^3$).
