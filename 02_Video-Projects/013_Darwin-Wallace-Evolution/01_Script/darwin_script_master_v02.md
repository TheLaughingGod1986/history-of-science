He kept the idea mostly to himself for twenty years. Then, in June eighteen fifty-eight, a letter arrived from a collector on the other side of the world. Inside was Darwin's own theory, worked out by somebody else.

## PART 02: The Birds He Forgot to Label (1:20–2:40)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Birds He Forgot to Label · 1:20 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:23 — the Galápagos Islands, 1835: black lava shore, cactus, small dark birds hopping between rocks; young Darwin (readable face, mid-twenties, sideburns, wide-brimmed hat) bags a bird without writing a label · WHEN: Galápagos Islands, September 1835 · LABEL: NO LABELS]

[EXPLORER ACTS: once. On the Beagle's deck the Explorer turns a bird skin over looking for a label, finds none, and shrugs at the camera]
[TEACH: Darwin collected small birds on the Galápagos but mostly didn't record which island each came from. In London, John Gould showed they were a group of closely related finches with very different beaks.]

When people picture Darwin on the Galápagos Islands, they imagine a flash of genius on the beach. It didn't happen like that.

In eighteen thirty-five, Darwin collected small dark birds there and thought they were a mix of blackbirds, wrens and finches. For most of them, he didn't write down which island they came from.

Even the giant tortoises should have told him. The ship's crew ate them and threw the shells overboard. Then the islands' vice-governor, Nicholas Lawson, mentioned something odd: he could tell which island a tortoise came from just by the shape of its shell. Darwin only understood how important that was much later.

Back in London, a bird expert called John Gould looked at the skins and gave him a surprise. They weren't a mix at all. They were all finches: a group of close cousins, each with a differently shaped beak. Heavy ones for cracking hard seeds. Thin ones for picking at insects and cactus flowers.

Darwin had to borrow labelled birds from his shipmates to work out which beak came from which island.

And slowly, a question formed. What if these birds had all come from one kind of finch, long ago, and changed to fit each island?

That's the tree in the notebook. He could see that one kind of animal could split into many. But what was doing the shaping?

## PART 03: The Engine and the Barnacles (2:40–4:05)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Engine and the Barnacles · 2:40 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:43 — London, autumn 1838: Darwin reads by lamplight; the book's spine reads MALTHUS; his eyes widen · WHO: Thomas Malthus (1766–1834) — economist · WHEN: 28 September 1838 · LABEL: THE MISSING PIECE]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:00 — an inked curve climbs steeply on period paper (offspring born) while a flat line stays low beside it (food available); the gap between them fills with faded silhouettes · DATA: inked growth curve, offspring vs food, the gap labelled "most never survive" · SCALE: far more born than can ever live]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:30 — the explainer: a drought island, only hard thick seeds left; finches with deeper beaks crack them and raise chicks; the next generation's beaks are a little deeper]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:45 — Down House, 1840s–1850s: a study table crowded with jars of barnacles and a brass microscope; a counter of years ticks from 1846 to 1854 · WHO: Emma Darwin (1808–1896) — Darwin's wife · WHEN: 1846–1854 · DATA: an eight-year barnacle counter, 1846 → 1854 · SCALE: eight years at one microscope · FACT: Eight years on barnacles]
[TEACH: Natural selection: living things have more young than can survive; small differences decide who lives to breed; useful differences are passed on and build up over many generations.]

On the twenty-eighth of September, eighteen thirty-eight, Darwin started reading a book about human populations by Thomas Malthus. And the missing piece fell into place.

Every living thing has far more young than can ever survive. There isn't enough food for all of them. So most die young.

Now picture a dry year on one island. Only hard, thick seeds are left. The finches whose beaks happen to be a little deeper can crack them. They live, and they raise chicks with deep beaks like theirs. Repeat that over thousands of generations, and the whole species changes. Nobody steers it. Nature just keeps the ones that fit.

Darwin called it natural selection. So why didn't he tell the world?

He was afraid. Telling a friend that species could change was, he wrote, "like confessing a murder." And his wife, Emma, was deeply religious.

In eighteen forty-four he wrote it all out, about two hundred and thirty pages, sealed it, and left Emma a letter. If he died, she was to pay to have it published. A few months later, an unsigned book claiming life had changed over time was torn apart by scientists.

Then he spent about eight years studying barnacles. So many barnacles, the story goes, that one of his children asked a friend where their father did *his* barnacles.

Darwin's friends kept warning him that if he waited too long, someone else would get there first. Who was that someone?

[VISUAL MUST: 5:15 — Down House, June 1858: Darwin reads Wallace's pages and lowers them slowly · WHO: Charles Lyell (1797–1875) — geologist · WHEN: 18 June 1858]
[TEACH: Alfred Russel Wallace, collecting in the Malay Archipelago, worked out natural selection independently in 1858 and sent his paper to Darwin.]

Alfred Russel Wallace was not rich like Darwin. He made his living collecting animals and selling them to museums.

In eighteen fifty-two, coming home from four years in the Amazon, his ship caught fire and sank. Almost his whole collection went down with it. He spent ten days in an open boat at sea.

He went out again anyway, this time to the islands of what is now Indonesia and Malaysia. Over eight years he collected more than a hundred and twenty-five thousand specimens.

In February eighteen fifty-eight, lying ill with a fever, Wallace thought about the same book by Malthus. And, like Darwin twenty years before, he saw it: the ones that fit survive. Within days he had written it up, and he posted it to the one famous naturalist he knew would be interested. Charles Darwin.

So Darwin could simply hide the letter, and keep the credit? Case closed?

Wrong. On the eighteenth of June, eighteen fifty-eight, Darwin read Wallace's paper and wrote to his friend Charles Lyell: "All my originality, whatever it may amount to, will be smashed."

Darwin said he would rather lose the credit than seem to have acted unfairly to Wallace. Three days later, his baby son died of scarlet fever.

His friends found a way out. What happened when the world finally heard?

[TEACH: Darwin's and Wallace's papers were read together at the Linnean Society on 1 July 1858. Darwin's book *On the Origin of Species* followed on 24 November 1859.]

On the first of July, eighteen fifty-eight, Darwin's friends Charles Lyell and Joseph Hooker had both men's work read out together at the Linnean Society in London. Wallace's paper, and older writing from Darwin to show he had the idea first.

Neither man was in the room. Darwin was at home, grieving for his son. Wallace hadn't even been asked. He was still in the islands, and didn't know for months.

And the reaction? Almost nothing. The next year, the society's president looked back and said the year had not been marked by any striking discovery.

That pushed Darwin to write. In a little over a year he finished a shorter book for everyone: *On the Origin of Species*. It came out on the twenty-fourth of November, eighteen fifty-nine, and booksellers had ordered every copy before it even went on sale.

And Wallace? There was no bitter fight. When he read the book in the islands, he praised it, and said Darwin had done the work far better than he could. Years later he even titled his own book about the theory *Darwinism*.

But one problem worried Darwin for the rest of his life. And the answer was already in print.

## PART 06: The Missing Piece (6:55–8:00)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Missing Piece · 6:55 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:58 — Edinburgh, 1867: an engineer at a desk mixes two pots of paint, one dark, one white; the mix turns grey, then greyer · WHO: Fleeming Jenkin (1833–1885) — engineer · WHEN: June 1867 · LABEL: BLENDED AWAY?]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:20 — a monastery garden in Brno: rows of pea plants, a friar with a notebook; the inked paint pots turn into whole beads that never mix · WHO: Gregor Mendel (1822–1884) — friar · WHEN: 1866]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:40 — a key slides into a lock and turns; an inked label writes "fit = fits" · WHO: Herbert Spencer (1820–1903) — philosopher · WHEN: 1864 · LABEL: FIT MEANS FITS]
[TEACH: People then thought parents' traits blended like paint, which would wash out any useful change. Mendel showed traits are passed on whole. "Fittest" means best suited, not strongest.]

The problem came from an engineer called Fleeming Jenkin in eighteen sixty-seven. Everyone then thought parents' traits blended in their children, like mixing paint. If that was true, one bird with a better beak would have children with a half-better beak, then a quarter-better. The advantage would wash away before nature could keep it.

Darwin never solved it. But a year earlier, in eighteen sixty-six, a friar called Gregor Mendel had published his work on pea plants. Traits don't blend like paint. They're passed on whole, like beads.

And one more thing. "Survival of the fittest" wasn't Darwin's phrase. The writer Herbert Spencer coined it, and Darwin only added it to his book later. Fittest never meant the strongest. It means the one that fits its world best, like a key in a lock.

Put Darwin's selection together with Mendel's beads, and you have the core of how biologists explain the living world today.
