Why did Charles Darwin work out how evolution happens, and then lock his theory of natural selection in a drawer for twenty years?
In June 1858, a letter arrived at Darwin's home in Kent from Alfred Russel Wallace — a collector on the other side of the world who had worked out the exact same idea during a malarial fever.

In the 1830s, most naturalists believed every species had been made once and never changed. After returning from the Galápagos Islands — where he forgot to label which island most of his finches came from — a twenty-eight-year-old Darwin drew a small branching tree in a private notebook beneath the words "I think." He wrote out his theory in 1844, sealed it with a letter to his wife Emma in case he died, and spent eight years dissecting barnacles until Wallace's packet forced the secret into the open.
