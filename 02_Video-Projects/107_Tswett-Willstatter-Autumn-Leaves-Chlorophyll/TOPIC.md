## 2. The film (UNVERIFIED working notes — require `SOURCES.md` exact quotes before script lock)

- **People and dates (all to be sourced in `SOURCES.md` before any script lock; written from memory here, UNVERIFIED):** In 1817 French chemists Pierre-Joseph Pelletier and Joseph Bienaimé Caventou isolated and named the green pigment in leaves *chlorophylle* (chlorophyll). On 21 March 1903 Russian-Italian botanist Mikhail Semyonovich Tswett, working in Warsaw, described washing a petroleum-ether leaf extract down a narrow glass tube packed with powdered calcium carbonate (chalk): the single green liquid separated into distinct emerald-green, yellow-green, and golden-yellow/orange rings (chlorophyll $a$, chlorophyll $b$, xanthophylls, carotenes). In two 1906 papers in *Berichte der Deutschen Botanischen Gesellschaft* he named the method *chromatography* ("colour writing"). German chemist Richard Willstätter won the 1915 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his investigations on plant pigments, especially chlorophyll (proving magnesium sits at the core of the chlorophyll molecule).
- **Twist (UNVERIFIED):** The yellow and orange carotenoids are inside the green leaf all summer, masked by chlorophyll, and only become visible when the tree breaks down its chlorophyll in autumn to reclaim nitrogen and magnesium — whereas the bright autumn reds (anthocyanins) are newly made in fading leaves. Meanwhile, Willstätter's circle dismissed Tswett's chalk-tube chromatography because chlorophyll decomposed on some adsorbents; Tswett died in poverty in 1919 at age 47, and his tube waited roughly 25 years (until Kuhn, Winterstein & Lederer 1931 and Martin & Synge's 1952 Nobel Prize) before becoming one of the most universal separation tools in chemistry and biology.
