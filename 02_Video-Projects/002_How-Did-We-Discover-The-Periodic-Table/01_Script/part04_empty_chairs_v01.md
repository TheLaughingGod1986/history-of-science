## Teach (say out loud after this minute)

1. An **element** is a pure chemical that will not break into a simpler chemical.  
2. Mendeleev sorted known elements by **atomic weight** into **columns** (families) — the start of the **periodic table**.  
3. He left **empty seats** for missing elements and predicted their properties.  
4. **Why groundbreaking:** chemistry could hunt a metal **before** it was found — discovery as navigation, not luck.

## VO (spoken · British · clear · any-age)

*(file: `02_Voiceover/part04_empty_chairs_v01.txt`)*

Eighteen sixty-nine. A Russian chemist named Dmitri Mendeleev sits at a desk covered in cards.

Each card is one chemical element — a pure substance that cannot be broken into a simpler chemical. About sixty-three are known.

He lines them up by atomic weight: how heavy each atom is compared with hydrogen.

Cousins fall into columns. Soft, reactive metals together. Choking halogen gases together. Stubborn loners in their own lane. That grid of columns is the start of the periodic table — a seating chart for the building blocks of matter.

Then he does something that looks like cheating. He leaves seats empty on purpose.

He names the missing seats. Eka-aluminium. Eka-boron. Eka-silicon. Not magic words. Placeholders. A predicted weight. A predicted density. A predicted way each unknown will behave when you heat it or dissolve it.

Why is that groundbreaking? For the first time, chemistry can hunt a metal before anyone has found it. Discovery stops being pure luck. It becomes navigation — walk toward a vacant chair with an address.

There is real risk. If nothing arrives, he invented imaginary metals. If something arrives matching his description, the table is no longer a poster. It is a machine for finding the unknown.

Sometimes he even trusts a family over a messy weight. Tellurium before iodine, because how they burn and salt matters more than a dirty number. Later a sharper count — atomic number — will explain those swaps. He does not have that yet. He has a pattern that refuses to die.

He publishes the map with holes in it.

Now the world has to wait, and hunt — and either prove him right, or bury him.
