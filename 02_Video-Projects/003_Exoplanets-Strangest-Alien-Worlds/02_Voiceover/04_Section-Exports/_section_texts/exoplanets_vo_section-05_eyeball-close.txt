Then comes a world that looks, from space, like a staring eye.
A tidally locked planet near a smaller, cooler star may keep one face in permanent day and one in permanent night. The star’s gravity synchronises the spin — like our Moon always showing one face to Earth. Ice can claim the dark. Desert can claim the light. Between them: a ring of twilight — a possible sweet strip where temperatures might be less cruel.
Call it an eyeball planet. The name is informal. The geometry is serious.
On the dayside, rock can bake under endless noon. On the nightside, perpetual midnight can lock water and gases into ice that never sees a sunrise. But along the terminator — the line between — light arrives at a shallow angle, forever. Climate models suggest that strip can be the coolest, most stable band on the world.
This is where imagination and science shake hands carefully. We do not know which eyeball worlds — if any — host life. We do know tidal locking is a common fate in certain orbits, and that climate models for such worlds are some of the most interesting puzzles in modern astronomy.
Some researchers imagine oceans hugging the twilight ring — a liquid bracelet between fire and frost. Others warn that thick atmospheres or strong winds could smear heat around and blur the eyeball pattern. Data, not daydreams, will decide.
Orbit likes the metaphor too much to ignore it: a planet that never blinks… and a thin eyelid of maybe.
He stays in the ring a moment longer than the schedule allows.
Because this world is not a spectacle only. It is a question mark written in geography — a reminder that “habitable” might look nothing like Earth’s round green map.

So… could any of these host life?
Honest answer: we do not know yet.
Diamond crusts and glass rain are not automatic homes. Hot Jupiters are brutal furnaces. Multi-star light can be beautiful and unstable for gentle climates. Eyeball twilight belts are promising in models — and still unproven as gardens.
What we can do is define the question better.
The habitable zone is the region around a star where, under the right conditions, a rocky world might keep liquid water on its surface. Not steam-only. Not ice-only. A range where water could pool.
It is not a guarantee. Venus sits near the inner edge of the Sun’s habitable zone in some diagrams — and it is a volcanic pressure cooker. Mars sits farther out — and it is a cold desert. Atmosphere matters. Magnetic fields matter. How the world formed matters.
The habitable zone is a starting line, not a finish line.
Then we look for clues: atmospheric gases out of balance… surface fingerprints… something that is hard to explain with rocks and sunlight alone.
On Earth, oxygen piled up in our air because life kept producing it faster than it was removed. Methane hangs around in our atmosphere in ways that would be odd without ongoing biology. Those are biosignature ideas — not proof templates, but conversation starters.
JWST and future observatories are already sniffing exoplanet air — measuring carbon dioxide, water vapour, methane, and more when a world transits. One day, a spectrum might show a combination that makes astronomers sit very still at their desks.
That is the quiet revolution of exoplanets explained in one sentence: we are learning to study the air of worlds we will never walk — not in this century, maybe — and still ask whether something breathes there.
Orbit’s eyes soften.
He does not need a handshake from an alien. He needs a true spectrum… and the patience to believe what it says.

Pull the journey into one thought.
Alien worlds are not a single idea. They are a catalogue of answers to a question Earth never asked itself loudly enough:
What else can a planet be?
A carbon-rich glittering crust.  
Sideways glass weather.  
A sky with three suns.  
Nights that still burn.  
A staring eyeball of day and ice.
None of that requires conspiracy. It requires telescopes, maths, doubt, and time.
Before nineteen ninety-five, exoplanets were a hope. Now they are a field. Kids born today may grow up with a number — thousands known — updating every year. They may never remember a time when we wondered whether other planets existed. They will wonder what those planets are like.
That shift is already happening.
And it requires a kind of courage: to let the universe be stranger than the stories we wrote before we could measure it.
The strangest planets in the universe are not waiting at the edge of imagination. Many are already in the data — waiting for better instruments to tell us their next secret.

The strangest planets in the universe are already on the list — not as posters, but as data that became wonder.
Orbit did not conquer them. He visited them the only way we can today: with evidence, imagination, and respect for uncertainty.
