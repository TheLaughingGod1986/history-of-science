# Why Doesn't the Moon Fall to Earth? (script master v01)

## PART 01: The Falling Moon (0:00–1:20). No chapter card.

Right now, as you watch this, the Moon is falling towards the Earth.

So why doesn't it ever hit us? And what does it have to do with an apple?

[VISUAL MUST: 0:06 — an apple drops from a branch in slow motion, and in the same frame the Moon hangs in the sky behind it · LABEL: THE SAME PULL?]

By the end of this film, you'll know how a young man in a farmhouse garden, an imaginary cannon and one careful sum showed that the pull that drops an apple also holds the Moon in the sky.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:14 — three things appear one after another on a wooden table: a red apple, a small brass cannon, a sheet of paper covered in sums · LABEL: AN APPLE · A CANNON · A SUM]

For thousands of years, people believed the sky followed different rules from the Earth. Down here, things fell. A stone dropped, a cup tipped, a rider fell from a horse. But up there, the Moon, the Sun and the planets moved in perfect circles, for ever, and never fell at all. Why should a stone fall, but not the Moon? It was one of the oldest mysteries in the world.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:32 — a painted old map of the heavens: the Earth at the centre, crystal spheres around it, the Moon and planets fixed on gleaming rings · LABEL: TWO SETS OF RULES]

Picture yourself on a clear night in sixteen sixty-five. You would see the Moon rise over the fields, huge and silent. You would feel the cold ground under your feet. And if you dropped a stone, you would hear it thud. Would you ever think that the stone and the Moon were doing the same thing?

[VISUAL MUST: 0:50 — a young man stands in a dark field, drops a pebble from his hand and watches it fall, then looks up at the rising Moon · LABEL: 1665]
[TEACH: For centuries, people thought the heavens and the Earth followed different rules. Things on Earth fell, but the Moon and planets seemed to move for ever without falling.]

One young man did. And it started in a year when the whole country was afraid.

## PART 02: An Apple in a Plague Year (1:20–3:00)

[CHAPTER CARD: An Apple in a Plague Year · 1:20 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:23 — Cambridge, 1665: college gates swing shut; a notice on the gate; students carry trunks out to waiting carts · LABEL: CAMBRIDGE CLOSES]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:38 — Woolsthorpe Manor, a small stone farmhouse; Isaac Newton (readable face, about twenty-three, long dark hair, plain dark coat, white collar) carries a stack of books through the door · LABEL: ISAAC NEWTON · WOOLSTHORPE]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:00 — the orchard in late summer; Newton sits on a bench under an apple tree, thinking; an apple falls in the grass a few feet away, not on his head · LABEL: THE APPLE]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:20 — Newton looks from the fallen apple up to the pale daytime Moon above the trees; a faint line of pull stretches from the Earth up towards it · LABEL: HOW HIGH DOES IT REACH?]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once. In the orchard the Explorer picks up the fallen apple, tosses it gently into the air, catches it, then holds it up beside the pale Moon and squints at the two of them]
[TEACH: Newton asked whether the pull that makes an apple fall could reach all the way to the Moon.]

In sixteen sixty-five, a terrible plague swept through England. To keep students safe, the university at Cambridge closed its doors. And a young student called Isaac Newton went home, to his family's farmhouse in the village of Woolsthorpe.

He had been born in that same house, on Christmas Day, sixteen forty-two. His father had died a few months before. Newton was so tiny, his mother later said, that he could have fitted inside a quart pot.

For almost two years, he worked alone. He studied light, invented new kinds of mathematics, and asked questions no one had asked before.

Many years later, when he was an old man, Newton told a friend how one of those questions began. He was sitting in the garden, thinking, when an apple fell from a tree.

The apple didn't hit him on the head. That part was added later. But it made him wonder. The apple always falls straight down, towards the centre of the Earth. The pull that drags it down works at the top of the tree. It works at the top of a tall tower, and at the top of a mountain.

So how far up does it reach? Could it reach all the way to the Moon?

It was an extraordinary idea. If the Earth was pulling on the Moon, then the Moon should be falling. But it was still up there, night after night. So what was holding it up?

## PART 03: The Cannon on the Mountain (3:00–4:40)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Cannon on the Mountain · 3:00 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:03 — Newton's own drawing comes to life: a giant mountain on a small round Earth, a cannon on the very top pointing sideways · LABEL: NEWTON'S CANNON]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:18 — the cannon fires a ball gently; it curves down and lands near the foot of the mountain · LABEL: SLOW]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:30 — a faster shot curves further and lands far around the globe · LABEL: FASTER]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:45 — the fastest shot curves down, but the Earth curves away beneath it just as fast; the ball sweeps all the way around and comes back past the mountain · LABEL: FALLING ALL THE WAY ROUND]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:10 — the cannonball's circle fades into the Moon's much larger orbit around the Earth · LABEL: AN ORBIT]
[TEACH: An orbit is a fall that keeps missing. A ball moving sideways fast enough falls towards the Earth, but the Earth curves away beneath it, so it falls all the way around.]

Newton's answer was so simple it is easy to miss. The Moon isn't held up at all. It really is falling. It just never lands.

To explain it, he imagined a cannon, on top of a mountain so tall it poked above the air.

Fire a cannonball sideways, gently, and it curves down and hits the ground near the bottom of the mountain. Fire it faster, and it flies further before it lands.

Now imagine firing it so fast that, as it falls, the round Earth curves away beneath it, just as quickly. What would you see? The ball keeps falling, and keeps missing the ground. It falls all the way around the world, and comes back to the mountain.

That is an orbit. Not something floating, but something falling, and moving sideways so fast that it never hits.

And that's what the Moon is doing. Every second, it moves sideways about a kilometre, and falls a tiny bit towards the Earth. Sideways and down, sideways and down, for ever.

But an idea isn't proof. If the same pull holds the Moon and drops the apple, Newton should be able to calculate one from the other. Could he?

## PART 04: The Moon Test (4:40–6:30)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Moon Test · 4:40 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:43 — a calm teach diagram: the Earth, and a line of sixty Earths laid end to end reaching out to the Moon · LABEL: 60 TIMES FURTHER]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:52 — a hand sprays paint at a wall from close up, a small dark spot; then from twice as far, a patch four times as big and paler · LABEL: TWICE AS FAR, A QUARTER AS STRONG]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:00 — a square of 60 by 60 small dots fills the screen, then shrinks the pull arrow on the Moon to one tiny dot · LABEL: 3,600 TIMES WEAKER]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:20 — split screen: on the left, an apple falls for one second beside a ruler; on the right, the Moon falls for one minute along its curve; both drop the same distance · LABEL: 1 MINUTE = 1 SECOND]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:45 — Cambridge, 1684: a visitor (readable face: Edmond Halley, about twenty-eight, fair wig, travelling coat) sits across from an older Newton in his rooms, books piled everywhere · LABEL: EDMOND HALLEY · 1684]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:10 — a London printer's shop, 1687; a thick book comes off the press; its title page reads PHILOSOPHIAE NATURALIS PRINCIPIA MATHEMATICA · LABEL: PRINCIPIA · 1687]
[TEACH: The Moon is about sixty times further from the Earth's centre than we are, so the Earth's pull on it is about 3,600 times weaker. Newton showed that this weaker pull is exactly what keeps the Moon in its orbit.]

Newton worked out that a pull like this should get weaker with distance. Twice as far away, it should be four times weaker. Three times as far, nine times weaker.

Why would it work like that? Think of a can of spray paint. Hold it close to a wall and you get a small, dark spot. Step back twice as far, and the same paint spreads over a patch four times as big, so every part of it gets only a quarter as much. Newton's pull spreads out in the same way.

The Moon is about sixty times further from the centre of the Earth than you are. So the pull on the Moon should be sixty times sixty weaker. Three thousand six hundred times weaker.

Then came the beautiful part. In one second, an apple falls about five metres. If the pull on the Moon is 3,600 times weaker, then in one minute, the Moon should fall about the same distance, five metres, along its curve.

Newton checked it against the real Moon. Years later, he wrote that the two answered pretty nearly. The apple and the Moon were obeying the same rule.

But Newton was a careful, private man, and his numbers weren't yet perfect. He put the idea aside for almost twenty years.

Then, in sixteen eighty-four, a young astronomer called Edmond Halley came to see him in Cambridge. Halley asked him a question. If the Sun pulled on the planets with a force that got weaker in just this way, what path would they follow?

An ellipse, Newton said at once. He had worked it out years before.

Halley was amazed, and he begged Newton to write it all down. The result, three years later, was one of the most important books ever written. Newton called it the Principia. And because the Royal Society had spent its money on a book about fish, Halley paid to print it himself.

## PART 05: Falling Around the World (6:30–8:20)

[CHAPTER CARD: Falling Around the World · 6:30 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:33 — a teach diagram of the solar system: the same faint lines of pull reach from the Sun to each planet, and from each planet to its moons · LABEL: ONE LAW EVERYWHERE]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:45 — a night sky over a German farmhouse, Christmas 1758; a farmer-astronomer at a small telescope spots a faint comet with a glowing tail · LABEL: HALLEY'S COMET · 1758]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:55 — a calm sea at a harbour; the tide slowly rises up the harbour wall as the Moon hangs overhead · LABEL: THE MOON PULLS BACK]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:15 — the International Space Station glides over a blue Earth; inside, astronauts and a floating apple drift together · LABEL: FALLING, TOGETHER]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:45 — back in the Woolsthorpe orchard at dusk; an apple falls, and the camera tilts up past the tree to the rising Moon · LABEL: THE SAME PULL]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once. In the orchard at dusk the Explorer sets the apple down on the bench where Newton sat, looks up at the Moon, and gives it a small, knowing nod]
[TEACH: The same law of gravity explains falling apples, the Moon's orbit, the paths of the planets and the tides. Astronauts float because they and their spacecraft are falling around the Earth together.]

Newton's law didn't stop at the Moon. The same pull keeps the Earth falling around the Sun, and the planets, and the moons of Jupiter. The Moon even pulls back on the Earth, and lifts the oceans into tides.

Halley used Newton's law to work out the path of a bright comet, and he made a bold prediction. It would come back in seventeen fifty-eight. Halley died years before it was due. But on Christmas night, seventeen fifty-eight, the comet appeared in the sky, right on time. It has carried his name ever since.

And you can see Newton's cannon at work today. The International Space Station flies around the Earth at about twenty-eight thousand kilometres an hour. It's not floating above gravity. It's falling, all the time, just like the Moon. The astronauts inside float because they're falling too, right along with it.

Imagine being up there, with an apple drifting in front of your face. It looks as if gravity has switched off. But it hasn't. You, the apple and the station are all falling together, around the world.

Why was this so groundbreaking? Because for the first time, the heavens and the Earth followed the same rules. The force that drops a cup off your table is the same force that steers the planets. One law, written in mathematics, that works everywhere we have ever looked.

So tonight, if you can see the Moon, take a moment to look at it. It is falling towards you, right now. And it will keep on missing.

An apple, a cannon and a sum. Together, they pulled the heavens down to Earth.

But gravity can bring more than apples down. Sixty-six million years ago, something far bigger fell out of the sky. And the clue was hidden in a thin layer of clay.
