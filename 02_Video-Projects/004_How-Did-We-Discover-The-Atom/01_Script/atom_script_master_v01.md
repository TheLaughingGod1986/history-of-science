## PART 02: The Table That Broke Its Own Rule (1:10–2:50)
[CHAPTER CARD: The Table That Broke Its Own Rule]
[VISUAL MUST: Manchester 1808 schoolroom; Dalton with wooden balls of different sizes on a balance. Then St Petersburg 1869: element cards sorted on a desk; printed tables mailed out as letters flying across a map of Europe (letters lock); Te and I cards swapped by hand]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once. He picks up the Te card, checks the weight, frowns, puts it back before I]
[TEACH: Dalton said each element has its own atom with its own weight. Mendeleev sorted by weight, but had to break that rule to keep the columns right.]

Manchester, eighteen-oh-eight. A schoolteacher named John Dalton puts the old idea to work. His **atomic theory** says every element is made of its own kind of atom, and each kind has its own weight. Hydrogen is the lightest. Oxygen is heavier. Gold is heavier still.

Now chemists had a ruler. Weigh the atoms and line them up.

Sixty years later in Saint Petersburg, Dmitri Mendeleev does exactly that. He writes each element on a card and sorts the cards by weight. The properties repeat: soft metals that fizz in water line up in one column, and sharp-smelling poisons like chlorine and iodine line up in another. He even leaves gaps for elements nobody has found. Then he prints his table and mails it to chemists across Europe.

But look closer. Tellurium is heavier than iodine, so by weight tellurium should come after it. Mendeleev puts it before anyway, because that's where it behaves right. Cobalt and nickel have the same trouble.

Mendeleev guessed the weights had been measured wrong. They hadn't.

So the table works, but it breaks its own rule. If weight isn't the real order, what is?

## PART 03: The Crumb Inside the Atom (2:50–4:30)
[CHAPTER CARD: The Crumb Inside the Atom]
[VISUAL MUST: Cambridge 1897; J.J. Thomson (readable face, moustache, round glasses) at a long glass tube with a glowing beam; magnet brought close, beam visibly bends; then a big cartoon "plum pudding" sphere with electron raisins]
[EXPLORER ACTS: none]
[TEACH: Thomson showed cathode rays are made of tiny charged particles, far lighter than any atom. Atoms have parts.]

Cambridge, eighteen ninety-seven. **J.J. Thomson** is studying a strange glow. Pump the air out of a glass tube, run electricity through it, and a beam shoots from one end to the other. Scientists called them cathode rays. But what were they made of? Light, or stuff?

Thomson brings a magnet close. The beam bends. He tries electric plates. It bends again. Light doesn't do that. Tiny charged particles do.

Then he measures them. Each particle is about two thousand times lighter than a hydrogen atom, the lightest thing anyone knew. And it doesn't matter which metal he uses. The same particles come out every time.

[LABEL: ELECTRON. Thomson called them "corpuscles".]

So the uncuttable thing can be cut. Every atom carries these tiny negative particles, and today we call them electrons.

But atoms have no overall charge, so something positive must balance them out. Thomson's guess was a ball of positive stuff with electrons dotted through it, like raisins in a pudding.

[LABEL: PLUM PUDDING MODEL]

It was a lovely idea. It was also wrong.

## PART 04: The Shell That Bounced Back (4:30–6:10)
[CHAPTER CARD: The Shell That Bounced Back]
[VISUAL MUST: Manchester 1909; Rutherford (readable face, big moustache), Geiger and young Marsden in a dark room; a stream of tiny glowing bullets hits gold foil; almost all pass through, one flies straight back; small flashes on a screen. Then a giant empty stadium]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once. He stands in the centre of an empty stadium holding up a pea]
[TEACH: Almost all of an atom's mass sits in a tiny positive centre, the nucleus. The rest is mostly empty space.]

Manchester, nineteen-oh-nine. Ernest Rutherford has a new kind of bullet: alpha particles, tiny and fast, thrown out by radioactive stones. His team, Hans Geiger and a young student called Ernest Marsden, fire them at gold beaten thinner than paper.

If Thomson's pudding is right, every bullet should punch straight through. Almost all of them do.

But about one in eight thousand bounces back.

[LABEL: "Like firing a 15-inch shell at tissue paper, and it came back and hit you." Rutherford]

In nineteen-eleven Rutherford explains it. Nearly all of an atom's weight is packed into a tiny, positive centre, which he calls the **nucleus**. The electrons sit far outside it. Everything in between is almost empty.

[TEACH CARD: If an atom were a stadium, the nucleus would be a pea in the centre.]

That raised a new question. If every element has its own nucleus, what makes one nucleus different from another? A Dutch lawyer and amateur scientist, Antonius van den Broek, had a bold guess: an element's place in the table might equal the positive charge in its nucleus.

It was a brilliant guess, but nobody could measure it. Yet.

## PART 05: Counting With X-rays (6:10–8:30, including the end screen)
[CHAPTER CARD: Counting With X-rays]
[VISUAL MUST: Manchester 1913; young Henry Moseley (readable face, neat hair) at a long tube with a little toy-train carriage carrying metal targets past the beam; a crystal splits the X-rays into lines; lines step down like ladder rungs, one rung per element; the table re-sorts itself with the numbers 1, 2, 3 … appearing in each box; a gap glows at 43, 61, 72, 75]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once, at the end. He steps back to see the whole table glowing with numbers]
[TEACH: Moseley's X-rays counted the positive charge in each nucleus. That count, the atomic number, is the real order of the table.]

Manchester, nineteen thirteen. Henry Moseley is still in his twenties. He builds a tube with a little train inside, carrying a different metal target into the beam each time. He fires electrons at each target, and each metal gives off X-rays.

[X-RAYS CALLBACK, the ONE beat (about 4 s): quick flash of Bertha's hand plate. LABEL: SEE "How Did We Discover X-rays?"]
It's the same invisible light Röntgen used to see bones.

Moseley measures the X-rays with a crystal and finds a pattern. Element by element, they step up in perfectly even jumps, like the rungs of a ladder. One rung per element.

Count the rungs and you get a whole number for every element: the positive charge in its nucleus.

[LABEL: ATOMIC NUMBER]

Now sort the table by that number. Tellurium is fifty-two and iodine is fifty-three. Cobalt is twenty-seven and nickel is twenty-eight. The broken rule fixes itself. Mendeleev's instinct was right all along. He was just using the wrong ruler.

And the ladder had missing rungs, at forty-three, sixty-one, seventy-two and seventy-five. Those were elements nobody had found yet. Over the next thirty years, every one of them was found.

Why do the columns repeat? The charge in the nucleus decides how many electrons an atom holds, and the outer electrons decide how it behaves. Elements with the same kind of outer electrons end up in the same column.

Two years later, in nineteen fifteen, Moseley was killed in the First World War at Gallipoli. He was twenty-seven. Many scientists believe he would have won a Nobel Prize.

So this is how we discovered the atom. First it was an idea. Then Dalton weighed it. Thomson found a piece inside it. Rutherford found its centre. And Moseley counted it. Every element has its own number, and that number is the order of the periodic table.

But the nucleus had one more secret. Some nuclei don't sit still. They break apart and throw out rays of their own. That's the next story.

[END SCREEN (20 s): X-rays + Periodic Table + playlist + Subscribe]

## Teach (say out loud after the film)
1. An **atom** is the smallest piece of an element. The word means "uncuttable", but atoms have parts.
2. **J.J. Thomson** found the electron in 1897. **Rutherford** found the nucleus in 1911.
3. **Moseley** used X-rays in 1913 to count the positive charge in each nucleus. That count is the **atomic number**.
4. **Why groundbreaking:** the periodic table's order is counting, not weighing, and it predicted four missing elements.

## Fact notes for Ben / UAT
- Tellurium (127.6) is heavier than iodine (126.9) but comes before it. Mendeleev suspected a mismeasured weight.
- Geiger–Marsden reported about 1 in 8,000 alpha particles deflected more than 90°. The Rutherford "15-inch shell" line comes from a later lecture and is widely quoted.
- Moseley's gaps at 43, 61, 72 and 75 were later filled by Tc (1937), Pm (1945), Hf (1923) and Re (1925). Moseley was killed at Gallipoli on 10 Aug 1915, aged 27.
- Thomson measured the charge-to-mass ratio. "About 2,000× lighter than hydrogen" is the modern ~1/1836, simplified for kids.
