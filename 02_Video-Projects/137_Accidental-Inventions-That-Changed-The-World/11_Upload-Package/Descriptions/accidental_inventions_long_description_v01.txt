Accidental inventions that changed the world — how did five laboratory mistakes survive the rubbish bin and remake modern life? In 1956, engineer Wilson Greatbatch grabbed a resistor 100 times too large and watched his oscilloscope beat like a human heart, launching the implantable pacemaker.
Follow five famous lab surprises — Greatbatch's pacemaker, Charles Goodyear's hot-stove rubber spill, Édouard Bénédictus's shatterproof Triplex flask, Alexander Fleming and Oxford's penicillin, and Spencer Silver and Art Fry's Post-it microspheres — and see why prepared minds turned each accident into proof.

In this film:
• Wilson Greatbatch's 1956 100× resistor error (1.8 ms pulse) and the June 1960 Chardack-Greatbatch implantable human pacemaker
• Charles Goodyear's 1839 Woburn stove spill, 270°F vulcanisation (U.S. Patent 3,633), and dying $200,000 in debt
• Édouard Bénédictus's 1903 collodion-lined flask, 1909 Triplex laminated safety glass, and WWI gas masks
• Alexander Fleming's September 1928 Penicillium dish and the 12-year gap until Oxford's May 1940 eight-mouse proof
• Spencer Silver's 1968 acrylate microspheres, Art Fry's 1974 choir bookmark, and the 1978 Boise sample blitz (90% buyer intent)
