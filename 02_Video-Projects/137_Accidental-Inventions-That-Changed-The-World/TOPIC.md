## 4. Story Angle & 5-Part Structure Sketch (UNVERIFIED Working Outline)

- **Lane:** Five familiar everyday objects (tyres, windscreens, antibiotics, pacemakers, sticky notes), each told as a tight ~75–90 s cause-and-effect lab beat: *what the inventor was actually trying to make → the mistake → the moment they noticed instead of sweeping it up → what changed.*
- **First 30 seconds (proof-first):** Open on Wilson Greatbatch grabbing the wrong resistor out of a parts box in 1956 — and hearing a circuit beat like a human heart (`0:05` proof apparatus). Answer the search premise at once: five real lab mistakes that reshaped everyday modern life.
- **5 Parts (7–9 min, ~1,200–1,300 words, Teach on Screen: ≥3 DATA+SCALE incl. 1839–1974 timeline, WHO cards, WHEN stamps, 2–4 FACT beats):**
  1. **Part 1 — The Wrong Resistor (Wilson Greatbatch, 1956 · Implantable Pacemaker):** Reaches for a 10 kΩ resistor, grabs a 1 MΩ resistor by mistake — circuit pulses at 1.8 ms every second, matching a human heartbeat.
  2. **Part 2 — The Hot Stove Spill (Charles Goodyear, 1839 · Vulcanised Rubber):** Dropping sulfur-treated rubber onto a hot stove turns brittle, melting gum into weatherproof elastic rubber.
  3. **Part 3 — The Dropped Glass Flask (Édouard Bénédictus, 1903 · Laminated Safety Glass):** A cellulose-nitrate-coated flask falls from a ladder and cracks into a spiderweb without shattering — later saving drivers behind windscreens. *(Alternative swap: Microwave 1945 if reviewer wants a teaser for `HOS:129`.)*
  4. **Part 4 — The Unwashed Petri Dish (Alexander Fleming, 1928 · Penicillin):** Returning from holiday to a contaminated Staphylococcus plate with a bacteria-free halo around *Penicillium* mould — and why turning it into medicine still took another decade.
  5. **Part 5 — The Glue That Wouldn't Stick (Spencer Silver & Art Fry, 1968–1974 · Post-it Notes):** Trying for a strong aerospace adhesive, making weak reusable microspheres instead, and turning a "failed" glue into a bookmark that stayed put — handoff to Cluster 3 single-subject deep dives.
