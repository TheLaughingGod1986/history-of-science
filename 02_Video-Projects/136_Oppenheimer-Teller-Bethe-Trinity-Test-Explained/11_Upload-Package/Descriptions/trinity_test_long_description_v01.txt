Trinity Test Explained: could the first atomic test in 1945 really have ignited Earth's entire sky? In July 1942, Edward Teller asked J. Robert Oppenheimer a question that froze a Berkeley seminar room: could nuclear fission heat surrounding air hot enough to fuse nitrogen-14 nuclei in a runaway chain reaction?
Discover how Hans Bethe and Emil Konopinski proved radiative cooling always wins, why reactor-bred plutonium-240 forced Los Alamos to invent microsecond spherical implosion, and how Enrico Fermi measured the 16 July 1945 blast from ten miles away using falling scraps of paper.

In this film:
• Edward Teller's July 1942 nitrogen-fusion question at Berkeley — and why Earth's 78% nitrogen atmosphere sparked the check
• how Hans Bethe and Emil Konopinski proved Bremsstrahlung and inverse Compton cooling drain heat faster than fusion (LA-602)
• where the famous "three chances in a million" story really came from in 1959
• Emilio Segrè's April 1944 discovery of plutonium-240 spontaneous fission and the microsecond explosive-lens redesign
• dawn on 16 July 1945 in New Mexico — rain delay, Fermi's side bet, and measuring a 10-kiloton blast wave with torn paper scraps
• trinitite green glass and what first-principles calculation proved
