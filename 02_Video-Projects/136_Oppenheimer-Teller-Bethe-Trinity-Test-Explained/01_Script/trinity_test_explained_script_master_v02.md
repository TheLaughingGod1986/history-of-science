## PART 02: Checking the Sky (1:35–3:15)

[CHAPTER CARD: Checking the Sky · 1:35 · ~1.5 s]

Hans Bethe and Emil Konopinski attacked the problem from first principles. For the sky to ignite, nuclear fusion in hot air had to generate energy faster than that hot patch could cool down.

[VISUAL MUST: 1:38 — 3D cartoon sunlit 1942–1943 theoretical physics room lined with wooden desks, mechanical calculators, and green chalkboards · WHEN: 1942–1943]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:42 — 3D cartoon over-the-shoulder shot of Hans Bethe writing hand-verified energy-balance equations neatly across green slate · WHO: Hans Bethe (1906–2005) — theoretical physics chief]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:47 — 3D cartoon close-up of Emil Konopinski checking cross-section tables with a brass slide rule beside stacks of scratch pads · WHO: Emil Konopinski (1911–1990) — nuclear theorist]

So they measured both sides of the ledger. Nitrogen nuclei carry seven positive protons each, so they repel one another fiercely. Only a tiny fraction of collisions fuse, even at extreme temperatures.

[VISUAL MUST: 1:54 — 3D cartoon close-up of chalk-drawn energy ledger: two glowing nitrogen nuclei deflect apart under strong electrostatic repulsion · LABEL: CHECKING THE MATH]

Meanwhile, as air turns into superheated plasma, free electrons whip past charged nuclei and violently brake, pouring out X-rays and light. Physicists call this braking radiation Bremsstrahlung. Along with inverse Compton scattering, it bleeds heat straight out of the fireball into the cold sky around it.

Here was the paradox at the heart of the physics. At every reachable temperature, radiation drained energy faster than nitrogen fusion could replace it, keeping a safety factor above one even under the worst assumptions. Trying to ignite the atmosphere was like trying to boil a frozen lake with a spark that loses heat faster than it can ever burn.

[VISUAL MUST: 2:10 — 3D cartoon period parchment explainer (inked in edit, hand-verified): a warm golden heat-loss curve climbs steeply like a wall while a faint nitrogen-fusion curve stays trapped beneath it; glowing radiation arrows stream out from racing electrons · DATA: animated Bremsstrahlung and inverse Compton cooling vs nitrogen-14 fusion curve — LA-602 safety factor stays above 1 at every temperature (~1.6 worst-case floor, 7.4 with inverse Compton) · SCALE: like trying to boil a frozen lake with a spark that loses heat faster than it can burn · LABEL: RADIATION WINS]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once. In the warm wooden hallway outside the Los Alamos chalkboard room, the Explorer peers around the doorway, tilts his head at the steeply rising cooling curve chalked on the slate, taps his blue notebook twice with a relieved nod, and slips quietly out of frame]

Wait — then why do so many people still repeat the story of "three chances in a million"?

Fourteen years after the test, in nineteen fifty-nine, a magazine interview with Arthur Compton quoted that figure as a safety cutoff. Hans Bethe later rejected the claim outright, calling atmospheric ignition simply impossible: their wartime math had left no chance of setting the sky ablaze.

After the war, in August nineteen forty-six, Konopinski, Teller, and a colleague wrote up the complete proof in Los Alamos report LA-six-oh-two. Its verdict was plain: energy losses to radiation always overcompensate the gains from nuclear reactions.

[VISUAL MUST: 2:57 — 3D cartoon cream-paper report folder stamped "LA-602 · AUGUST 1946" (hand-verified edit ink) resting on a warm oak archive desk · WHEN: August 1946]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:03 — 3D cartoon close-up on the declassified LA-602 cover sheet and neatly inked cooling margin bar · FACT: LA-602 published in 1946]

The sky was safe. Case closed? Not yet. Because in April nineteen forty-four, an unexpected mystery inside a quiet wooden shack at Los Alamos suddenly threatened to wreck the entire project.

## PART 03: The Plutonium Trap (3:15–5:00)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Plutonium Trap · 3:15 · ~1.5 s]

In early nineteen forty-three, Oppenheimer built the secret laboratory at Los Alamos, New Mexico, with Bethe leading theoretical physics. Their simplest plan for plutonium was a gun-type device, firing one subcritical piece into another down a barrel.

[VISUAL MUST: 3:18 — 3D cartoon wide establishing shot of timber laboratory buildings rising on the sunlit pine mesa of Los Alamos, New Mexico · WHEN: Los Alamos, 1943]

Then, in April nineteen forty-four, physicist Emilio Segrè tested the first tiny samples of plutonium bred inside a nuclear reactor at Oak Ridge. His detectors clicked far too fast.

[VISUAL MUST: 3:30 — 3D cartoon Los Alamos wooden laboratory bench bathed in warm tungsten light beside a sealed sample vial from Oak Ridge · WHEN: Los Alamos, April 1944]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:35 — 3D cartoon medium close-up of Emilio Segrè leaning over a glowing brass-and-glass neutron counter whose needle jumps rapidly · WHO: Emilio Segrè (1905–1989) — experimental physicist]

Reactor plutonium carried an unwanted sibling isotope: plutonium-two-forty. Plutonium-two-forty splits on its own — spontaneous fission — at thousands of times the rate of plutonium-two-thirty-nine, flooding the metal with stray neutrons.

[VISUAL MUST: 3:44 — 3D cartoon close-up of a glowing metallic sample emitting bright rapid neutron sparks across a dark velvet lab mat · FACT: Neutrons fired too early]

In a slow gun barrel taking milliseconds to close, a stray neutron would spark the chain reaction too early, blowing the core apart in a weak fizzle before it could assemble.

[VISUAL MUST: 3:55 — 3D cartoon conceptual physics sketch inked on period paper (strictly educational, zero blueprints): a slow horizontal tube stalls as an early stray neutron sparks a premature fizzle · LABEL: PLUTONIUM-240 FLAW]

The gun plan was dead overnight. Why? Because to beat plutonium-two-forty, Los Alamos had to compress a solid sphere symmetrically from every direction in mere microseconds — millionths of a second. In one microsecond, light itself travels barely three hundred metres, about three football fields.

[VISUAL MUST: 4:07 — 3D cartoon conceptual physics diagram inked on period paper (hand-verified edit ink, strictly educational, zero blueprints): the stalled tube dissolves into a clean glowing sphere squeezed evenly inward from all sides beside a timing bar · DATA: gun assembly vs spherical implosion timing scale — milliseconds (too slow for Pu-240) vs microseconds (symmetric inward squeeze) · SCALE: in one microsecond, light travels barely 300 metres — about three football fields · LABEL: MICROSECOND SQUEEZE]

How do you turn an outward blast into a perfectly round inward squeeze? With explosive lenses. Just as curved glass lenses bend scattered light rays to a single focus, paired blocks of fast and slow chemical explosives shaped diverging shockwaves into one smooth, converging sphere.

[VISUAL MUST: 4:28 — 3D cartoon animated optical analogy on warm cream parchment: paired fast and slow curved shock-wave wedges bend expanding ripples smoothly into one round inward wave, like glass lenses focusing sunlight to a point · LABEL: EXPLOSIVE LENSES]
[TEACH: Reactor-bred plutonium contained plutonium-240 whose rapid spontaneous fission would cause a slow gun-type device to fizzle, forcing Los Alamos to invent microsecond spherical implosion.]

Nobody had ever tested anything like it, and the secret math left almost no room for error. So Oppenheimer and his test directors insisted on one full desert experiment first. Would the microsecond squeeze actually work?

Then, at five twenty-nine in the morning, the rain cleared and the countdown reached zero.

Imagine holding dark welding glass up to your face ten miles away in pitch blackness, when the whole desert suddenly turns brighter than noon. What would you hear first? Nothing at all — just silent, radiant heat on your cheeks.

Forty seconds later, the air blast finally crossed those ten miles to Base Camp.

Separately from his pre-shot joke, Fermi was ready with the simplest instrument in physics: tiny scraps of paper torn from his notebook. Standing in still air, he dropped them from about six feet high before, during, and after the wave passed.

[VISUAL MUST: 5:58 — 3D cartoon dramatic warm golden dawn horizon (zero combat/casualty imagery): dark welding glass reflects a blinding flash across desert hills, followed forty seconds later by Fermi standing in calm air outside the Base Camp shelter dropping tiny white torn paper scraps from six feet high · FACT: Measured blast with paper]

As the shockwave swept past, the falling scraps jumped horizontally by roughly two and a half metres — about the length of a small sofa. From that single number, Fermi immediately estimated a blast equal to ten thousand tons of TNT.

[EXPLORER ACTS: once. Beside the low wooden parapet of Base Camp in the quiet desert wind after the wave passes, the Explorer kneels to pick up one of Fermi's fallen white paper scraps, holds it up against a brass ruler, smiles in pure wonder at the physics, and steps back out of frame]
[TEACH: After a rainstorm delay to 5:29 a.m. on 16 July 1945, Enrico Fermi measured the Trinity shockwave from ten miles away using falling paper scraps, estimating ~10 kilotons of blast energy inside a ~21-kiloton total release.]

Later measurements put the full yield near twenty-one kilotons — and a modern Los Alamos re-analysis near twenty-five kilotons — because Fermi's scraps measured only the air blast, not the energy carried away as light and heat. Even so, with nothing but torn paper, he nailed the right order of magnitude in seconds.

## PART 05: What the Equations Proved (6:50–8:20)

[CHAPTER CARD: What the Equations Proved · 6:50 · ~1.5 s]

When the dust settled, the desert sand beneath the vaporised tower had fused into pale green glass that scientists named trinitite. Overhead, the sky remained clear blue.

[VISUAL MUST: 6:53 — 3D cartoon sunlit desert sand close-up: tiny pale-green beads of smooth melted glass — trinitite — glint softly under a clear blue New Mexico sky · LABEL: TRINITITE GLASS]

The equations of Bethe, Konopinski, and Teller had held. Because radiative cooling strips energy from hot plasma faster than nitrogen can fuse, no fission explosion — and not even far larger fusion tests years later — could ever turn Earth's atmosphere into a self-sustaining fireball.

And look at the deeper lesson that links every scene in this story: when nature threw physicists two terrifying surprises — a potential atmospheric chain reaction in nineteen forty-two, and stray neutrons from plutonium-two-forty in nineteen forty-four — they did not guess. They calculated the cooling rates, measured the isotopes, reshaped shockwaves to the microsecond, and checked the answer with falling scraps of paper.
