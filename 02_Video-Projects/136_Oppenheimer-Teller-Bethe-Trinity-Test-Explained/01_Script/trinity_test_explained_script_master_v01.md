[TEACH: Radiative cooling — chiefly Bremsstrahlung and inverse Compton losses — drains heat far faster than nitrogen fusion can generate it, giving Earth's atmosphere a large safety margin.]

Hans Bethe and Emil Konopinski attacked the problem from first principles. For the sky to ignite, nuclear fusion in hot air had to generate energy faster than that hot patch could cool down.

So they measured both sides of the ledger. Nitrogen nuclei carry seven positive protons each, so they repel one another fiercely. Only a tiny fraction of collisions fuse, even at extreme temperatures.

Meanwhile, as air turns into superheated plasma, free electrons whip past charged nuclei and violently brake, pouring out X-rays and light. Physicists call this braking radiation Bremsstrahlung. Along with inverse Compton scattering, it bleeds heat straight out of the fireball into the cold sky around it.

Here was the paradox at the heart of the physics. At every reachable temperature, radiation drained energy far faster than nitrogen fusion could replace it. Trying to ignite the atmosphere was like trying to boil a frozen lake with a spark that cools a thousand times faster than it can burn.

Wait — then why do so many people still repeat the story of "three chances in a million"?

Fourteen years after the test, in nineteen fifty-nine, a magazine interview with Arthur Compton quoted that frightening phrase as a safety cutoff. Hans Bethe later rejected it outright as a misunderstanding: their wartime math had left no realistic chance of setting the sky ablaze.

After the war, in August nineteen forty-six, Konopinski, Cloyd Marvin, and Teller wrote up the complete proof in Los Alamos report LA-six-oh-two. Its verdict was plain: energy losses to radiation always overcompensate the gains from nuclear reactions.

The sky was safe. Case closed? Not yet. Because in April nineteen forty-four, an unexpected mystery inside a quiet wooden shack at Los Alamos suddenly threatened to wreck the entire project.

In early nineteen forty-three, Oppenheimer built the secret laboratory at Los Alamos, New Mexico, with Bethe leading theoretical physics. Their simplest plan for plutonium was a gun-type device, firing one subcritical piece into another down a barrel.

Then, in April nineteen forty-four, physicist Emilio Segrè tested the first tiny samples of plutonium bred inside a nuclear reactor at Oak Ridge. His detectors clicked far too fast.

Reactor plutonium carried an unwanted sibling isotope: plutonium-two-forty. Plutonium-two-forty splits on its own — spontaneous fission — at thousands of times the rate of plutonium-two-thirty-nine, flooding the metal with stray neutrons.

In a slow gun barrel taking milliseconds to close, a stray neutron would spark the chain reaction too early, blowing the core apart in a weak fizzle before it could assemble.

The gun plan was dead overnight. Why? Because to beat plutonium-two-forty, Los Alamos had to compress a solid sphere symmetrically from every direction in mere microseconds — millionths of a second, shorter than light takes to cross a football field.

How do you turn an outward blast into a perfectly round inward squeeze? With explosive lenses. Just as curved glass lenses bend scattered light rays to a single focus, paired blocks of fast and slow chemical explosives shaped diverging shockwaves into one smooth, converging sphere.

Nobody had ever tested anything like it, and the secret math left almost no room for error. So Oppenheimer and test director Kenneth Bainbridge insisted on one full desert experiment first. Would the microsecond squeeze actually work?

On the morning of the sixteenth of July, nineteen forty-five, the test device sat atop a hundred-foot steel tower in New Mexico's Jornada del Muerto desert. Scheduled for four in the morning, a fierce desert rainstorm forced Bainbridge to hold the countdown for nearly ninety tense minutes.

Before zero hour, Enrico Fermi famously offered a dark-humour side bet at Base Camp on whether the test would ignite New Mexico or the entire planet. Bainbridge was furious — he knew Bethe's math had settled the sky question, and worried Fermi's joke was spooking the guards.

Then, at five twenty-nine in the morning, the rain cleared and the countdown reached zero.

Imagine holding dark welding glass up to your face ten miles away in pitch blackness, when the whole desert suddenly turns brighter than noon. What would you hear first? Nothing at all — just silent, radiant heat on your cheeks.

Forty seconds later, the air blast finally crossed those ten miles to Base Camp.

Separately from his pre-shot joke, Fermi was ready with the simplest instrument in physics: tiny scraps of paper torn from his notebook. Standing in still air, he dropped them from about six feet high before, during, and after the wave passed.

As the shockwave swept past, the falling scraps jumped horizontally by roughly two and a half metres — about the length of a small sofa. From that single number, Fermi immediately estimated a blast equal to ten thousand tons of TNT.

Later measurements put the full yield near twenty-one kilotons — and a modern Los Alamos re-analysis near twenty-five kilotons — because Fermi's scraps measured only the air blast, not the energy carried away as light and heat. Even so, with nothing but torn paper, he nailed the right order of magnitude in seconds.

When the dust settled, the desert sand beneath the vaporised tower had fused into pale green glass that scientists named trinitite. Overhead, the sky remained clear blue.

The equations of Bethe, Konopinski, and Teller had held. Because radiative cooling strips energy from hot plasma far faster than nitrogen can fuse, no fission explosion — and not even far larger fusion tests years later — could ever turn Earth's atmosphere into a self-sustaining fireball.

And look at the deeper lesson that links every scene in this story: when nature threw physicists two terrifying surprises — a potential atmospheric chain reaction in nineteen forty-two, and stray neutrons from plutonium-two-forty in nineteen forty-four — they did not guess. They calculated the cooling rates, measured the isotopes, reshaped shockwaves to the microsecond, and checked the answer with falling scraps of paper.

Three years before that desert dawn, however, Enrico Fermi had already lit the very first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction in human history — quietly, with zero explosion, inside a pile of black graphite bricks beneath a Chicago squash court.
