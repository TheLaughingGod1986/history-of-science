Here is the assumption that killed people politely.
If sickness rides on smell, the cure is perfume and ventilation. If it rides on living seeds, the cure is stopping those seeds — on hands, on instruments, on breath.
Suddenly the enemy has an address. Not the night air. Not a curse. A hitchhiker.
Ignaz Semmelweis watched wards where doctors went from autopsy to childbirth without washing. Death rates screamed. Handwashing cut them. Colleagues mocked him anyway.
Why reject a fix that works?
Because it attacked identity. It said: you are the vector. Your prestige hands are the bridge.
Would you change your ritual if the data embarrassed your friends?
Some did. Many did not — until the evidence became impossible to sneer away.
Then the argument moved from wards to laboratories. The next leap was not a better smell. It was a flask that trapped the truth.
