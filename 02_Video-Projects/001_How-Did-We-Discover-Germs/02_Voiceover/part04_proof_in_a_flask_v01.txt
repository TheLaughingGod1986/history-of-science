Louis Pasteur did not win with poetry. He won with a flask shaped like a question mark.
Boil broth. Keep the swan neck intact. Air can drift — but dust and microbes settle in the curve. The liquid stays clear.
Break the neck, or tip the trap, and life blooms into cloudiness. Watch it: clear, clear, still clear. Then one breach — and the broth becomes a storm you can see without a lens.
If life simply appears from nothing, the old story holds. If life arrives as passengers from the outside, then disease can be a passenger too.
Could a curved tube of glass change surgery? Vaccines? Public health?
Inside that liquid sits a paradox the old world hated: emptiness that stays empty — until contact.
A flask can change a civilisation, because it turns an invisible claim into a demo you can repeat on Tuesday. You do not need faith. You need a result that returns.
Germs were not a metaphor. They were biology with an address. And once you have an address, you can block the road.
