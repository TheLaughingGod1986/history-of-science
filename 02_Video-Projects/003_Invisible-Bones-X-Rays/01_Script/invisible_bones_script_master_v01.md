## Hook (open cold · first seconds)

What if you could see a living skeleton — without cutting anyone open?

Würzburg, 1895. A dark lab. A glass tube. A cardboard screen that should stay dark… and then it glows.

Would you trust a light that has no name? What would you risk to prove it was real — a photograph of your wife’s hand, ring and all?

This is how we discovered X-rays — and why a cardboard glow changed medicine forever.

---

## Teach (say out loud after the film)

1. **X-rays** are a kind of invisible light that can pass through soft tissue more than bone.  
2. In **1895**, Wilhelm Röntgen in Würzburg found them by accident with a cathode-ray tube and a glowing cardboard screen.  
3. The **proof** was a photograph of Bertha Röntgen’s hand — bones and wedding ring.  
4. **Why groundbreaking:** doctors could look inside a living body without surgery — diagnosis stopped being only a knife and a guess.

[CHAPTER CARD: A Dark Lab, 1895]
[VISUAL MUST: Dim Würzburg lab — Crookes / cathode-ray tube, coils, dark curtains; ominous soft green-violet tube glow; no modern hospital yet]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once — peeks at the tube, steps back when it flares]
[TEACH: In 1895 physicists hunted cathode rays in sealed glass tubes. The lab was about invisible electrical “rays,” not about bones.]

Würzburg, eighteen ninety-five. A physicist named Wilhelm Röntgen works late with a glass tube almost emptied of air — a cathode-ray tube. Electricity races inside. Scientists think they are studying rays that stop at the glass.

The room is dark on purpose. Eyes need the dark. So does the proof that is about to arrive by accident.

Why chase rays you cannot see? Because the invisible is where the next map of nature often hides.

---

[CHAPTER CARD: The Cardboard That Should Stay Dark]
[VISUAL MUST: Cardboard sheet painted with barium platinocyanide across the room; tube covered; cardboard fluoresces green while tube is hidden; Röntgen hands OTS/faceless]
[EXPLORER ACTS: none this chapter — cardboard is the hero]
[TEACH: A fluorescent cardboard glowed even when the tube was covered — something invisible was leaving the tube and lighting the screen.]

Röntgen covers the tube so cathode rays should stay trapped. Across the room sits a cardboard screen painted with a chemical that glows when light hits it.

The cardboard should stay dark.

It does not.

A soft glow blooms where no ordinary light is shining. Something left the tube. Something that passes paper, wood, and soft things more easily than metal. He has not named it yet. He calls it X — for unknown.

Would you publish a discovery that looks like a haunted glow?

He does not rush the newspapers. He locks the door and tests. Block the path with a book. With a hand. With metal. The glow changes. Pattern, not ghost story.

---

[CHAPTER CARD: Bones Without a Knife]
[VISUAL MUST: Living hand silhouette in the beam — soft tissue reads faint, bones harder; ring dark blob; no gore; 3D cartoon clarity]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once — holds his own hand toward a safe stylised beam, eyes wide, then lowers it]
[TEACH: X-rays pass soft tissue more than bone, so a living skeleton can appear without cutting.]

Then the leap that still feels like science fiction.

A hand in the path. Soft flesh fades in the glow. The bones hold. A denser shape — a ring — sits darker still.

You are looking at a living skeleton. No knife. No pain. Just a new kind of light.

What happens to medicine the day you can see a break before you cut?

Fear arrives with wonder. Invisible light that writes bones on cardboard can also burn if you stay too long. Power and danger travel together — a theme medicine will learn the hard way.

---

[CHAPTER CARD: Bertha’s Ring]
[VISUAL MUST: Classic proof beat — Bertha’s hand plate: bones + wedding ring; then newspapers / telegraphs / stunned doctors (faceless); no Orbit]
[EXPLORER ACTS: none — proof photo is the hero]
[TEACH: The photograph of Bertha Röntgen’s hand (bones and ring) proved the discovery to the world.]

Proof needs a witness the world cannot argue with.

Bertha Röntgen places her hand in the beam. The plate records bones — and her wedding ring. When people see that image, the haunted glow becomes a fact.

Letters fly. Labs copy the tube. Doctors lean in. A bullet lodged where eyes cannot go. A break that used to be a guess. The body becomes a map you can read while the patient is still whole.

Why is that groundbreaking? Surgery is no longer the only way to look inside. Diagnosis gains a new eye.

---

[CHAPTER CARD: A New Kind of Seeing]
[VISUAL MUST: Simple teach diagram — soft cartoon body layers vs beam; then modern-safe hospital silhouette as payoff (not photoreal); last beat: question mark over the next invisible window]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once — watches the diagram, then looks at his own hand, soft return]
[TEACH: X-rays are high-energy light. They changed medicine by letting us see inside without cutting — and opened a century of caution about dose.]

So what are X-rays, in plain words?

They are light with more punch than the colours your eyes catch — a band of the electromagnetic spectrum past ultraviolet. They slip through soft tissue more than bone and metal. A detector (cardboard once; sensors now) turns that difference into a picture.

The zoo of medical guessing gained a seating plan for shadows inside the body.

You live inside that victory every time a dentist checks a tooth, or a hospital checks a fall, without opening skin first.

But every new eye asks a new question: what else are we still blind to — and what will it cost to see?

Hold on the last faint glow of a cardboard screen. Soft return. The next invisible window is already late for dinner.
