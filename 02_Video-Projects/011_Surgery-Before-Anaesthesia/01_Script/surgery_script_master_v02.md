## PART 01: The Fastest Knife (0:00–1:30). No chapter card.

Before eighteen forty-six, the best surgeon in the world was simply the fastest one. Why? Because the patient was awake for every second of it, and in Europe and America, nobody knew how to stop the pain.

In London, one surgeon was famous for his speed. His students timed him with their pocket watches, and he could take off a leg in just a few minutes.

## PART 02: A Gas That Made People Laugh (1:30–3:10)

[CHAPTER CARD: A Gas That Made People Laugh · 1:30 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:33 — Bristol, 1800: a young Humphry Davy (readable face, early twenties, curly hair, high collar) breathes from a silk bag of gas in a cluttered laboratory, then breaks into a grin · WHO: Humphry Davy (1778–1829) — young chemist · WHEN: Bristol, 1800]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:58 — a close shot of a printed page, the ink lines too small to read; a single highlighted phrase glows · LABEL: DURING SURGICAL OPERATIONS]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:15 — a candlelit parlour party: guests take turns breathing from a bag and stagger about laughing; one bumps his shin on a table and doesn't notice · FACT: Used at parties first]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:40 — Jefferson, Georgia, 1840s: a young country doctor, Crawford Long (readable face, late twenties, dark hair, waistcoat), watches the party guests from a doorway, frowning thoughtfully · WHO: Crawford Long (1815–1878) — country doctor]
[TEACH: Humphry Davy suggested laughing gas for surgery in 1800. For decades, people used it, and ether, mostly for fun.]

In eighteen hundred, in Bristol, a young chemist called Humphry Davy was breathing a gas called nitrous oxide. It made him giddy and made him laugh. People soon called it laughing gas.

Davy noticed something else. When he had a sore tooth, the gas seemed to take the pain away. So he wrote that it might be useful during surgical operations.

Nobody followed it up. Instead, laughing gas, and another vapour called ether, became party tricks. Travelling showmen let audiences breathe them on stage, and young people held "ether frolics", where guests staggered around, laughing.

And at those parties, something odd kept happening. People would fall over, bang their knees and elbows, and not feel a thing until the next morning.

In a small town in Georgia, a young doctor called Crawford Long noticed. In March eighteen forty-two, a patient called James Venable needed a lump removed from his neck. Long gave him ether on a towel, and cut. Venable felt nothing.

[VISUAL MUST: 2:58 — a quiet country surgery: Long holds a folded towel near James Venable's face; Venable's head tips gently back; a WHEN marker drops onto a timeline that slides in along the bottom of the frame · WHEN: Georgia, 1842 · DATA: a timeline that builds as each date is said: 1800 · 1842 · SCALE: 42 years from Davy's laughing gas (1800) to Long's ether operation (1842)]

It worked. But Long didn't tell the wider world. He didn't publish what he'd done for another seven years.

So if a country doctor had already done it, quietly, who would be the one to show everyone?

In Hartford, Connecticut, a dentist called Horace Wells saw a laughing gas show in December eighteen forty-four. The next day, he breathed the gas himself, and had a colleague pull out one of his own teeth. He felt nothing.

Wells was thrilled. A few weeks later, he went to a famous hospital in Boston to show the doctors there.

It went wrong. The gas wasn't enough, and the patient said it hurt. The students in the room called it a humbug. A fake.

But Wells's old business partner was in that room. A young dentist called William Morton. And he didn't give up on the idea. He tried a different vapour instead. Ether.

On the sixteenth of October, eighteen forty-six, Morton walked into the same Boston hospital, carrying a glass globe. A young man called Edward Abbott had a lump on his neck. Morton held the globe to his mouth, and Abbott breathed the ether in, and slowly fell asleep.

The surgeon, John Collins Warren, began. Abbott didn't scream. He didn't move. When it was over, Warren is said to have turned to the packed room and told them: "Gentlemen, this is no humbug."

[VISUAL MUST: 4:45 — a fast montage: newspapers rolling off a press; a ship leaving Boston harbour with letters in its hold · LABEL: THE NEWS SPREAD]

The room where it happened is still there. It's called the Ether Dome. And within weeks, the news was crossing the Atlantic.

A happy ending, then? Case closed? Wrong.

## PART 04: The Fight Over Who Was First (5:00–6:30)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Fight Over Who Was First · 5:00 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:03 — Morton at a desk, carefully hiding a bottle of ether inside a bottle tinted orange, then writing a label: LETHEON · LABEL: A SECRET RECIPE]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:25 — a slow push through a candlelit study; a letter on the desk, a quill beside it; the word ANAESTHESIA glows on the page · WHO: Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. (1809–1894) — doctor and poet · FACT: A poet named it]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:45 — the three portrait frames from the opening again, now lit: Long, Wells and Morton, with a fourth frame, Charles Jackson, sliding in beside them; quick back-and-forth cuts between the faces · WHO: Charles T. Jackson (1805–1880) — Boston chemist · LABEL: FOUR CLAIMS]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:10 — Boston Public Garden: a tall stone monument stands among autumn trees; the camera tilts up its carved sides; no names anywhere · FACT: The monument names nobody · WHEN: 1868]
[TEACH: Morton tried to keep ether secret and patent it. Four men claimed the discovery, and the arguments lasted for decades.]

Morton didn't want to give his discovery away. He coloured the ether and hid its smell, so nobody could tell what it was. He called it "Letheon", after a river in the old Greek stories that made people forget. And he took out a patent on it.

But doctors soon worked out it was ether. You can't keep a secret like that in a busy hospital.

A month after the operation, a doctor and poet called Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote Morton a letter. He suggested a new word for this sleep without pain. Anaesthesia. From the Greek for "without feeling".

Then the arguing started. Morton said he was first. His old teacher, a chemist called Charles Jackson, said the idea had been his. Horace Wells said he'd done it two years earlier. And from Georgia, Crawford Long said he'd done it before any of them.

The fight dragged on for years, in newspapers and in courtrooms, and none of the four men ended it rich or happy.

So who really was first? In a way, Boston gave its answer. In the city's Public Garden there's a tall stone monument to ether. It celebrates the discovery. And it doesn't name a single one of them.

Meanwhile, across the ocean, the fastest surgeon in London was about to try it for himself.

[TEACH: Ether reached London in December 1846, and chloroform followed in 1847. When Queen Victoria used chloroform in 1853, ordinary people stopped fearing it. Today, anaesthesia is very safe.]

On the twenty-first of December, eighteen forty-six, in London, Robert Liston tried ether for himself. His patient slept through the whole operation. When he woke, he is said to have asked when it would begin. It was already over.

Liston was delighted. He is said to have told the room: "This Yankee dodge, gentlemen, beats mesmerism hollow." The fastest knife in London no longer needed to be fast.

The next year, in Edinburgh, a doctor called James Young Simpson went looking for something even better. One evening, he and two friends sniffed a liquid called chloroform at his dinner table, and moments later all three were out cold on the floor. When they woke, Simpson knew he'd found it.

Not everyone trusted it. Then, in eighteen fifty-three, a London doctor called John Snow gave chloroform to Queen Victoria, for the birth of her son, Prince Leopold. If it was good enough for the Queen, people decided, it was good enough for them.

But here's the frightening question. Is it safe to be put to sleep like that? Doesn't it sometimes go wrong?

Today, for a healthy adult, deaths caused by an anaesthetic are very rare. About one in every hundred thousand. Imagine a stadium full of ninety thousand people, all going to sleep for an operation. On average, fewer than one of them would die because of the anaesthetic.

That's because a specially trained doctor, an anaesthetist, stays beside you the whole time. They watch every heartbeat and every breath, and they keep adjusting the medicine, so you stay just asleep enough, and no more.

Why was this so groundbreaking? Because it changed what surgery could be. Once the patient slept, surgeons could slow down, take care, and try operations that nobody would ever have dared before.

But there was still one problem. Many patients slept peacefully through their operations, and then fell ill a few days later. The answer to that would need a microscope, a spray, and a surgeon called Joseph Lister.

A gas, a bag and a globe. Together, they turned surgery from a race into a sleep.
