# Why Did Franklin Fly a Kite in a Storm? (script master v01)

## PART 01: Fire From the Sky (0:00–1:25). No chapter card.

In the summer of seventeen fifty-two, a grown man walked out into a thunderstorm and flew a kite into the clouds, on purpose.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:00 — a dark summer sky over a green field outside Philadelphia; a diamond-shaped silk kite climbs into rolling storm clouds; at the end of the wet string a brass key glints, and a tiny blue spark jumps from it to a knuckle (ANSWER IMAGE by 0:04) · LABEL: PHILADELPHIA · 1752]

Why would anyone do that? And why does almost every tall building you have ever seen still carry the answer on its roof?

[VISUAL MUST: 0:07 — a slow tilt up a modern city tower to a thin metal rod at the very top, a storm gathering behind it · LABEL: THE ANSWER IS ON THE ROOF]

By the end of this film, you'll know how a kite, a key and a glass jar proved what lightning really is, and how one simple idea stopped it burning our towns down.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:14 — three things appear one after another on a wooden table: a silk kite, an iron key, a glass jar lined with metal foil · LABEL: A KITE · A KEY · A JAR]

For thousands of years, lightning was a mystery. It fell without warning, split trees, killed animals in the fields and set roofs alight. Many people believed it was a punishment sent from the heavens.

Picture yourself in a town in those years, on a hot summer night. You would see the sky flash white, hear the thunder crack right overhead, and smell the smoke as a barn went up in flames. And you could do nothing but wait for the next strike.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:32 — a European town at night in a summer storm; a bolt hits a church steeple and the wooden spire catches fire; townspeople run with buckets · LABEL: LIGHTNING]

The tallest buildings were hit most of all. Church steeples were struck again and again. And when a storm came, the bell-ringers were sent up the tower to ring the bells, because people believed the sound would drive the lightning away.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:48 — inside a stone bell tower, two ringers haul on the ropes as rain lashes the louvres; a flash lights the tower white · LABEL: RING THE BELLS]
[TEACH: Lightning set fire to buildings and killed people and animals. Tall church towers were hit most often, and the bell-ringers inside were in real danger.]

It was the worst place to stand. One eighteenth-century count found more than a hundred bell-ringers killed by lightning in just a few decades.

So what was lightning? A punishment? A kind of fire? Or something else, something you could catch and study?

## PART 02: The Sparks in a Jar (1:25–3:05)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Sparks in a Jar · 1:25 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:28 — a fashionable room in the 1740s; a travelling lecturer rubs a glass tube with silk, and a guest's hair lifts towards it; the audience gasps and laughs · LABEL: ELECTRICITY · A PARTY TRICK]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:45 — Philadelphia: a printer's shop with a press and stacks of paper; Benjamin Franklin (readable face, about forty-five, plain brown coat, a plain short wig, no spectacles) leaves the press to a younger partner and carries a glass tube into the back room · LABEL: BENJAMIN FRANKLIN · PHILADELPHIA]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:05 — a glass jar lined inside and out with metal foil; a spark jumps from the ring-topped wire through its cork stopper to a fingertip with a loud snap · LABEL: THE LEYDEN JAR]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:30 — Franklin's notebook; a quill writes a numbered list down the page, 1 to 12; the words LET THE EXPERIMENT BE MADE appear at the bottom · LABEL: 7 NOVEMBER 1749]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once. In Franklin's back room the Explorer reaches a cautious finger towards the foil jar, flinches at the snap of the spark, shakes his hand, then grins and does it again]
[TEACH: Electricity could be stored in a Leyden jar and released as a spark. Franklin listed twelve ways a spark was like lightning, then said the idea had to be tested.]

In the seventeen-forties, electricity was a party trick. Travelling lecturers rubbed glass tubes with silk and made sparks fly from people's fingers and hair. Audiences paid to feel the shock.

In Philadelphia, a printer called Benjamin Franklin saw one of these shows, and could not stop thinking about it. He was so curious that he handed his printing business to a partner, so he would have time to study electricity.

His most exciting tool was a new invention called a Leyden jar. It was a glass jar lined with metal foil, and it could store electricity, then let it all go at once, in a bright spark with a loud crack.

And that spark reminded Franklin of something. A flash of light. A crack of noise. A sharp smell in the air. It was like a tiny thunderstorm in a jar.

So in November seventeen forty-nine, he wrote a list in his notebook. Twelve ways a spark was just like lightning. They both gave light of the same colour. They both moved in crooked lines. They both made a crack, set things on fire and could kill animals.

But a list of likenesses isn't proof. At the bottom of the page, Franklin wrote five famous words. Let the experiment be made.

But how do you catch a bolt of lightning, without it killing you?

## PART 03: A Kite in the Storm (3:05–5:00)

[CHAPTER CARD: A Kite in the Storm · 3:05 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:08 — a drawing on Franklin's desk: a small sentry-box on top of a tall tower, a long pointed iron rod rising from its roof into the clouds · LABEL: THE SENTRY-BOX]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:25 — Marly, France, May 1752: in a garden, a tall iron rod held up by silk cords between three wooden poles rises from a small sentry box on a stool set on wine bottles; an old soldier holds a brass wire in a glass handle near it and a spark leaps across as storm clouds pass · LABEL: MARLY, FRANCE · MAY 1752]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:45 — Franklin at his desk making a kite: a silk handkerchief stretched over two crossed cedar sticks, a sharp wire fixed to the top · LABEL: SILK · CEDAR · WIRE]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:05 — a field at the edge of Philadelphia under a dark sky; William Franklin (readable face, young man about twenty-one, tricorn hat) runs and lifts the kite into the wind while his father watches from the doorway of a wooden shed · LABEL: JUNE 1752]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:25 — the bottom of the string: rough hemp twine, an iron key tied where it meets a dry silk ribbon; Franklin holds only the ribbon, standing inside the shed out of the rain · LABEL: KEEP THE SILK DRY]
[TEACH: Franklin's plan was to use a pointed rod, high up, to draw electricity from a storm cloud. A kite could reach higher than any building in Philadelphia.]

Franklin had a plan. Points, he had found, were very good at drawing electricity out of the air. So he proposed putting a tall iron rod, sharp at the tip, on top of a high tower. If lightning really was electricity, the rod should pull sparks out of a passing storm cloud.

He published the idea, and it crossed the ocean. In May seventeen fifty-two, in a garden at Marly, near Paris, French scientists put up a tall pointed rod and tried it. As a storm cloud passed, sparks jumped from the rod. It worked.

But Franklin didn't know yet. News took weeks to cross the Atlantic. And Philadelphia had no tower tall enough. So, about a month later, he had another idea. A kite could fly higher than any steeple.

He made one from a silk handkerchief and two crossed sticks of cedar, so it would survive the rain. On top he fixed a sharp wire. The string was ordinary twine, and near his hand he tied an iron key, then a ribbon of silk to hold it by.

The silk was the clever part. Wet twine would carry electricity, but dry silk would not. So Franklin stood inside a shed, out of the rain, and kept the ribbon dry.

He told almost no one. He was afraid people would laugh if it failed. Only his son William, a young man of about twenty-one, came along to help.

William ran across the field and the kite climbed into the dark clouds. Then they waited. Imagine standing in that doorway, the storm overhead, holding a string that runs up into the sky. What would you feel, waiting for something to happen?

For a long time, nothing did. Then Franklin noticed the loose threads of the twine. They were standing up on end, and pushing away from each other.

## PART 04: The Spark From the Key (5:00–6:35)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Spark From the Key · 5:00 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:03 — extreme close-up: loose fibres on the wet twine lift and spread apart like the hairs on an arm · LABEL: THE THREADS STAND UP]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:15 — Franklin slowly brings his knuckle towards the iron key; a small blue spark leaps the gap with a snap · LABEL: A SPARK]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:30 — Franklin touches the key to the brass rod of a Leyden jar; then a second hand brings a wire near the jar, and the same spark jumps out of it · LABEL: LIGHTNING IN A JAR]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:55 — an inside page of the Pennsylvania Gazette, 19 October 1752, with a short column describing the kite · LABEL: PENNSYLVANIA GAZETTE · 1752]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:15 — a calm teach card: a storm cloud, a kite well below it, and gentle lines of charge running down the wet string; no bolt touches the kite · LABEL: NO BOLT, JUST CHARGE]
[TEACH: The kite was never struck by lightning. It collected electric charge from the storm cloud, which ran down the wet string to the key. That charge behaved exactly like electricity from a jar.]

Slowly, Franklin brought his knuckle close to the key. A spark jumped across, with a tiny snap. Exactly the kind of spark he had made a thousand times in his own room.

Then he touched the key to his Leyden jar, and filled it, with electricity from the sky. It behaved in every way like the electricity he made with glass and silk.

Lightning and the spark in the jar were the same thing. Electricity.

And here is the part that most people get wrong. The kite was never struck by lightning. If it had been, Franklin would almost certainly have died. Instead, the kite gathered a little of the storm's electric charge, which ran quietly down the wet string to the key.

It was still dangerous. The next summer, a scientist in Russia called Georg Richmann was trying a similar experiment with a rod during a storm, and he was killed. Franklin had been careful, and lucky.

In October, Franklin published how to do the kite experiment in his own newspaper. His letters were read at the Royal Society in London, and the next year it gave him its highest award, the Copley Medal.

But Franklin wasn't only interested in proving an idea. He wanted to use it. If a pointed rod could pull electricity from the sky, could it also keep lightning away from people's homes?

## PART 05: The Rod on Every Roof (6:35–8:30)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Rod on Every Roof · 6:35 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:38 — Franklin's own house in Philadelphia, 1752: an iron rod rises above the chimney; inside, two small bells hang on a wire by the stairs and begin to ring as a storm rolls in · LABEL: THE BELLS THAT RING]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:00 — a page of Poor Richard's Almanack with a small drawing of an iron rod fixed up the side of a house, its sharp tip above the roof and its foot in the ground · LABEL: POOR RICHARD · 1753]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:20 — a cutaway teach diagram: a bolt hits the tip of a rod on a church steeple, runs down the iron rod on the outside wall and into the earth; the wooden spire stays dark and safe · LABEL: A SAFE PATH TO THE GROUND]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:45 — a modern city skyline in a storm at night; lightning strikes the rod on top of a tall tower, again and again, and the tower stands calm · LABEL: STILL ON EVERY TALL BUILDING]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once. On a rooftop at golden hour, after the storm, the Explorer taps the base of a lightning rod, follows it down the wall with his eyes to the ground, nods and gives the rod a small salute]
[TEACH: A lightning rod gives lightning an easy metal path into the ground, so the electricity goes round the building instead of through it.]

In September seventeen fifty-two, Franklin put an iron rod on top of his own house. He ran a wire from it into the house and hung two little bells by the stairs. Whenever a storm cloud passed overhead, the bells rang on their own.

Then he told everyone how to protect their homes. In his popular yearly almanac, Poor Richard, he printed simple instructions. Fix an iron rod up the outside wall, with a sharp point high above the roof, and the other end buried deep in damp ground.

Here's how it works. Lightning is a giant spark, hotter than the surface of the Sun. It's looking for the easiest path to the ground. A wooden roof or a stone steeple is a terrible path, so it bursts into flame or cracks apart. But a metal rod is an easy path. So the lightning takes the rod, round the outside of the building, and goes harmlessly into the earth.

Franklin never patented his lightning rod. He wanted everyone to have it. Within a few years, rods were going up on churches, houses and ships' masts. And one by one, church towers became safer places to stand.

Why was this so groundbreaking? Because it was one of the very first times an idea from a laboratory reached out and protected ordinary people from one of nature's deadliest forces. It showed that the wild things in the sky follow rules, and that once you understand the rules, you can stay safe.

So next time a storm rolls in, look up at the tallest building you can see. There's a good chance you'll spot a thin metal rod at the very top. Some tall towers are struck by lightning more than twenty times in a single year, and stay standing.

A kite, a key and a jar. Together, they took lightning out of the realm of mystery, and put a little piece of it on every roof.

Long before Franklin, a young man in an English garden asked a different question about the sky. If an apple falls to the ground, why doesn't the Moon?
