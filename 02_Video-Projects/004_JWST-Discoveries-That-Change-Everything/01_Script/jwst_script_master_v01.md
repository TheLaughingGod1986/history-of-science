### SCENE 03 — Cosmic dawn map

[SCENE: 03 — cosmic-dawn-map]  
[SET: Orbit inside a holographic timeline — Big Bang → first stars → first galaxies; Webb “window” glowing at the far end]  
[ORBIT: walks/floats along timeline; pauses at “first billion years”]  
[CAM: tracking along hologram → push into the early window]  
[VISUAL MUST: readable timeline; Orbit pointing at early epoch]  
[SFX: soft holographic UI hum, gentle whoosh along timeline]

For a long time, our story of the early universe said: after the Big Bang, the cosmos took its time.

Gas cooled. Stars formed. Galaxies assembled. Black holes grew. Step by step.

Then Webb started sending back pictures of the first billion years — sometimes of light from only a few hundred million years after the beginning — and something felt… early.

Not “wrong.”  
**Early.**

Like arriving at a city that was supposed to be a construction site… and finding towers already lit.

[CHAPTER CARD: card_02_galaxies — ACT I · BEGIN · TOO EARLY]

[SCENE: 04 — galaxies-too-early]  
[SET: deep field; a luminous compact galaxy at cosmic dawn; Orbit tiny nearby, facing it]

[CAM: orbit around galaxy → Orbit reaction close-up]  
[VISUAL MUST: bright early galaxy; Orbit reacting; no UFO tropes]  
[SFX: bright distant galaxy shimmer, high crystalline sparkle, restrained]

Some of the galaxies Webb confirmed are luminous when the universe was only about **three hundred million years** old.

That’s young. Cosmically young.

And yet they shine. Some look bigger, brighter, more grown-up than many models expected for that chapter of time.

[ORBIT VO / soft:]  
…How are you already… you?

Scientists don’t shrug and invent magic. They do what science does when the sky surprises us: they check distances with spectra. They count how common these objects are. They ask whether star formation was more efficient… whether dust is tricking us… whether black holes are boosting the light… whether our recipes for galaxy growth need a rewrite.

[ORBIT: writes floating hypotheses; crosses none out yet]

The point isn’t panic.  
The point is: **the early universe made light faster — or differently — than our old sketches suggested.**

And that changes what “normal” looks like at cosmic dawn.

### SCENE 05 — Black holes too big too soon

[CHAPTER CARD: card_03_middle — ACT II · MIDDLE · TOO BIG TOO SOON]

[SCENE: 05A — black-holes-too-soon]  
[SET: early galaxy core; dark gravity well; soft accretion glow; Orbit braced at a safe distance]

[CAM: slow push toward core → pull back to Orbit]  
[VISUAL MUST: early supermassive black hole sense without gore]

Then came another surprise: black holes that seem… too massive, too soon.

In the local universe, giant black holes and their galaxies grow up together — a kind of cosmic partnership. But Webb keeps finding systems in the early universe where the black hole looks **overmassive** compared with the stars around it… as if the dark heart grew ahead of schedule.

[SCENE: 05B — little-red-dots]  
[SET: field of compact “little red dot” galaxies; Orbit peeks between them]  
[ORBIT: curious peek; mild startle at one glowing core]  
[VISUAL MUST: reddish compact early galaxies; Orbit readable]  
[SFX: soft red-dot pulse tones, muted warm clicks]

Some of these objects show up as compact, reddish sources — nicknamed **little red dots** — and inside some of them, the signatures of hungry black holes appear earlier than many textbooks casually promised.

[ORBIT VO:]  
You grew… fast.

How do you grow a mountain of darkness that quickly?

Ideas race: heavier “seeds,” short bursts of extreme feeding, quiet phases after wild growth. Webb doesn’t hand us a finished answer. It hands us a **better problem**.

And better problems are how cosmology levels up.

### SCENE 06 — Why it feels impossible

[CHAPTER CARD: card_04_textbook — ACT II · MIDDLE · THE TEXTBOOK GAP]

[SCENE: 06 — textbook-vs-data]  
[SET: Orbit between two floating panels — left: clean textbook diagram; right: messy beautiful JWST mosaic]  
[ORBIT: looks left → right → camera; soft laugh-eyes]  
[CAM: split-screen feel without hard UI chrome]  
[VISUAL MUST: clear contrast textbook sketch vs JWST richness]  
[SFX: paper page rustle into data mosaic wash]

So why does it feel like the pictures “don’t match the textbook”?

Because textbooks are snapshots of the best models we had — written before a new observatory opened a deeper infrared window on cosmic dawn.

When Webb finds more luminous early galaxies than old extrapolations, or black holes that look ahead of their hosts, it doesn’t mean science failed.

It means the map got an update.

[ORBIT: crumples the left panel gently; pins the right beside it]

That’s the James Webb Telescope’s real power: not drama. **Revision.**

Revision is how we learn the universe is stranger — and more inventive — than our first drafts.
