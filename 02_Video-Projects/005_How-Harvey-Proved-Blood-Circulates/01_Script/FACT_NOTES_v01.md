# 005 fact notes (v01, for `blood_script_master_v01.md`)

Every number and claim in the script, with the source. Primary text: William Harvey, *Exercitatio Anatomica de Motu Cordis et Sanguinis in Animalibus* (Frankfurt, 1628), cited below as *De Motu Cordis* (Willis translation, 1847, public domain).

| Script line | Fact | Source / note |
|---|---|---|
| "Your heart pumps more blood in half an hour than your whole body holds." | Harvey's own conclusion (ch. 9), from deliberately low estimates. Modern check: resting cardiac output about 5 L/min; adult blood volume about 5 L. | *De Motu Cordis* ch. 9; any physiology text (e.g. Guyton & Hall). |
| "For fourteen hundred years, doctors taught that blood was made fresh from food and used up." | Galen (c. AD 129–216), working in Rome from AD 162. His system: the liver makes blood from digested food; veins carry it out; tissues consume it. Taught in European universities until the 17th century. AD 170 to 1628 is about 1,450 years. | V. Nutton, *Ancient Medicine* (2004); C. R. S. Harris, *The Heart and the Vascular System in Ancient Greek Medicine* (1973). |
| "the heart wasn't a pump at all. It was a warm stove." | In Galen's scheme the heart is the seat of the innate heat that warms the blood and makes the vital spirit; its active motion is drawing in (diastole), not pushing out. | Harris (1973); Nutton (2004). |
| Tiny holes in the wall between the two sides | Galen held that blood seeps through invisible pores in the interventricular septum. | Galen, *On the Natural Faculties* III.15. |
| "in fifteen fifty-five, … Vesalius wrote that he could not see how even the smallest drop could pass through that wall" | 2nd edition of *De humani corporis fabrica* (1555). The 1543 first edition still accepted the pores. | C. D. O'Malley, *Andreas Vesalius of Brussels* (1964). |
| "London, sixteen-sixteen … starts teaching anatomy at the Royal College of Physicians" | Elected Lumleian lecturer in 1615; first lectures April 1616. | Royal College of Physicians, Harvey biography; G. Keynes, *The Life of William Harvey* (1966). |
| "A short, dark-eyed doctor" | Aubrey: "not tall, but of the lowest stature … little eie, round, very black, full of spirit". | John Aubrey, *Brief Lives* (written 1680s). |
| "He will spend the next twelve years on one question" | 1616 lectures to 1628 publication. The exact year he first grasped circulation is debated (the "circulation" note in the 1616 lecture notes may be a later addition), so the script says he spent the years on the question, not that he announced it in 1616. | Keynes (1966); Wear, "William Harvey and the 'way of the anatomists'" (1983). |
| Galen "in Rome nearly two thousand years ago, and a brilliant anatomist" | AD 162 onward in Rome; his anatomy came from animal dissection (apes, pigs). Not shown on screen. | Nutton (2004). |
| "Around sixteen hundred, in Padua … studied under … Fabricius" | Harvey at Padua 1599/1600–1602 (MD 25 April 1602). Hieronymus Fabricius ab Aquapendente, professor of anatomy and surgery. | Keynes (1966). |
| "tiny flaps, in pairs. He called them little doors." | Fabricius, *De Venarum Ostiolis* ("On the little doors of the veins"), 1603; he had shown them since 1574. | Fabricius (1603); K. J. Franklin's translation (1933). |
| "Fabricius thought they slowed the blood, so it wouldn't all pool in your feet." | His explanation: to slow the flow and spread blood evenly, so it does not collect in the limbs. | *De Venarum Ostiolis* (1603). |
| "Harvey, a doctor at Saint Bartholomew's Hospital" | Physician to St Bartholomew's Hospital, 1609–1643. | Keynes (1966). |
| "slow hearts of cold-blooded animals, like frogs and eels" | Harvey found warm-blooded hearts too fast to follow and used cold-blooded animals (frogs, snakes, fish, eels). Referred to only; ink drawings on screen. | *De Motu Cordis* ch. 1–2, 4. |
| "Each squeeze pushes blood out into the arteries, and that push is the pulse" | Systole is the heart's active phase; the arterial pulse follows it. | *De Motu Cordis* ch. 2–3. |
| "Say the left side of the heart holds about two ounces. Say just one eighth of that goes out with each beat. In half an hour, a heart beats more than a thousand times." | Harvey's numbers: the left ventricle holds "two ounces, three ounces, one ounce and a half"; suppose "the fourth, or fifth, or sixth, or even but the eighth part" goes out each beat; "in the course of half an hour, the heart will have made more than one thousand beats". | *De Motu Cordis* ch. 9. |
| "more blood than there is in the whole body" | "…a larger quantity in every case than is contained in the whole body!" | *De Motu Cordis* ch. 9. |
| "you'd have to eat more than your own weight every single day" | Harvey's lowest case: 1,000 × ¼ oz = 250 oz (about 7 kg) every half hour, so about 340 kg a day. Even far lower numbers beat a body weight per day. Harvey made the same point: the blood could not be supplied by food (ch. 9, 13). | Arithmetic from ch. 9. |
| "your heart pumps all of your blood round your body in about a minute" | ~5 L/min at rest vs ~5 L blood volume. | Guyton & Hall, *Textbook of Medical Physiology*. |
| The tied arm: tight band, the pulse below is gone and the hand keeps its colour, slowly cooling; artery throbs above; looser band, hand flushes, veins swell below. **Not "pale"** (fixed 3 Oct 2026, desk 5952762841). | Harvey, ch. 11, Willis tr. (1847): under a tight ligature the hand "retains its natural colour and appearance; in the course of time it begins to fall somewhat in temperature, indeed, but nothing is drawn into it." | *De Motu Cordis* ch. 11, Willis tr.: [Fordham Modern History Sourcebook](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/mod/1628harvey-blood.asp) |
| "the arteries, which run deep inside the arm … the veins near the skin" | Harvey's explanation: a tight ligature stops the deep arteries; a medium one lets arterial blood in but stops the superficial veins. | *De Motu Cordis* ch. 11. |
| Valves: blood pushed towards the hand stops at a valve; towards the heart it passes; an emptied stretch refills only from below | Harvey's four figures of the forearm veins (from Fabricius's plates). | *De Motu Cordis* ch. 13 and figures 1–4. |
| "Harvey showed these experiments at the College for more than nine years" | Dedication to Dr Argent and the College: he had demonstrated it "for nine years and more" before them. | *De Motu Cordis*, dedication. |
| "in sixteen twenty-eight … a small Latin book, printed in Frankfurt" | 72 pages, printed by William Fitzer, Frankfurt, 1628. | Keynes (1966). |
| "On the Motion of the Heart and Blood" | Short English title of *De Motu Cordis et Sanguinis*. | — |
| Aubrey: "fell mightily in his practice", people thought him crack-brained | "…he fell mightily in his Practize, and that 'twas beleeved by the vulgar that he was crack-brained." Aubrey knew Harvey; spelling modernised in the VO. | Aubrey, *Brief Lives*. |
| "Harvey guessed the blood passed through tiny gaps in the flesh … He never saw them." | Harvey proposed passage through "porosities" of the flesh; he never saw capillaries. | *De Motu Cordis* ch. 11; *De Circulatione Sanguinis* (1649). |
| "He died in sixteen fifty-seven" | 3 June 1657, aged 79. | Keynes (1966). |
| "Four years later, in Bologna, … Malpighi looked at the thin lung of a frog through a microscope" | *De pulmonibus* (1661), letters to Borelli; capillaries in the frog lung. | M. Malpighi, *De pulmonibus* (1661); H. B. Adelmann, *Marcello Malpighi and the Evolution of Embryology* (1966). |
| "capillaries, from the Latin word for hair" | *capillaris*, from *capillus* (hair). | OED. |
| "Within ten years of Harvey's death, doctors in England and France were trying the first blood transfusions" | Richard Lower, Oxford/London, animal-to-animal 1665–66; Jean-Baptiste Denys, Paris, first animal-to-human 1667. On screen: tools only. | A. Farr, "The first human blood transfusion", *Medical History* (1980). |
| Blood pressure cuff "squeezes until the blood stops getting in, then lets go, and listens for the moment it starts to flow again" | Cuff method: inflate above systolic pressure to stop arterial flow, deflate, listen for the Korotkoff sounds. | Any clinical-methods text. |
| "Thirteen years later, a Dutch cloth merchant … looked into a drop of pond water, and found it full of life" | Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, draper of Delft; first report of microorganisms in lake water, 1674 (letter to the Royal Society). 1661 + 13 = 1674. Hands off to film 001 (germs). | Leeuwenhoek's letter of 7 Sep 1674, *Phil. Trans.*; B. J. Ford, *The Leeuwenhoek Legacy* (1991). |
