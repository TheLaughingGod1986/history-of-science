[TEACH: Galen taught that the liver turns food into blood, the veins carry it out, and the body uses it up. Harvey's teacher Fabricius had found valves inside the veins, but read them the wrong way.]

Galen was a Greek doctor in Rome nearly two thousand years ago, and a brilliant anatomist. For fourteen centuries, hardly anyone dared to argue with his books.

Here is what Galen taught. You eat. Your food turns into a milky juice, and your liver turns that juice into blood. Your veins carry the blood out to every part of you, and your flesh soaks it up as nourishment.

The heart's job was to warm the blood. A little of it was meant to seep from the right side of the heart to the left, through tiny holes in the wall between them. Nobody had ever seen those holes. Doctors believed they had to be there anyway.

Then, in fifteen fifty-five, the great anatomist Andreas Vesalius wrote that he could not see how even the smallest drop could pass through that wall. So why did everyone keep believing it? Because nobody had a better answer.

Around sixteen hundred, in Padua, a young Englishman named William Harvey studied under an old professor called Fabricius. Fabricius had found something strange inside the veins: tiny flaps, in pairs. He called them little doors.

But why would a vein need doors? Fabricius thought they slowed the blood, so it wouldn't all pool in your feet. He held the most important clue in the story, and he read it the wrong way.

Harvey took the clue home to London.

## PART 03: The Sum That Broke the Old Idea (2:25–3:55)

[VISUAL MUST: 3:40 — the same red stream leaves the heart and comes back to it, round and round, a glowing loop · LABEL: THE SAME BLOOD]
[TEACH: The heart is a pump: each squeeze pushes blood into the arteries, and that push is your pulse. Harvey's sum showed the heart moves far more blood than food could ever make, so the same blood must go round.]

Picture yourself in that room. It's cold, the candles are lit, and you're on a hard wooden bench. Harvey, a doctor at Saint Bartholomew's Hospital, has been chosen to give the College's anatomy lectures.

He has spent years watching the slow hearts of cold-blooded animals, like frogs and eels, where each beat is easy to see. And what he saw was simple. The heart isn't a stove. It's a muscle, and it squeezes. Each squeeze pushes blood out into the arteries, and that push is the pulse you feel in your wrist.

Then he asks how much blood each squeeze pushes out. He picks small numbers on purpose. Say the left side of the heart holds about two ounces. Say just one eighth of that goes out with each beat. In half an hour, a heart beats more than a thousand times.

Multiply it out. In half an hour, the heart pushes out more blood than there is in the whole body.

Why does that matter? Because if the liver made all that blood fresh from food, you'd have to eat more than your own weight every single day. Nobody does. And if the body used it all up, where would it go?

There's only one answer that works. It's the same blood. The heart pumps it out through the arteries, and it comes back through the veins, round and round. It circulates.

We now know he was being very careful: your heart pumps all of your blood round your body in about a minute.

But a sum isn't proof. Harvey needed something people could see with their own eyes.

## PART 04: The Tied Arm (3:55–5:40)

[VISUAL MUST: 5:22 — 1628: a small printed book comes off a press in Frankfurt; Latin title page (checked by hand) · LABEL: DE MOTU CORDIS · 1628]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:32 — a London street; people point and whisper as Harvey passes in his gown]
[TEACH: Blood goes into a limb through the deep arteries and comes back through the veins. The valves only let blood in the veins flow towards the heart, so the blood must travel in a loop.]

Harvey ties a band round a man's upper arm and pulls it really tight. Below the band, the hand goes pale and cold. Above it, the artery throbs. Why? The tight band has stopped the blood getting in.

Then he loosens it, just a little. Now the arteries, which run deep inside the arm, can push blood under the band. But the veins near the skin are still squeezed shut. The hand flushes red, and the veins below the band swell up like ropes.

So blood comes into the arm through the arteries, and it leaves through the veins. In, and back out.

Now the little doors. Along the swollen veins you can see small knots. Each one is a valve, the flaps Fabricius found. Harvey presses a finger on a vein and pushes the blood towards the hand. It stops dead at the next valve. He pushes it the other way, towards the heart, and it slides through easily.

He empties a short stretch of vein between two fingers. It stays empty until he lifts the finger nearer the hand. Then it fills straight back up, from below.

Every door in every vein opens one way: towards the heart. Fabricius thought they slowed the blood. Harvey saw they were steering it home.

You can see a little of this yourself. Look at the veins on the back of your hand. The blood inside them is on its way back to your heart.

Harvey showed these experiments at the College for more than nine years. Then, in sixteen twenty-eight, he published them in a small Latin book, printed in Frankfurt: On the Motion of the Heart and Blood.

Not everyone was impressed. John Aubrey, who knew him, later wrote that after the book came out, Harvey "fell mightily in his practice", and many people thought he was crack-brained.

And one gap was still open. Harvey knew it.

Harvey's circle had a hole in it. How did the blood get from the arteries into the veins? Somewhere in your body they had to join, and nobody could see where.

Harvey guessed the blood passed through tiny gaps in the flesh, too small to see. He never saw them. He died in sixteen fifty-seven, with his great idea still missing one piece.

Four years later, in Bologna, a doctor called Marcello Malpighi looked at the thin lung of a frog through a microscope. And there it was. A net of tiny vessels, finer than hairs, carrying blood from the smallest arteries into the smallest veins. We call them capillaries, from the Latin word for hair.

The circle was closed. Your blood leaves your heart, runs out through your arteries, crosses through the capillaries, and comes home through your veins.

So why was this so groundbreaking? Because once you know the blood goes round, you know that anything put into one vein reaches the whole body. Within ten years of Harvey's death, doctors in England and France were trying the first blood transfusions. That idea makes no sense at all unless your blood circulates.

And the next time a nurse wraps a cuff round your arm, think of Harvey. A blood pressure cuff is his band. It squeezes until the blood stops getting in, then lets go, and listens for the moment it starts to flow again.

A sum, a band, and a row of little doors. Harvey proved that your heart is a pump and that your blood goes round, long before anyone saw the last link.

Thirteen years later, a Dutch cloth merchant with an even better lens looked into a drop of pond water, and found it full of life. That is where the story of germs begins.
