Picture yourself in his lecture room in London. It's cold, the candles are lit, and you're on a hard wooden bench.

Harvey has spent years watching the slow hearts of cold-blooded animals, like frogs and eels, where each beat is easy to see. And what he saw was simple. The heart isn't a stove. It's a muscle, and it squeezes. Each squeeze pushes blood out into the arteries, and that push is the pulse you feel in your wrist.

Then he asks how much blood each squeeze pushes out. He picks small numbers on purpose. Say the left side of the heart holds about two ounces. Say just one eighth of that goes out with each beat. In half an hour, a heart beats more than a thousand times.

Multiply it out. In half an hour, the heart pushes out more blood than there is in the whole body.

Why does that matter? Because if the liver made all that blood fresh from food, you'd have to eat more than your own weight every single day. Nobody does. And if the body used it all up, where would it go?

There's only one answer that works. It's the same blood. The heart pumps it out through the arteries, and it comes back through the veins, round and round. It circulates.

We now know he was being very careful: your heart pumps all of your blood round your body in about a minute.

But a sum isn't proof. Harvey needed something people could see with their own eyes.
