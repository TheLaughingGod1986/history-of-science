Harvey showed his experiments at the College for more than nine years before he printed a word. The most famous one needs nothing but a strip of linen.

He ties a band round a man's upper arm and pulls it tight. Below the band, the hand goes pale and cold. Above it, the artery throbs. Why? The tight band has stopped the blood getting in.

Then he loosens it, just a little. Now the arteries, which run deep inside the arm, can push blood under the band. But the veins near the skin are still squeezed shut. The hand flushes red, and the veins below the band swell up like ropes.

So blood comes in through the arteries and leaves through the veins.

Now the little doors. Along the swollen veins you can see small knots. Each one is a valve, the flaps Fabricius found. Harvey presses a finger on a vein and pushes the blood towards the hand. It stops dead at the next valve. He pushes it the other way, towards the heart, and it slides through easily.

He empties a short stretch of vein between two fingers. It stays empty until he lifts the finger nearer the hand. Then it fills straight back up, from below.

Every door in every vein opens one way: towards the heart. Fabricius thought they slowed the blood. Harvey saw they were steering it home.

You can see a little of this yourself. Look at the veins on the back of your hand. The blood inside them is on its way back to your heart.

In sixteen twenty-eight, Harvey published it all in a small Latin book, printed in Frankfurt: On the Motion of the Heart and Blood.

Not everyone was impressed. John Aubrey, who knew him, later wrote that after the book came out, Harvey "fell mightily in his practice", and many people thought he was crack-brained.

And one gap was still open. Harvey knew it.
