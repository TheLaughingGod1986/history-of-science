On the first of July, Jenner did the test. He gave James a standard variolation, the old treatment that used real smallpox matter, which would normally cause at least a mild illness.

Then he waited. One day. Three days. A week.

Nothing happened. In Jenner's words, no disease followed. Months later, he tried again. Still nothing. The cowpox had protected the boy.

Jenner wasn't the first to try cowpox. Benjamin Jesty had done it twenty-two years earlier. What Jenner did that was new was test it, carefully, and then publish every case, so any doctor could check his work and do it themselves.

He tested more people, and in seventeen ninety-eight he paid to print a short book. He called the disease by a Latin name, variolae vaccinae, which means smallpox of the cow. From vacca, the Latin word for cow, came the name for the treatment: vaccination.

Not everyone believed him. Some doctors mocked the idea, and cartoonists drew people sprouting little cow's heads after their jab. But the results were hard to argue with.

Jenner sent cowpox matter to anyone who asked, and in a little hut in his own garden in Berkeley he vaccinated poor families for free. Within a few years, doctors were vaccinating people across Britain, Europe and the Americas.
