Cows sometimes caught a disease of their own, called cowpox. It made small blisters on their udders. And the milkmaids who milked them sometimes caught it too, on their hands. It was a mild illness. A few sore spots, a day or two of feeling unwell, and that was all.

But in the dairy country, people said something odd. Anyone who had had cowpox would never catch smallpox.

Imagine being a milkmaid in a smallpox year. While your neighbours lock their doors, you walk to market without fear, because you had a few sore spots on your hands from the cows. It sounds like an old wives' tale. But it kept coming true.

Some farmers even acted on it. In seventeen seventy-four, a Dorset farmer called Benjamin Jesty scratched cowpox into the arms of his wife and his two sons, to protect them. It worked, but he told almost no one, and he never wrote it down for doctors to read.

Not far away, in the little town of Berkeley, a country doctor called Edward Jenner had heard the same story from his patients. Jenner was a curious man. He studied cuckoos and hedgehogs, sent up one of the first hydrogen balloons ever seen in England, and wrote poems for his friends. But the milkmaids' story was the puzzle he could not leave alone. As a boy, Jenner had been put through variolation himself. He knew how hard it was.

His old teacher in London, the surgeon John Hunter, had once given him some famous advice. Why think? Why not try the experiment?

So Jenner started collecting cases, of people who had caught cowpox and then never caught smallpox. For years, he wrote them down.

But a list of stories isn't proof. Jenner needed a test.
