In May 1796, a young dairymaid called Sarah Nelmes came to Jenna with cowpox on her hand.  She'd caught it from a cow on a nearby farm, a cow later remembered by the name Blossom.  Jenna saw his chance. Now, the 14th of May, he'd on Sarah's hand and placed it in two small  scratches on the arm of a boy called James Phipps. In his own notes, Jenna described him simply  as a healthy boy, about eight years old. Over the next week, James felt little unwell,  a slight fever, a poor appetite, a night of restlessness. Then, just as Jenna had hoped,  he was perfectly well again. Picture Jenna in those days, watching the boy run about the garden.  If the old saying was wrong, he had given a child a disease for nothing. If it was right,  he might be holding the key to the most feared illness on earth. So James had caught cowpox.  Now came the question that really mattered. Would it protect him from smallpox?
