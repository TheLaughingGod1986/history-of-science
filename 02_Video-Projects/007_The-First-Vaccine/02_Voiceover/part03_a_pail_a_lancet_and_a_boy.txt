In May seventeen ninety-six, a young dairymaid called Sarah Nelmes came to Jenner with cowpox on her hand. She had caught it from a cow on a nearby farm. A cow later remembered by the name Blossom.

Jenner saw his chance.

On the fourteenth of May, he took a little matter from one of the spots on Sarah's hand, and placed it in two small scratches on the arm of a boy called James Phipps. In his own notes, Jenner described him simply as a healthy boy, about eight years old.

Over the next week, James felt a little unwell. A slight fever, a poor appetite, a night of restlessness. Then, just as Jenner had hoped, he was perfectly well again.

Picture Jenner in those days, watching the boy run about the garden. If the old saying was wrong, he had given a child a disease for nothing. If it was right, he might be holding the key to the most feared illness on Earth.

So James had caught cowpox. Now came the question that really mattered. Would it protect him from smallpox?
