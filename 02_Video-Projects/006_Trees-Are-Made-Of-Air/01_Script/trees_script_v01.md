Here is exactly what he did. He took a big clay pot and filled it with two hundred pounds of soil, dried in a furnace first, so no hidden water could fool the scales. Then he planted a young willow that weighed five pounds.

He covered the pot with a metal lid full of tiny holes, so rain could get in, but dust could not. And for five years, he gave that tree nothing but pure water.

Picture yourself in that garden. Every spring, the leaves burst out. Every autumn, they fall. Year after year the willow grows taller, and the pot of soil never changes.

Then, after five years, he pulled the tree out and weighed it. A hundred and sixty-nine pounds, about the weight of a grown man. And that didn't even count the leaves that had fallen in four autumns.

Now came the moment that mattered. He dried the soil again and put it back on the scales. Two hundred pounds, less about two ounces.

A hundred and sixty-four pounds of new tree, and only two ounces of soil gone. The tree had not eaten the soil. So why had it grown?

Van Helmont's answer seemed obvious. The only thing he had added was water. So, he wrote, the wood, the bark and the roots had come from water alone.

It was a brilliant experiment, and his answer was mostly wrong.

And here is the strangest part of the mystery. Van Helmont also burned sixty-two pounds of charcoal and found just one pound of ash left behind. The rest had vanished into the air, as a wild, invisible spirit. He gave it a new name: gas. We now know that gas was mostly carbon dioxide.

He had discovered the very thing his tree was made of. He never put the two together.

Nearly a century later, the question moved from the soil to the sky.

Just outside London, in seventeen twenty-seven, a country parson called Stephen Hales published careful measurements of how much water plants drink up and give off through their leaves. And he made a bold guess. Plants, he wrote, probably take some of their food from the air.

But a guess isn't proof.

Leeds, seventeen seventy-one. Joseph Priestley, a minister who lived next door to a brewery, was fascinated by air. He knew that if you burn a candle inside a sealed glass jar, the flame soon dies, and the air left behind won't let another candle burn. Priestley called that air injured.

So, on the seventeenth of August, he slipped a sprig of mint into a jar of that injured air, and waited.

Ten days later, he lowered in another candle. It burned perfectly well.

Then he tried air that mice had made stale with their breathing. In the jar where mint had grown, a mouse lived perfectly well.

Why does that matter? Because every fire and every animal on Earth uses the air up. Something had to be putting it back. Priestley wrote that the damage animals do to the air is repaired by "the vegetable creation." Plants repair the air.

But there was a problem. Sometimes his plants repaired the air. Sometimes they didn't. And nobody could say why.

Summer, seventeen seventy-nine. A Dutch doctor called Jan Ingenhousz took a house in the countryside just outside London, and in about three months, he ran more than five hundred experiments.

He plunged fresh leaves into jars of water and watched. Picture yourself at his window. In the sunshine you see tiny silver bubbles appear on the green leaf and rise to the top.

He collected the bubbles and tested the gas. A candle flame burned brighter in it than in ordinary air. It was the gas Priestley had discovered a few years earlier, which we now call oxygen.

Then came the clue that solved Priestley's puzzle. Move the jar into the shade, and the bubbles stop. Move it back into the sun, and they start again. Only the green parts made bubbles. Roots and flowers didn't. And at night, the plant did the opposite: it made the air slightly worse, just like an animal.

So that was why Priestley's plants sometimes failed. They needed light.

Why light? Because light is energy. A plant catches sunlight and uses it to make its own food. We now call that photosynthesis, from the Greek for putting together with light.

But one question still remained. The bubbles were something the leaf was giving out. What was it taking in?

In seventeen eighty-two, in Geneva, a pastor called Jean Senebier showed that leaves only made their bubbles when the water held fixed air, the gas we now call carbon dioxide. Then, in eighteen-oh-four, another Genevan, Nicolas-Théodore de Saussure, did what van Helmont had done, but far more carefully. He weighed the plant, the water, and the air around it.

And at last, the sums added up.

We now know that a leaf takes in carbon dioxide through tiny pores. It draws up water through its roots. Then, with the energy of sunlight, it joins them together into sugar, and lets the spare oxygen go. That sugar becomes everything else: leaves, roots, bark and wood.

So here is what van Helmont's willow was really made of. About half of dry wood is carbon, and every bit of that carbon came out of the air. Most of the rest is oxygen and hydrogen, from carbon dioxide and water. The soil gave only a pinch of minerals, which is why his scales barely moved.

Van Helmont wasn't completely wrong. Water does go into a tree. But the solid wood is mostly built from the sky.

Why was this so groundbreaking? Because it means almost every bite of food you have ever eaten began as air and sunlight, caught by a leaf. Bread, rice, apples, even meat, because the animal ate plants first. And it explained why the air never runs out. The oxygen in every breath you take was given out by plants on land and tiny algae in the sea.

Today, only about four molecules in every ten thousand in the air are carbon dioxide. Out of that thin trace, a willow builds a hundred and sixty-nine pounds of tree.

A pot of soil, a sprig of mint, and a leaf in the sun. Together, they proved that a tree isn't pulled up out of the ground. It's pulled down out of the air.

Every one of those carbon atoms is billions of years old. So what is an atom actually made of? To find out, a young student fired tiny particles at gold beaten thinner than paper.
