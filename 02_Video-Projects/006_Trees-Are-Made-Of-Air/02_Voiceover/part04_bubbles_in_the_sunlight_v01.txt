Summer, seventeen seventy-nine. A Dutch doctor called Jan Ingenhousz took a house in the countryside just outside London, and in about three months, he ran more than five hundred experiments.

He plunged fresh leaves into jars of water and watched. Picture yourself at his window. In the sunshine you see tiny silver bubbles appear on the green leaf and rise to the top.

He collected the bubbles and tested the gas. A candle flame burned brighter in it than in ordinary air. It was the gas Priestley had found in seventeen seventy-four, which we now call oxygen.

Then came the clue that solved Priestley's puzzle. Move the jar into the shade, and the bubbles stop. Move it back into the sun, and they start again. Only the green parts made bubbles. Roots and flowers didn't. And at night, the plant did the opposite: it made the air slightly worse, just like an animal.

So that was why Priestley's plants sometimes failed. They needed light.

Why light? Because light is energy. A plant catches sunlight and uses it to make its own food. We now call that photosynthesis, from the Greek for putting together with light.

But one question still remained. The bubbles were something the leaf was giving out. What was it taking in?
