## PART 02: Burning Glass Tubes and the Razor Slit in Gold Foil (1:25–3:05)

[CHAPTER CARD: Burning Glass Tubes and the Razor Slit in Gold Foil · 1:25 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:28 — Philadelphia & Murray Hill, New Jersey, April 1945 – March 1946: Lee De Forest's 1906 glowing glass triode vacuum tube boiling electrons across a wire grid beside William Shockley's failed April 1945 semiconductor cylinder and John Bardeen's March 1946 chalkboard diagram of surface-state electrons · WHO: Lee De Forest (1873–1961) — American electrical inventor · WHEN: 1906 · WHEN: April 1945 · WHEN: March 1946 · FACT: Gap cut with razor blade]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:58 — Explainer diagram on period paper: an inked physics diagram shows John Bardeen's March 1946 surface-state electron trap on n-type germanium and Walter Brattain's 16 December 1947 gold-foil polystyrene wedge injecting positive minority "holes" across a 1/500th-inch (50 µm) razor-cut gap to amplify output power ~18× and voltage ~100× · WHEN: 16 December 1947 · DATA: inked physics diagram on period paper showing Bardeen's March 1946 surface-state electron trap on n-type germanium and Brattain's 16 December 1947 gold-foil plastic wedge injecting minority "holes" across a 1/500th-inch (50 µm) gap to amplify output current ~100× · SCALE: instead of boiling electrons in a hot glass bulb the size of a fist, Bardeen and Brattain steered them through a solid crystal across a gap thinner than tissue paper]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:32 — Murray Hill, New Jersey, 16–23 December 1947: Walter Brattain (readable face, lab coat) slices a razor blade across gold foil wrapped on a plastic triangle and presses it onto a germanium slab with a bent paperclip spring while John Bardeen watches the oscilloscope jump · WHEN: 23 December 1947 · LABEL: THE MIRACLE MONTH]
[TEACH: After John Bardeen discovered in March 1946 that surface states trapped electrons on semiconductors, he and Walter Brattain built the first working point-contact transistor on 16 December 1947 by pressing two razor-slit gold contacts 1/500th of an inch apart onto germanium.]

To understand why engineers in nineteen forty-five were desperate to replace the vacuum tube, imagine what you feel when you touch Lee De Forest's nineteen hundred and six glass triode bulb. Inside, a glowing red-hot filament boiled electrons across a vacuum through a wire grid, letting a weak phone signal control a strong current.

Those glass bulbs made coast-to-coast phone calls and ENIAC possible. But they burned scaldingly hot, gulped one hundred and fifty kilowatts in ENIAC, and popped so often that operators wheeled shopping carts of spare tubes down the aisles! Worse, what if you tried to build a computer ten times larger? It would melt down before finishing a single calculation!

At Bell Labs in New Jersey, solid-state team leader William Shockley calculated in April nineteen forty-five that a semiconductor crystal — an element like silicon or germanium halfway between a conductor and an insulator — should switch current using an electric field, with no hot filament and no vacuum.

However, when Walter Brattain built Shockley's device, nothing happened — the needle didn't move!

Why? In March nineteen forty-six, quiet quantum theorist John Bardeen looked deeper and solved the mystery: electrons were getting trapped on the outer skin of the crystal, forming an invisible shield of "surface states" that blocked Shockley's field from reaching inside!

Probing that crystal skin in late nineteen forty-seven, Bardeen calculated that two metal contacts would unlock the current only if they sat less than one two-hundredth of an inch apart on pure germanium.

But how could you place two wires closer than a sheet of paper without touching? On the sixteenth of December, nineteen forty-seven, Walter Brattain wrapped gold foil around the sharp tip of a plastic triangle, sliced through the gold apex with a razor blade until a gap of one five-hundredth of an inch remained, and then pressed it down onto germanium with a bent paperclip spring!

Suddenly, when a tiny current entered one gold edge, it injected positive charge carriers — "holes" — into the crystal's skin, opening a floodgate of current through the second gold edge nearly a hundred times stronger in voltage! Now, on the twenty-third of December, nineteen forty-seven, Brattain spoke into a microphone and amplified his voice through solid stone — the first **transistor**.

So why was their boss William Shockley furious — and what happened when he locked himself alone in a Chicago hotel room over New Year's Eve?

## PART 03: The Hotel Room Rivalry and the Man Who Won Two Physics Nobels (3:05–4:50)

[VISUAL MUST: 4:18 — Stockholm, 10 December 1956 & 1972: John Bardeen receives the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics alongside Brattain and Shockley, and returns in 1972 for his 1957 BCS superconductivity theory · WHEN: 10 December 1956 · WHEN: 1957 · WHEN: 1972 · LABEL: TWO PHYSICS NOBEL PRIZES]
[TEACH: Stung that his name was left off the point-contact patent, William Shockley invented the three-layer bipolar junction transistor in January 1948 (made in silicon by Gordon Teal in May 1954), while John Bardeen left Bell Labs in 1951 and became the only person ever to win two Nobel Prizes in Physics (1956 and 1972).]

Instead of celebrating his team's triumph, William Shockley felt a bitter shock when Bell Labs lawyers found that his nineteen forty-five field-effect idea had already been patented back in nineteen thirty by physicist Julius Edgar Lilienfeld — meaning the December nineteen forty-seven point-contact patent would carry only Bardeen and Brattain's names, not Shockley's!

Determined to outdo his own men, Shockley locked himself in a Chicago hotel room over New Year's Eve and filled notebook pages until the twenty-third of January, nineteen forty-eight.

Discarding the fragile plastic wedge and razor-cut gold foil, Shockley invented the **bipolar junction transistor**: a solid three-layer crystal sandwich. By doping the two outer layers to hold extra negative electrons — *n-type* — and the paper-thin middle layer to hold empty positive "holes" — *p-type* — Shockley built an internal electrical dam. Send a tiny trickle of current into the middle layer, and the barrier drops, unleashing a river of current a hundred times larger straight through the crystal!

Once Bell Labs chemist Gordon Teal stunned a May nineteen fifty-four conference by pulling working **silicon** transistors from his pocket — withstanding scorching heat that made germanium fail — Shockley's crystal sandwich became the workhorse of electronics.

However, Shockley pushed himself to the front of every photograph and barred Bardeen and Brattain from working on his junction transistor! Disgusted, John Bardeen left Bell Labs in nineteen fifty-one. After sharing the nineteen fifty-six Physics Nobel Prize on the tenth of December nineteen fifty-six, Bardeen solved the forty-six-year mystery of superconductivity in nineteen fifty-seven — returning to Stockholm in nineteen seventy-two as the only person in history ever to win two Nobel Prizes in Physics!

By nineteen fifty-seven, silicon transistors were replacing glass tubes everywhere. Case closed? Wrong. Why did a new crisis called the **"Tyranny of Numbers"** threaten to paralyse computing — and how did eight young engineers rebelling against Shockley in California solve it?

## PART 04: The "Traitorous Eight" and the Summer-Vacation Microchip — Midpoint Twist (4:50–6:40)

In nineteen fifty-six, William Shockley opened Shockley Semiconductor Laboratory among the apricot orchards of Mountain View, California — putting the silicon in **Silicon Valley**.

He recruited brilliant young physicists and chemists, including Robert Noyce, Gordon Moore, and Jean Hoerni. Yet Shockley's temper turned so paranoid that he forced his own staff to take lie-detector tests! On the eighteenth of September, nineteen fifty-seven, eight young researchers — the **"Traitorous Eight"** — walked out together and founded Fairchild Semiconductor.

Right then, engineers hit a terrifying wall called the **"Tyranny of Numbers"**. A single advanced computer or rocket needed tens of thousands of separate transistors, resistors, and capacitors, all soldered together by hand with miles of wire. If one solder joint out of a hundred thousand cracked, the rocket exploded or the computer died!

Two engineers solved the trap six months apart. In May nineteen fifty-eight, Kansas engineer Jack Kilby joined Texas Instruments in Dallas. Because he was a brand-new hire, he had earned no summer holiday — so in July nineteen fifty-eight, while the whole factory went on vacation, Kilby sat alone in the silent lab!

Suddenly he asked: instead of making transistors from silicon, resistors from carbon, and capacitors from foil, why not carve *every* part out of one single bar of crystal? On the twelfth of September, nineteen fifty-eight, Kilby flipped the switch on a sliver of germanium smaller than a toothpick, and a clean sine wave lit the screen — the first **integrated circuit**, or microchip!

Six months later, on the twenty-third of January, nineteen fifty-nine, at Fairchild in California, Robert Noyce used Jean Hoerni's protective layer of silicon dioxide to print flat microscopic metal wires directly onto a silicon wafer with light — printing thousands of circuits at once like pages on a press!

Why did Gordon Moore predict in nineteen sixty-five that components on a chip would double every year or two — and how did one hand-sliced switch grow to fifteen billion switches in your pocket?

## PART 05: Moore's Law and the Moving Ground Beneath Silicon Valley (6:40–8:15)

[TEACH: Silicon makes up 27.7% of Earth's crust (ordinary sand), and following Gordon Moore's 19 April 1965 law (Moore's Law), photolithography scaled Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce's 1958–1959 microchip to more than 15 billion transistors per chip today.]

Many people think silicon is a rare metal mined only in California, or that a microchip's billions of switches are wired together one by one under a microscope. Is that true?

Not at all. Silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust — nearly twenty-eight percent of the ground beneath your feet, the main ingredient of ordinary beach sand! And nobody wires those switches one by one: shining ultraviolet light through microscopic stencils etches billions of transistors onto a silicon wafer at once.

On the nineteenth of April, nineteen sixty-five — before co-founding Intel with Robert Noyce in July nineteen sixty-eight — Gordon Moore noticed that components on a chip were doubling roughly every one to two years: **Moore's Law**. From one hand-sliced switch in nineteen forty-seven and Jack Kilby's nineteen fifty-eight chip — winning Kilby the two thousand Physics Nobel Prize — modern chips pack over fifteen billion transistors onto a fingernail of silicon, each gate just fifteen atoms wide!

And if the silicon in your phone comes from Earth's crust — including the earthquake-faulted hills beneath Silicon Valley — how did we discover that the solid ground under your feet is drifting across the globe at the speed your fingernails grow? In Frankfurt on the sixth of January nineteen twelve, Alfred Wegener was laughed out of geology halls for claiming South America and Africa were once joined together — until in nineteen fifty-two, barred from research ships because she was a woman, Marie Tharp mapped the Atlantic floor and found the sixteen-thousand-kilometre scar tearing the planet apart.

A bent paperclip spring, a razor slit in gold foil, and a lonely summer holiday in Dallas. Together, they turned ordinary sand into the thinking heart of the modern world.
