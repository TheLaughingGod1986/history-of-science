### PART 02: 1879–1885 & 1950 — Hermann Ebbinghaus's 2,300 Nonsense Syllables, the Forgetting Curve & Karl Lashley's 30-Year Maze Hunt (1:25–3:05)
- **Cold open (how do you weigh a memory when poems and stories are already tangled in your head?):** **Berlin, Germany, 1879–1885 & Harvard/Yerkes Labs, 1920–1950**. Until 1879, philosophers insisted you could never measure human memory with mathematics because every word you know (`like "mother" or "fire"`) is already linked to a hundred old memories.
- **1879–1885 — Hermann Ebbinghaus invents 2,300 nonsense syllables (`WID, ZOF, KAX`):** To study a brand-new memory from birth in a "sterile" mind, 29-year-old German psychologist **Hermann Ebbinghaus** (1850–1909) sat alone in his Berlin study with a ticking metronome (`150 beats per minute`) and invented **`2,300` meaningless three-letter consonant-vowel-consonant syllables (`DAX, BOK, YAT`)**!
  - Testing himself relentlessly from **1879 to 1885** (*Über das Gedächtnis*), Ebbinghaus discovered the mathematical **Forgetting Curve**: without review, you forget **`~58%` within 20 minutes**, **`~66%` within 24 hours**, and **`~75%` after 6 days** — unless you revisit the memory just as it starts to fade (**spaced repetition**, which flattens the curve into lifelong retention)!
- **1920–1950 — Karl Lashley's 30-year hunt for the "engram" (`and why he joked that memory was impossible`):** Where inside the brain did those memories actually live? American psychologist **Karl Lashley** (1890–1958) spent **30 years (`1920–1950`)** training rats to run mazes and making tiny surgical cuts across different parts of their cerebral cortex to erase the memory trace (`the engram`).
  - No matter which piece of cortex Lashley removed, **the rats still remembered the maze**! In **1950**, Lashley concluded in despair that memories aren't stored in any specific brain structure at all, but are smeared evenly across the entire brain!
- *Markers:* `· WHO: Karl Lashley (1890–1958) — American neuropsychologist · WHEN: 1879–1885 · WHEN: 1885 · WHEN: 1920–1950 · WHEN: 1950 · FACT: 2,300 nonsense syllables · DATA: inked Ebbinghaus forgetting vs spaced-repetition curve on period paper from Hermann Ebbinghaus (1885, Über das Gedächtnis) showing exponential memory decay (58% lost at 20 min, 66% at 1 day, 75% at 6 days, 79% at 31 days) vs step-wise relearning savings that flatten decay into permanent storage · SCALE: within twenty minutes of cramming a new list, more than half of it has already evaporated from your brain — unless you reset the curve with spaced review`
- **Ends on:** Just **three years** after Karl Lashley declared in **1950** that no single brain structure controls memory, an operating room in **Hartford, Connecticut, on 25 August 1953** proved him dramatically wrong. What happened when a surgeon removed two seahorse-shaped ridges from the brain of a 27-year-old man known to the world only by his initials — **H.M.**?

### PART 03: 25 August 1953 & 1957 — Patient H.M. (`Henry Molaison`) & Brenda Milner's Mirror-Star Twist — Midpoint Twist (3:05–4:50)
- **Cold open ("Case closed? Wrong — Patient H.M. couldn't remember meeting Brenda Milner five minutes ago, yet his hands remembered a puzzle he swore he had never seen!"):** **Hartford Hospital, Connecticut, 25 August 1953 & Montreal/Hartford, 1955–1957**. Since a childhood bicycle accident, 27-year-old **Henry Molaison (`Patient H.M.`, 1926–2008)** suffered from crippling epileptic seizures that no medicine could stop.
- **25 August 1953 — Removing the hippocampus:** On **25 August 1953**, neurosurgeon **William Beecher Scoville** (1906–1984) tried an experimental operation, suctioning out an8-centimetre thumb-sized core of tissue on both sides of Henry's inner temporal lobes — including the seahorse-shaped **hippocampus**.
  - The seizures dropped — and Henry's IQ (`112`), kindness, sense of humour, and **childhood memories before 1950** remained intact.
  - Yet from that afternoon until his death **55 years later in 2008**, Henry could **never form a single new conscious long-term memory again**! He read the same magazine over and over without knowing it, couldn't remember what he had eaten for lunch 30 minutes ago, and if you stepped out of the room, he greeted you as a complete stranger when you walked back in!
- **1955–1957 — Brenda Milner's five-pointed mirror star (`Scoville & Milner 1957; Milner 1962`):**
  - Called in from Montreal by Scoville, young British-Canadian neuropsychologist **Brenda Milner** (born 1918) tested Henry at his kitchen table and made a discovery that rewrote neuroscience.
  - She placed a sheet of paper with a **double-outlined five-pointed star** on the table, hid Henry's hand and the paper behind a metal screen so he could see his hand **only reflected backwards in a mirror**, and asked him to trace a line between the two star borders without crossing the lines (`a fiendishly tricky visuomotor task where everyone makes ~30 mistakes at first!`).
  - Over **three days and 30 trials**, Henry's hand grew steadier and steadier until his errors dropped from **~30 mistakes to almost zero**!
  - On **Day 3**, Milner showed him the star apparatus. Henry looked at it blankly and said he had **never seen the contraption in his life** — and then picked up the pencil and traced it like a master, declaring, *"Well, this is strange — I thought that would be difficult, but it looks as if I've done it well!"*
  - Milner had proved that the human brain has **two completely separate memory systems**: conscious **declarative memory** (`facts and events, which must pass through the hippocampus`) and unconscious **procedural/motor memory** (`how to ride a bike or trace a mirror star, stored in the basal ganglia and cerebellum without the hippocampus!`)!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: 25 August 1953 · WHEN: 1955–1957 · WHEN: 1957 · WHEN: 1962 · WHEN: 2008 · FACT: Swore he'd never seen it · DATA: inked 3-day mirror-drawing learning curve on period paper from Brenda Milner (1962, Physiologie de l'Hippocampe, & Scoville & Milner 1957 J. Neurol. Neurosurg. Psychiatry 20: 11–21) showing Patient H.M.'s error score dropping across 30 trials over 3 days from ~30 line-crossings on Trial 1 to <3 on Day 3 while conscious recognition of the apparatus remained 0% · SCALE: for fifty-five years, Henry Molaison lived inside a thirty-second sliding window of the present — yet his hands learned new skills as smoothly as yours`
- **Ends on:** Patient H.M. showed *where* new conscious memories are indexed (`the hippocampus`) before being filed across the cortex during deep and REM sleep (`046`). But what actually *is* a single memory at the microscopic level — how does a fleeting electrical spark between two neurons (`033`) turn into a physical trace that lasts **eighty years**?

### PART 04: 1966–2000 — Bliss & Lømo's High-Frequency Spark & Eric Kandel's Sea Slug (`How a Thought Grows New Flesh`) (4:50–6:40)
- **Cold open (why would a Nobel Prize brain scientist study a 1-kilogram sea slug with only 20,000 brain cells?):** **Oslo, Norway, 1966–1973 & Columbia University, New York, 1970–2000**. Your human brain packs **86 billion neurons** (`033`), each wired to thousands of neighbours — far too tangled to watch a single memory form under a microscope in the 1960s.
- **1966–1973 — Tim Bliss & Terje Lømo discover Long-Term Potentiation (`LTP`):** In **1966** in Oslo (published **1973**), **Terje Lømo** and **Tim Bliss** stimulated a nerve pathway in the rabbit **hippocampus** with a brief burst of high-frequency electrical pulses — and discovered that the receiving synapses stayed **super-charged for hours or weeks (`Long-Term Potentiation`, confirming Donald Hebb's 1949 rule: *"neurons that fire together, wire together"*)**!
- **1970–2000 — Eric Kandel wires up the giant neurons of the sea slug (`Aplysia californica`):**
  - To see the exact molecules that build a memory, Austrian-born psychiatrist and neurobiologist **Eric Kandel** (born 1929 — who had fled Vienna as a 9-year-old boy in 1939 and wanted to understand how the human mind remembers!) made a radical move: he switched to the California sea slug, ***Aplysia californica***!
  - Why a sea slug? Because *Aplysia* has only **`20,000` neurons**, and its nerve cells are **giant (`up to 1 millimetre across` — visible to the naked eye!)**, wired in the exact same defensive gill-withdrawal circuit in every single slug!
  - Working with ** Craig Bailey** (`1983`) and colleagues up to **2000 (`Nobel Prize`)**, Kandel proved what separates **short-term memory** from **long-term memory**:
    - **Short-term memory** (lasting minutes, from a single gentle training shock) doesn't change the shape of the cell at all — a burst of **serotonin** and **cyclic AMP** simply makes the existing **`~1,300` synaptic terminals** release more chemical neurotransmitter (`glutamate`).
    - **Long-term memory** (from repeated spaced training, just like Ebbinghaus's spaced repetition!) sends a chemical messenger (`PKA` and **`CREB`**) all the way into the neuron's **cell nucleus**, switching on genes to manufacture new proteins and **physically grow brand-new synaptic terminals — doubling the neuron's connections from `~1,300` to `~2,600`**!
- *Markers:* `· WHO: Donald Hebb (1904–1985) — Canadian neuropsychologist · WHO: Craig H. Bailey (born 1951) — American neurobiologist · WHEN: 1949 · WHEN: 1966 · WHEN: 1973 · WHEN: 1983 · WHEN: 2000 · FACT: 20,000 giant slug neurons · DATA: inked short-term vs long-term synaptic architecture diagram on period paper from Eric Kandel & Craig Bailey (1983 Science 220: 91–93 & 2000 Nobel Lecture) showing 1 training pulse causing temporary cAMP/glutamate enhancement across 1,300 existing terminals (short-term memory, minutes) vs 4–5 spaced training pulses activating CREB gene transcription to grow ~2,600 physical terminals (long-term memory, weeks) · SCALE: learning something for the rest of your life literally changes the physical anatomy of your brain — sprouting twice as many microscopic bridges between the nerve cells that carry that memory`
- **Ends on:** Every time you learn a new skill or remember a story, your brain cells are physically growing new branches while you sleep (`046`). What does that mean for how you should study — and why can recalling an old memory actually make it *editable*?

### PART 05: Why Your Brain Rewires Itself When You Learn — & Why Chili Peppers Trick Your Mouth Into Feeling on Fire (6:40–8:15)
- **Cold open (why cramming the night before an exam fails at the molecular level):** Why did Hermann Ebbinghaus's **1885** spaced repetition work? Because as Eric Kandel proved a century later, cramming all at once only triggers **short-term chemical boosts** that fade in hours, whereas **spaced intervals** give the **`CREB`** genetic switch time to manufacture proteins and **grow permanent new synaptic branches**!
- **The synthesis of mind and matter:** From Ebbinghaus's 2,300 nonsense syllables in Berlin (`1885`), to Henry Molaison's mirror-star pencil in Connecticut (`1953–1957`), to Kandel's giant sea-slug neurons in New York (`2000`), science proved that memories are living, growing bridges of flesh inside your skull.
- **After-act hand-off (7:45–8:15):**
  - Your brain builds your entire inner world out of electrical signals arriving from your senses.
  - What happens when a plant evolves a chemical molecule that **hijacks** those sensory nerves and tricks your brain into feeling a physical temperature that isn't really there?
  - Why does biting into a fiery **chili pepper** make your mouth feel like it is literally **burning in boiling water**, while **peppermint** makes cold air feel **icy freezing** — even though a thermometer inside your mouth doesn't move by a tenth of a degree? How did pharmacist **Wilbur Scoville in May 1912** invent a human taste test diluted in **16 million drops of sugar water**, and how did **David Julius on 23 October 1997** and **Ardem Patapoutian in 2002** discover the exact molecular thermometer doors (`TRPV1` and `TRPM8`) that open at **`43 °C` heat** and **`26 °C` cold** (`051` candidate *Why Do Chilis Burn & Peppermints Feel Freezing Cold?*)?
