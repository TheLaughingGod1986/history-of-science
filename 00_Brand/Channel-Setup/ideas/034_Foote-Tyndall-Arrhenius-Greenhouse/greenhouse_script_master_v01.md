## PART 02: Fourier's Frozen Earth, Agassiz's Ice Age & Eunice Foote's 1856 Glass Cylinders (1:25–3:05)

In eighteen twenty-four, French mathematician Joseph Fourier asked a deceptively simple question: Earth orbits a hundred and fifty million kilometres from the Sun in freezing space — so what sets our planet's temperature?

When Fourier calculated the balance between incoming sunlight and outgoing heat, he hit a paradox: a bare rock at Earth's distance from the Sun should sit near minus eighteen degrees Celsius! Instead, Fourier realised, our atmosphere acts like a one-way blanket: it lets visible sunlight stream in, but slows invisible **"dark heat"** — infrared radiation — on its way out!

Then, on the twenty-fourth of July eighteen thirty-seven, Swiss geologist Louis Agassiz stunned scientists by proving from polished alpine boulders that Europe and North America had once been buried under a mile of prehistoric ice — an **Ice Age**! Suddenly, everyone wanted to know: what could turn Earth's atmospheric blanket up and down?

In the summer of eighteen fifty-six in Seneca Falls, New York, thirty-seven-year-old scientist and women's-rights campaigner Eunice Newton Foote decided to test which gases actually trap heat. Taking two identical thirty-inch glass cylinders with thermometers inside, she used an air pump to test dry air against damp air, and hydrogen, oxygen, and **carbonic acid gas** — carbon dioxide.

When she set the tubes in the sun, damp air heated twelve degrees Fahrenheit more than dry air — and the tube of carbon dioxide climbed to a scorching one hundred and twenty-five degrees Fahrenheit, nineteen degrees hotter than ordinary air! Foote wrote a prophetic conclusion: *"An atmosphere of that gas would give to our earth a high temperature."*

However, on the twenty-third of August eighteen fifty-six in Albany, women were not allowed to speak at the podium. Smithsonian Secretary Joseph Henry read her paper aloud, European journals skipped her climate insight, and although her paper appeared in November eighteen fifty-six, her priority was forgotten for a hundred and fifty-five years until two thousand and eleven!

Yet why did physicists point out that glass tubes alone couldn't test the dark heat rising from the Earth at night — and how did an Irish mountaineer in a London basement in May eighteen fifty-nine solve the puzzle with windows carved from salt?

## PART 03: John Tyndall's 1859 Rock-Salt Spectrophotometer & the 99% Shock (3:05–4:50)

Like Agassiz, Irish physicist John Tyndall — Michael Faraday's successor at the Royal Institution in London — spent his summers climbing Alpine glaciers to understand what caused the Ice Ages.

Unaware of Eunice Foote's paper in America, Tyndall noticed a crucial physical distinction. What if you feel the warmth of sunlight through a glass window, versus the invisible heat radiating from a warm stove in the dark? Sunlight is mostly visible light and short-wave infrared, which passes right through glass. But the warm Earth cools itself at night by radiating long-wave thermal infrared — and ordinary glass blocks long-wave infrared completely!

So in early eighteen fifty-nine, in the basement of the Royal Institution, Tyndall built the world's first **ratio spectrophotometer**: a four-foot brass tube sealed at both ends not with glass, but with polished plates of crystal **rock salt** — which lets invisible infrared rays pass straight through! At one end sat a copper cube of boiling water radiating dark heat toward a sensitive thermopile and galvanometer needle.

For weeks, Tyndall filled the tube with pure oxygen, pure nitrogen, and dry air. Nothing happened! Ninety-nine percent of Earth's atmosphere lets infrared heat escape as freely as empty space!

Then, on the eighteenth of May eighteen fifty-nine, Tyndall let trace amounts of carbon dioxide, water vapour, and coal gas into the tube — and suddenly the galvanometer needle swung across the dial! That night he wrote in his diary: *"The subject is completely in my hands!"*

Why do oxygen and nitrogen ignore infrared rays while carbon dioxide and water vapour trap them? Because two-atom molecules like $O_2$ and $N_2$ are balanced dumbbells that don't shift their electrical charge when they vibrate — whereas three-atom molecules like $H_2O$ and $CO_2$ bend and waggle unevenly, catching infrared waves like a tuned antenna! As Tyndall declared in his Bakerian Lecture on the seventh of February eighteen sixty-one, without that trace blanket of water vapour and carbon dioxide, Earth would be *"held fast in the iron grip of frost"*!

Foote and Tyndall had found the gases in the lab. Case closed? Not yet. How many degrees would the whole planet actually warm or cool if carbon dioxide doubled or halved — and why did a heartbroken chemist in Stockholm spend all of eighteen ninety-five doing ten thousand sums by hand?

On Christmas Eve eighteen ninety-four in Stockholm, Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius — who would win the nineteen hundred and three Nobel Prize in Chemistry — sat down at his desk as his marriage fell apart. To numb his heartbreak and solve what caused the prehistoric Ice Ages, he threw himself into a full year of gruelling arithmetic!

Today, climate physicists use supercomputers. In eighteen ninety-five, Arrhenius had only a pencil, infrared moonlight tables, and reams of paper. Working fourteen hours a day, he completed between ten thousand and a hundred thousand hand calculations across every latitude belt from the Arctic to the Equator!

Even more impressively, Arrhenius included the crucial **water-vapour feedback**: when extra carbon dioxide warms the air slightly, warmer air evaporates more ocean water — and because water vapour is a powerful greenhouse gas, it amplifies the warming!

Published in April eighteen ninety-six, Arrhenius's numbers solved the Ice Age mystery at a stroke: cutting carbon dioxide in half cools Europe by four to five degrees Celsius — enough to bury the continent in glaciers! Conversely, doubling carbon dioxide warms the globe by five to six degrees Celsius — astonishingly close to the ~three degrees calculated on early computers by Syukuro Manabe in May nineteen sixty-seven!

And here is the twist: in eighteen ninety-six, humanity burned five hundred million tonnes of coal a year. Arrhenius thought it would take three thousand years for industry to double carbon dioxide — and living in freezing Sweden, he wrote in nineteen hundred and eight that coal burning would prevent future Ice Ages and give our descendants warmer, gentler weather!

So why does a real glass greenhouse in your garden actually work differently from Earth's atmosphere — and how did scientists prove carbon dioxide was rising in a single human lifetime?

Many people think Earth's atmosphere works just like a glass garden greenhouse that traps hot air under a solid roof. Is that true?

Not quite! A glass greenhouse in your garden stays hot mostly because its glass roof physically stops warm air from rising and blowing away. Earth has no glass roof! Instead, carbon dioxide and water vapour high in the cold upper air absorb outgoing infrared rays and radiate part of that heat back downward — acting like an extra wool blanket on your bed! And when Charles David Keeling began measuring air atop Hawaii's Mauna Loa volcano in March nineteen fifty-eight, he proved carbon dioxide wasn't taking three thousand years to rise — it climbed from three hundred and fifteen parts per million in nineteen fifty-eight to over four hundred and twenty today!

And where does the carbon in that atmospheric carbon dioxide go when green leaves drink it in? Plants weave it into glucose sugar — the life-giving fuel inside every meal you eat, provided a microscopic hormone key from your pancreas unlocks your cells to let that sugar in! At two in the morning on the thirty-first of October nineteen twenty, a broke twenty-nine-year-old Canadian surgeon named Frederick Banting woke from sleep and scribbled twenty-five misspelled words — launching a frantic race with student Charles Best and biochemist James Collip that in January nineteen twenty-two woke dying diabetic children from comas across a Toronto hospital ward, before they sold the patent for just one dollar each!

Two glass tubes in the New York sun, a brass pipe with rock-salt windows in London, and ten thousand sums scribbled by lamplight in Stockholm. Together, they revealed how four molecules in ten thousand keep our world alive.
