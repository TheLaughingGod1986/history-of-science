Why can the Serengeti support millions of wildebeest and zebras, yet only a few thousand lions — and why are there **no super-predators on Earth that hunt lions or great white sharks** for a living?

Between **nineteen twenty-one and nineteen twenty-four**, young Oxford zoologist **Charles Elton** sailed to freezing **Bear Island** in the Arctic. Tracing every meal from marine plankton to seabirds, tundra plants, and Arctic foxes, Elton published *Animal Ecology* in **nineteen twenty-seven**, introducing **`food chains`**, **`food webs`**, and the **`Pyramid of Numbers`**: at the bottom sit billions of tiny plants, while predators at the top are large and scarce!

Why does the pyramid shrink so fast? At **Cedar Bog Lake, Minnesota** in **nineteen forty-one**, twenty-six-year-old **Raymond Lindeman** and his wife **Eleanor Hall Lindeman** measured the exact calories at every level of the lake. Aided by **George Evelyn Hutchinson**, Lindeman discovered the **`Ten-Percent Trophic Energy Law`**: at every step from plants to plant-eaters to meat-eaters, **roughly ninety percent of the food energy burns away as body heat and movement**, leaving **only about ten percent** to build the next rung (`published in Ecology in October nineteen forty-two, just three months after Lindeman tragically died at age twenty-seven`)!

Because top predators make up less than one percent of the biomass, ecologists assumed they were just passengers riding on top of the food web! What happened in **June nineteen sixty-three** when **Robert Paine** walked onto a Washington shore with a **steel crowbar** and tested what happens when you **throw the top predator into the sea**?

Case closed — green plants at the bottom of the food chain control everything in an ecosystem, and the rare predators at the top just eat whatever is left over! Wrong! In **June nineteen sixty-three** on a wave-lashed cliff in Washington State, a thirty-year-old zoologist picked up a **steel crowbar**, pried **every purple starfish** off an eight-metre ledge, and hurled them into the Pacific Ocean — and watched in shock as removing that one predator **destroyed the entire community underneath**!

Inspired by a **nineteen sixty** hypothesis that predators keep the world green from the top down, **Robert Paine** went to **Mukkaw Bay** in **June nineteen sixty-three**. An untouched rocky ledge teemed with **fifteen species** — barnacles, limpets, chitons, sponges, seaweeds, and one top predator: the purple ochre starfish (**`Pisaster`**). Twice a month at low tide, Paine pried off every starfish on his test strip and tossed them into deep water. Without starfish eating them, aggressive **California mussels** marched across the rock and smothered everything else — crashing diversity from **fifteen species down to eight, and toward a single-species monoculture** (`published in January nineteen sixty-six`)! In **January nineteen sixty-nine**, Paine named the starfish a **`keystone species`** (`like the single top stone that holds up a Roman arch`) — a **`trophic cascade`** confirmed by **James Estes** in **nineteen seventy-four** (`when losing sea otters let sea urchins devour kelp forests`) and by returning **wolves to Yellowstone in nineteen ninety-five**!

Yet if top predators sit on such a knife-edge at the tip of **Lindeman's ten-percent pyramid**, what happens when human factories spray a fat-soluble chemical into lakes and fields that **never washes out**?
