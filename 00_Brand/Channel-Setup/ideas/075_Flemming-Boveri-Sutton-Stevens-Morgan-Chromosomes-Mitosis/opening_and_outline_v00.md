### PART 02: 1879–1905 — Walther Flemming's Dyed Salamander Threads, Boveri & Sutton's Grasshoppers, and Nettie Stevens's `X` and `Y` Chromosomes (1:25–3:10)
- **Cold open (why for forty years after Robert Remak and Rudolf Virchow proved cells divide in the 1850s `015`, the cell nucleus looked like an empty drop of clear jelly — until a German anatomist poured synthetic purple textile dye onto salamander gills in 1879 and saw a microscopic ballet!):** **University of Kiel, Germany, 1879–1882; Naples & Columbia University, 1902–1903; & Bryn Mawr College, Pennsylvania, 1905**.
- **1879–1882 (*Zellsubstanz, Kern und Zelltheilung*) — Walther Flemming discovers `chromatin` and `mitosis`:**
  - Because living cell nuclei are completely transparent, nobody knew what happened inside a nucleus when a cell divided (`015`).
  - At the University of Kiel between **1878 and 1882**, German anatomist **Walther Flemming** (1843–1905) tested synthetic **aniline coal-tar dyes** (`048`) on the translucent tail fins and gills of fire salamanders (*Salamandra maculosa*).
  - Inside the nucleus, a substance soaked up the crimson dye like a sponge! Flemming named it **`chromatin`** (from the Greek for *colour*, renamed **`chromosomes`** — "coloured bodies" — by **Heinrich Waldeyer in 1888**) and named its division dance **`mitosis`** (from the Greek *mitos*, "thread"): watching each chromosome thicken, split **lengthwise** into identical twin threads (`sister chromatids`), line up at the cell's equator, and get pulled along spindle fibres into two new cells so every body cell receives an **exact copy of all 46 chromosomes** (`established as 46 in humans by Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan in 1956`)!
- **1883, 1902–1903 & 1905 — Meiosis, Boveri & Sutton's `Chromosome Theory of Inheritance`, and Nettie Stevens's `X` and `Y` Sex Chromosomes:**
  - Meanwhile, Belgian cytologist **Edouard van Beneden (1883)** and **Oscar Hertwig (1876)** discovered that sperm and egg cells divide by a second dance (**`meiosis`**): cutting the chromosome number **in half (`from 46 to 23`)** so that when a `23-chromosome` sperm meets a `23-chromosome` egg, the baby is restored to **46 (`23 pairs`)**!
  - In **1902–1903** — just after **Gregor Mendel's (`012`)** 1865 pea laws were rediscovered in **1900** — German zoologist **Theodor Boveri** (1862–1915, proving with sea-urchin embryos in Naples that every individual chromosome carries unique life-or-death instructions) and 25-year-old Kansas-born Columbia graduate student **Walter Stanborough Sutton** (1877–1916, studying giant paired chromosomes in "lubber" grasshoppers, *Brachystola magna*) unveiled the **`Boveri-Sutton Chromosome Theory of Inheritance`**: Mendel's invisible genes are physical passengers riding on maternal and paternal chromosome pairs!
  - And in **1905** at Bryn Mawr College, former schoolteacher **Nettie Maria Stevens** (1861–1912) studied mealworm beetles (*Tenebrio molitor*) and proved (`independently alongside Edmund Beecher Wilson`) that biological sex isn't caused by temperature or diet during pregnancy — females carry two large **`X` chromosomes (`XX`)**, while males carry one large **`X`** and one tiny **`Y` chromosome (`XY`)**!
- *Markers:* `· WHO: Edouard van Beneden (1846–1910) — Belgian embryologist and cytologist · WHEN: 1879 · WHEN: 1882 · WHEN: 1883 · WHEN: 1888 · WHEN: 1902 · WHEN: 1903 · WHEN: 1905 · FACT: 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs · DATA: inked Walther Flemming (1882 Zellsubstanz, Kern und Zelltheilung) salamander mitosis phases (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase keeping 2n = 46 constant) vs Walter Sutton (1903 Biol. Bull. 4: 231–251) grasshopper meiosis reduction division (2n → n = 23) & Nettie Stevens (1905 Carnegie Inst. Wash. Publ. 36) XX/XY sex chromosome karyotype chart on period paper · SCALE: mitosis is like photocopying a forty-six-volume encyclopaedia so two libraries get the exact same set — while meiosis shuffles your mother's twenty-three volumes with your father's twenty-three volumes to print a brand-new twenty-three-volume edition for every egg or sperm`

Why did Morgan start breeding **fruit flies in stolen glass milk bottles** to *disprove* the theory — and what happened in **May 1910** when **one white-eyed male fly** hatched in his bottle?

### PART 03: May 1910 — Sceptic Thomas Hunt Morgan & the One White-Eyed Fruit Fly That Proved Him Wrong — Midpoint Twist (3:10–4:55)
- **Cold open ("Case closed — Thomas Hunt Morgan is going to prove that chromosomes have nothing to do with Mendel's genes! Wrong! In May 1910, inside a sixteen-by-twenty-three-foot room at Columbia University stinking of fermented bananas, Morgan stared through a jeweller's loupe at a single white-eyed male fruit fly — and proved the very theory he had spent years attacking!"):** **Room 613 ("The Fly Room"), Schermerhorn Hall, Columbia University, New York, 1908 – 22 July 1910**.
- **Why Thomas Hunt Morgan doubted both Darwin (`013`) and Sutton (`1903`):**
  - **Thomas Hunt Morgan** (1866–1945) was an experimentalist who hated speculation: because nobody had ever seen a single specific trait tied to a single specific chromosome, Morgan argued in **1909** that chromosomes were just featureless blobs of chromatin.
  - Because mice and pigeons bred too slowly and cost too much, Morgan filled **Room 613 of Schermerhorn Hall** (`"The Fly Room"`) with ratusan glass milk bottles stopped with cotton wool and stocked with **mashed ripe bananas** to breed the common vinegar fruit fly, ***Drosophila melanogaster*** — which produces a new generation of hundreds of offspring every **12 days** and has just **4 pairs of chromosomes (`8 in total`)**!
- **May 1910 (*Science*, 22 July 1910) — The lone white-eyed male & `Sex-Linked Inheritance`:**
  - For two years, Morgan exposed thousands of fruit flies to X-rays (`003`), radium (`023`), acids, and heat, searching for mutations (`like Hugo de Vries's evening primroses`). Nothing worked — until in **May 1910**, peering through his hand lens at a bottle of normal brick-red-eyed flies, Morgan spotted **one single male fly with snow-white eyes**!
  - Morgan treated that tiny fly like gold (`rumour said he carried the bottle home to his wife Lilian's bedside when their baby was born!`) and bred the white-eyed male with his red-eyed sisters:
    1. In the **first generation (`F₁`)**, all **1,237 offspring** had normal **red eyes** (proving red eyes are dominant and white eyes are recessive, just like Mendel's peas `012`).
    2. When Morgan bred those `F₁` flies together, the **second generation (`F₂`)** produced **3,470 red-eyed flies** and **782 white-eyed flies** — an approximate **`3:1` Mendelian ratio** (`2,459 red-eyed females, 1,011 red-eyed males, and 782 white-eyed flies`).
    3. **The midpoint twist:** Every single one of the **782 white-eyed flies was MALE (`0 white-eyed females`)**!
  - Published in *Science* on **22 July 1910**, Morgan realized there was only one possible explanation: the gene for eye colour was physically riding on **Nettie Stevens's `X` sex chromosome**! Because females have **two `X` chromosomes (`XX`)**, a healthy red-eye gene on one `X` hides a recessive white-eye gene on the other — whereas males have **only one `X` and a tiny `Y` (`XY`)**, so a single recessive gene on a male's lone `X` chromosome shows up immediately (`explaining why 1 in 12 men are red-green colour-blind in 049 and why haemophilia ran through Queen Victoria's royal sons`)!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: 1909 · WHEN: May 1910 · WHEN: 22 July 1910 · FACT: All 782 white-eyed flies were male · DATA: inked Thomas Hunt Morgan (22 July 1910 Science 32: 120–122) X-linked white-eye Drosophila cross Punnett diagram & F2 count chart (2,459 red females, 1,011 red males, 782 white males, 0 white females) on period paper · SCALE: because a male only has one X chromosome with no backup copy on his tiny Y chromosome, a recessive mutation on the X chromosome is like flying a single-engine plane — if that one engine sputters, you see it straight away, whereas a female flies with twin X engines`
- **Ends on:** Within a year, Morgan's "Fly Room" found **dozens** of new fruit-fly mutations (yellow bodies, miniature wings, vermilion eyes) — yet a fruit fly only has **four pairs of chromosomes**! How could dozens of different genes sit on the **same chromosome**, why did genes on the same chromosome sometimes get separated during `meiosis`, and how did a **19-year-old undergraduate in 1911** use that puzzle to draw the **first map of a chromosome**?

### PART 04: 1909–January 1913 — Frans Janssens's `Chiasmata` & 19-Year-Old Alfred Sturtevant's All-Nighter Genetic Map (4:55–6:40)
- **Cold open (one night in the autumn of 1911, a 19-year-old Columbia undergraduate named Alfred Sturtevant pushed his college homework aside, stayed up until sunrise with sheets of numbers from the Fly Room, and handed Thomas Hunt Morgan the world's first map of genes along a chromosome!):** **Louvain, Belgium, 1909 & Columbia University, New York, 1911 – January 1913**.
- **1909 (*La Cellule*) — Frans Alfons Janssens spots chromosomes hugging and swapping pieces (`Chiasmata` / `Crossing Over`):**
  - If two genes sit on the **same `X` chromosome** (`say, white eyes and miniature wings`), they ought to travel together into every egg and sperm cell 100% of the time (`genetic linkage`, first noticed in sweet peas by **William Bateson, Edith Rebecca Saunders, and Reginald Punnett in 1905**).
  - Yet in Morgan's Fly Room, linked genes on the `X` chromosome stayed together *most* of the time, but **broke apart a fixed percentage of the time** (`for example, 1.0% of the time between yellow body and white eyes, but 33.7% of the time between yellow body and miniature wings`)! Why?
  - Morgan remembered a **1909** paper by Belgian priest and cytologist **Frans Alfons Janssens** (1863–1924): under the microscope during **`meiosis`**, paired maternal and paternal chromosomes twist around each other in X-shaped cross-points (**`chiasmata`**) and physically **break and swap matching segments (`crossing over`)**!
- **1911 – January 1913 (*Journal of Experimental Zoology*) — 19-year-old Alfred Sturtevant invents genetic mapping (`centimorgans`):**
  - Morgan suggested to his 19-year-old undergraduate student **Alfred Henry Sturtevant** (1891–1970 — who had grown up drawing pedigrees of his father's horses on an Alabama farm, and was himself red-green colour-blind `049`!) that genes that cross over more often might sit farther apart.
  - That night in **1911**, Sturtevant saw the mathematical secret: if genes sit in a **straight 1D line** along a chromosome like stations along a railway track, then a random crossover cut during meiosis is much more likely to land between **two distant genes (`A` and `C`)** than between **two neighbours (`A` and `B`)** — and the crossover frequency from **`A to B` (`1.0%`)** plus **`B to C` (`29.7%`)** must add up to **`A to C` (`30.7%`)**!
  - Blowing off his undergraduate homework, Sturtevant worked through the night and published in **January 1913** (*J. Exp. Zool.*) the **first linear genetic map** of six X-chromosome genes (`defining 1% recombination as 1 map unit, later named the "centimorgan" in honour of Thomas Hunt Morgan, who won the 1933 Nobel Prize and shared the prize money with Sturtevant and Calvin Bridges's children!`) — proved visually under the microscope by **Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock in corn in August 1931** (*PNAS*)!

- **Ends on:** Every single time a fertilised egg divides by **Walther Flemming's `mitosis`** (`using the genes mapped by Morgan and Sturtevant`), one of the very first organs those genes build in a tiny embryo — just **22 days after conception** — is a microscopic tube that suddenly starts **beating all by itself**: your **heart**!
