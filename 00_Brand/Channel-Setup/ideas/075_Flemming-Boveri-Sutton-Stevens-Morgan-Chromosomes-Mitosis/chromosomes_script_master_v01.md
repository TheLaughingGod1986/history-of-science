For forty years after biologists proved that new cells come from dividing cells, the cell nucleus looked like an empty drop of clear jelly. Then, between **eighteen seventy-eight and eighteen eighty-two** at the University of Kiel, German anatomist **Walther Flemming** tested synthetic **purple coal-tar fabric dyes** on the translucent gills of fire salamanders. Inside the nucleus, tiny threads soaked up the crimson dye like a sponge! Flemming named the dyed substance **`chromatin`** (`renamed "chromosomes" — coloured bodies — by Heinrich Waldeyer in eighteen eighty-eight`) and named its division dance **`mitosis`**: watching each chromosome split **lengthwise** into identical twin threads and march into two daughter cells so every body cell gets all **forty-six chromosomes**!

Meanwhile, Belgian biologist **Edouard van Beneden in eighteen eighty-three** discovered that sperm and egg cells divide by a second dance — **`meiosis`** — cutting the chromosome number **in half (`from forty-six to twenty-three`)** so that when sperm and egg unite, the baby is restored to **twenty-three pairs**! In **nineteen oh-two and nineteen oh-three**, right after Gregor Mendel's pea laws were rediscovered, German zoologist **Theodor Boveri** (`studying sea urchins in Naples`) and twenty-five-year-old Kansas graduate student **Walter Sutton** (`studying giant chromosomes in lubber grasshoppers at Columbia`) unveiled the **`Boveri-Sutton Chromosome Theory`**: Mendel's invisible genes are physical passengers riding on chromosome pairs! And in **nineteen oh-five** at Bryn Mawr College, former schoolteacher **Nettie Stevens** studied mealworm beetles and proved (`independently alongside Edmund Beecher Wilson`) that females carry two large **`X` chromosomes (`XX`)**, while males carry one large **`X`** and one tiny **`Y` chromosome (`XY`)**!

Yet Columbia University's **Thomas Hunt Morgan** flatly refused to believe that Mendel's genes sat on chromosomes! Why did Morgan start breeding **fruit flies in stolen glass milk bottles** to *disprove* the theory — and what happened in **May nineteen ten** when **one white-eyed male fly** hatched in his bottle?

Case closed — **Thomas Hunt Morgan** is going to prove that chromosomes have nothing to do with Mendel's genes! Wrong! In **May nineteen ten**, inside a cramped sixteen-by-twenty-three-foot room at Columbia University stinking of fermented bananas, Morgan stared through a jeweller's lens at a **single white-eyed male fruit fly** — and proved the very chromosome theory he had spent years attacking!

Morgan was a hard-nosed experimentalist who hated guesswork: in **nineteen oh-nine**, he argued that nobody had ever tied a single trait to a single chromosome. Because mice bred too slowly, Morgan filled Room 613 of Schermerhorn Hall — **`"The Fly Room"`** — with ratusan glass milk bottles stocked with mashed bananas to breed the common fruit fly, ***Drosophila melanogaster***, which produces hundreds of offspring every twelve days and has just **four pairs of chromosomes**!

For two years, Morgan dosed fruit flies with X-rays, radium, and acids looking for mutations, until in **May nineteen ten** he spotted **one lone male fly with snow-white eyes** among thousands of brick-red flies! When Morgan bred that white-eyed male with red-eyed sisters, all **`one thousand two hundred and thirty-seven` first-generation (`F₁`) offspring** had red eyes. Then he bred those `F₁` flies together: the second generation produced **`three thousand four hundred and seventy` red-eyed flies** and **`seven hundred and eighty-two` white-eyed flies** — **and every single one of the seven hundred and eighty-two white-eyed flies was MALE (`zero white-eyed females`)**! Published in *Science* on the **twenty-second of July nineteen ten**, Morgan realized the white-eye gene was physically riding on **Nettie Stevens's `X` sex chromosome** (`explaining why one in twelve men are red-green colour-blind and why haemophilia struck royal sons`)!

Within a year, Morgan's Fly Room found dozens of new mutations — yet a fruit fly only has **four pairs of chromosomes**! Why did genes sitting on the *same* chromosome sometimes get separated during `meiosis` — and how did a **nineteen-year-old undergraduate in nineteen eleven** use that clue to draw the **first map of a chromosome**?

One night in the autumn of **nineteen eleven**, a nineteen-year-old Columbia undergraduate named **Alfred Sturtevant** pushed his college homework aside, stayed up until sunrise with sheets of numbers from the Fly Room, and handed **Thomas Hunt Morgan** the **world's first map of genes along a chromosome**!

If two genes sit on the same `X` chromosome (`say, white eyes and miniature wings`), they ought to travel together into every egg and sperm cell. Yet in Morgan's Fly Room, genes on the same chromosome stayed together *most* of the time, but **broke apart a fixed percentage of the time** (`one percent between yellow body and white eyes, but thirty-three point seven percent between yellow body and miniature wings`)! Why? Morgan remembered a **nineteen oh-nine** paper by Belgian priest-biologist **Frans Alfons Janssens**: during **`meiosis`**, paired maternal and paternal chromosomes twist around each other in X-shaped cross-points (**`chiasmata`**) and physically **break and swap matching pieces (`crossing over`)**!

When Morgan mentioned this to nineteen-year-old undergraduate **Alfred Sturtevant**, the teenager saw the mathematical secret: if genes sit in a **straight line** along a chromosome like stations along a railway track, a random crossover cut is much more likely to fall between **two distant genes** than between **two close neighbours** — and the crossover percentage from **gene A to B (`one point zero`)** plus **B to C (`twenty-nine point seven`)** adds right up to **A to C (`thirty point seven`)**! Published in **January nineteen thirteen** (`and proved visually under the microscope in corn chromosomes by Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock in August nineteen thirty-one, before Morgan won the nineteen thirty-three Nobel Prize`), Sturtevant's **`centimorgan`** map became the foundation of modern genomics!

Wait — just twenty-two days after a sperm and egg fuse their `twenty-three plus twenty-three chromosomes` together, one of the very first organs those genes build in a tiny embryo is a muscle tube that starts **beating all by itself**: your **heart**! Why does your heart beat **one hundred thousand times a day** even outside your body — and how did a **London tap-water mistake**, a **six-hundred-pound salt-bucket machine**, and **one wrong resistor** invent the **`ECG`** and the **implantable pacemaker**?

Every time a doctor maps a disease gene across our **forty-six human chromosomes** (`counted accurately by Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan in nineteen fifty-six`), they use the exact **`centimorgan`** ruler that nineteen-year-old **Alfred Sturtevant** drew by lamplight in **Thomas Hunt Morgan's** banana-scented Fly Room!

And just three weeks after an egg and sperm fuse their chromosomes together, those genes build a tiny muscle pump in your chest that fires **one hundred thousand times a day** (`nearly three billion times in a lifetime`) — and keeps beating **all by itself even if a surgeon lifts it out of your body and places it in warm salt water**! How did London physician **Sidney Ringer in eighteen eighty-two** discover that a frog heart kept beating for hours only because his lab assistant had secretly used **London pipe water containing calcium ions** instead of pure distilled water (`inventing Ringer's Solution`)? A drop of purple dye in Kiel, a milk bottle of mashed bananas in New York, and a teenager's all-nighter map. Together, they revealed how life copies and shuffles the forty-six threads that make us who we are.
