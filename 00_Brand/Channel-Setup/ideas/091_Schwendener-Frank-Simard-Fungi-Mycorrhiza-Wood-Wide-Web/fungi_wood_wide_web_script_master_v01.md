In Victorian Europe, every textbook taught a simple rule of nature: every living body is a single species, and whenever a fungus touches a green plant, isn't that fungus a parasite trying to rot and kill it? What if two completely different kingdoms of life were secretly fused into one body?

Then on **nine September eighteen sixty-seven**, Swiss botanist **Simon Schwendener** sliced open a humble grey crust of **rock lichen** under his microscope and dropped a bombshell (`expanded in eighteen sixty-nine`): a lichen is **not one plant at all — it is two completely different kingdoms of life living inside one body**! The tough outer shell is a **fungus** that cannot make its own food from sunlight, and nestled inside its threads are thousands of microscopic **green algae** that photosynthesize sugar! Traditional botanists were outraged (`mocking Schwendener's proof as "the sensational romance of lichenology"`), while Schwendener himself called the fungus a "master" holding algal "slaves" captive.

In **eighteen seventy-nine** in Strasbourg, German mycologist **Heinrich Anton de Bary** proved that in a lichen, **both partners help each other survive** where neither could live alone: the fungus shields the algae from drying out and dissolves rock minerals, while the algae feed solar sugar to the fungus! To name this intimate partnership between different species, de Bary coined a brand-new word in **eighteen seventy-nine**: **`symbiosis`** (`"living together"`).

Biologists grudgingly accepted that tiny rock crusts were a symbiosis — yet they still insisted that **giant forest trees** stood completely alone on their own wooden roots! How did the **King of Prussia's craving for gourmet underground truffles** in **eighteen eighty-five** prove them wrong?

Case closed — mushrooms and fungi in the forest floor are just rot and decay feeding on dead wood! Wrong! In **eighteen eighty-one**, the Prussian Ministry of Agriculture hired Berlin botanist **Albert Bernhard Frank** to figure out how to farm expensive gourmet **truffles** under oak and beech trees — and when Frank dug up the roots of healthy forest trees in **eighteen eighty-five**, he discovered that the trees' feeder roots **weren't even touching the soil**!

Digging up thousands of living root tips of **oaks, beeches, pines, and firs** across Germany, what did Frank find on **seventeen April eighteen eighty-five**? Every healthy feeder root tip was wrapped inside a tight, living glove of fungal threads that wove between the outer root cells without harming a single cell! Why weren't the fungi killing the tree? Frank coined the word **`mycorrhiza`** — Greek for **"fungus-root"** — and called the fungus the **"wet nurse of the tree"**: because fungal threads are **up to a hundred times thinner than the finest root hair**, they worm into microscopic rock pores to mine **water, nitrogen, and phosphorus** for the tree in exchange for photosynthetic **sugar**! When Frank grew pine seedlings in **heat-sterilized soil** in **eighteen ninety-four**, why did the fungus-free pines turn yellow and starve? Because without their fungal partner, the trees couldn't eat!

Then in **nineteen sixty to nineteen ninety-seven**, scientists asked an electrifying question: if the **same underground fungal web** wraps around the roots of **dozens of neighbouring trees at once**, can different tree species **pump food back and forth to one another underground**?

How do you prove that two completely different species of trees in a wild forest are trading carbon sugar underground — without digging up the soil and snapping the microscopic fungal threads between them?

After **Erik Björkman** in **nineteen sixty** and **David Read** in **nineteen eighty-four** traced radioactive carbon moving through mycorrhizal fungi in lab boxes, Canadian forest ecologist **Suzanne Simard** took the test into the wild forests of **British Columbia**. She planted triplets of saplings: a **paper birch** and a **Douglas-fir** (`which share the same ectomycorrhizal fungi`), plus a **western redcedar** control (`which uses a different fungus and cannot plug into their network`). Sealing plastic bags over the crowns, she fed **radioactive carbon-14 gas** to the birch and **heavy carbon-13 gas** to the fir!

On **seven August nineteen ninety-seven** in *Nature* (`whose cover dubbed the network "The 'Wood-Wide Web'"`), Simard's **Geiger counter** and mass spectrometer proved that **birch and fir trees pump carbon back and forth underground through shared fungi** — while the unconnected redcedar beside them got almost nothing! Even more remarkable, when Simard **shaded the Douglas-fir with black cloth**, the sunlit birch sent **twice as much net carbon** to its shaded neighbour (`and in twenty thirteen, researchers showed that insect-bitten plants even send underground defence signals through mycorrhizal threads`)!
