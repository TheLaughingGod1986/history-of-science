### PART 02: The Scalpel Spark and the Iron Balcony (1:25–3:05)
- **Cold open (quirky anecdote before the electrophysiology):** **Bologna, Italy, 6 November 1780**. Physician and anatomist **Luigi Galvani** (1737–1798) works in his home laboratory alongside his wife **Lucia Galeazzi Galvani** (1743–1788), daughter of Bologna's leading physics professor. While dissected frogs lie on the table alongside an electrostatic friction machine, Lucia notices something bizarre: whenever an assistant touches the inner crural nerve of a frog's leg with the steel tip of a **scalpel** at the exact instant a spark jumps from the electrical machine across the room, every muscle in the dead leg contracts as if seized by a violent cramp!
- **Testing lightning:** Remembering Benjamin Franklin's kite (`008`), Galvani wonders if natural atmospheric electricity from storm clouds will do the same thing. He strings a long insulated wire from his roof down to frog legs during thunderstorms — and whenever lightning flashes, the legs jump!
- **The accidental breakthrough (20 September 1786):** On a calm, clear autumn evening with **no thunderstorm and no electrical machine**, Galvani hangs prepared frog legs by **brass hooks** poked through their spinal cords onto the **iron railing** of his garden balcony (`palazzo` on Via Zamboni).
- When he presses the **brass hook** against the **iron railing** to steady it, the dead frog leg kicks repeatedly!
- Moving indoors, Galvani places the frog leg on an iron plate and touches the brass hook to the iron: it kicks again. When he uses glass, resin or stone instead of metal, nothing happens.
- In **1791**, after eleven years of experiments, Galvani publishes his landmark *De Viribus Electricitatis in Motu Musculari Commentarius* (*Commentary on the Effects of Electricity on Muscular Motion*), announcing that every living creature possesses its own innate **"animal electricity"**, stored like a biological Leyden jar inside muscle fibres and discharged through the nerves!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: Bologna, 6 November 1780 · FACT: Lucia spotted the first spark · WHEN: 20 September 1786 · WHEN: 1791 · DATA: inked comparison diagram on period paper of Galvani's 1791 "biological Leyden jar" hypothesis (nerve and muscle storing internal animal electricity discharged by a metal arc) vs a glass Leyden jar · SCALE: a single frog sciatic nerve impulse travels at 30 metres per second — crossing the leg in less than two-thousandths of a second`
- **Ends on:** Why did Italy's greatest physicist read Galvani's paper, applaud it at first — and then decide that the frog's leg had nothing to do with making the electricity at all?

### PART 03: The Coin on the Tongue and the Pile of Metal (3:05–4:55)
- **Cold open:** **Pavia, Italy, 1792**. At the University of Pavia, physics professor **Alessandro Volta** (1745–1827) repeats Galvani's frog-leg experiments and is initially amazed.
- **The clue Galvani missed:** Soon, however, Volta notices a crucial detail in Galvani's own notes: the frog's leg kicks strongly *only* when the circuit is made of **two different metals** — such as **brass** (an alloy of copper and zinc) touching **iron**, or **silver** touching **tin**! When you touch the nerve and muscle with a single clean piece of one pure metal, almost nothing happens.
- **Rivalry beat & the tongue experiment (1792–1794):** Volta declares war on "animal electricity." What if the dead frog leg isn't generating electricity at all, but is simply acting as a super-sensitive wet electroscope — twitching in response to a tiny electric current created when **two dissimilar metals touch each other through a wet conductor**?
- To prove you don't need a frog to feel "metallic electricity," Volta places a **tin (or zinc) strip** on the tip of his **tongue** and a **silver spoon (or coin)** further back on his tongue, then touches their outer ends together: instantly, he tastes a sharp **sour, acidic tang** that lasts as long as the metals touch! Placed against his moist **eyeball**, the two touching metals even trigger a flash of light!
- **The Voltaic Pile (March 1800):** Determined to build a device that produces a strong, continuous current without a single piece of animal tissue, Volta stacks alternating discs of **silver (or copper)** and **zinc**, separating each pair with a disc of **cardboard or cloth soaked in salt water (brine)**.
- On **20 March 1800**, Volta mails a famous letter in French to **Sir Joseph Banks**, President of the Royal Society in London (read **26 June 1800**), unveiling the **Voltaic pile** — the world's **first electric battery** and the first steady electric current in human history!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: Pavia, 1792 · FACT: Tested coins on his tongue · WHEN: 20 March 1800 · WHO: Joseph Banks (1743–1820) — Royal Society president · WHEN: 26 June 1800 · DATA: inked cutaway diagram of Volta's March 1800 Voltaic pile showing 30 alternating pairs of silver (Ag) and zinc (Zn) discs separated by brine-soaked pasteboard discs, generating ~0.76 to 1.1 volts per cell pair (~25–30 volts total continuous current) · SCALE: 1 volt per silver-zinc pair — stack 30 pairs and you feel a sharp electric shock in wet hands that never runs out after a single spark`
- **Ends on:** Volta had invented the battery and crushed Galvani's theory in the eyes of Europe. Case closed? Not so fast — what had Galvani quietly done in 1794 with *zero metal at all*?

### PART 04: The Metal-Free Twitch and the Corpse That Opened Its Eyes — Midpoint Twist (4:55–6:40)
- **Cold open ("Case closed? Wrong."):** **Bologna, 1794–1797**. Everyone in Europe hailed Volta's metallic electricity and laughed off Galvani's "animal electricity." Yet before his death in **1798**, Luigi Galvani — aided by his fiery nephew **Giovanni Aldini** (1762–1834) — ran the most elegant counter-experiment of the century!
- **The 1794 metal-free proof:** In an anonymous **1794** tract (*Dell'uso e dell'attività dell'arco conduttore*), Galvani dissected two frog legs with a **glass knife** so no metal ever touched the tissue. Then he draped the bare **sciatic nerve** of one leg directly across the injured muscle of the second leg — with **no brass, no iron, no silver, and no metal of any kind**!
- The instant the nerve touched the muscle, **the leg kicked violently**! And in **1797**, Galvani went even further, making a single frog leg twitch by touching its own nerve to its own muscle!
- **The gothic spectacle (London, 17 January 1803) & Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* (1818):** To defend his late uncle's honour, **Giovanni Aldini** toured Europe with a large Voltaic pile. On **17 January 1803** at the Royal College of Surgeons in London, Aldini applied conducting rods from a battery of **120 zinc-and-copper plates** to the freshly executed body of murderer **George Forster**: the dead man's jaw quivered, his left eye opened, and his right hand rose and clenched — a spectacle that directly inspired nineteen-year-old **Mary Shelley** (1797–1851) when she wrote *Frankenstein* in **1816–1818**!
- **How both rivals were proven right (1840–1848):** Using ultra-sensitive multi-thousand-turn coil galvanometers, Italian physicist **Carlo Matteucci** (1811–1868) in **1840–1842** and German physiologist **Emil du Bois-Reymond** (1818–1896) in **1848** directly measured the electrical current of a living nerve impulse — the **action potential**!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: Bologna, 1794 · WHO: Giovanni Aldini (1762–1834) — Bolognese physics professor · FACT: Kicked with zero metal (1794) · WHEN: London, 17 January 1803 · WHO: Mary Shelley (1797–1851) — English gothic novelist · WHEN: 1818 · WHEN: 1848 · WHO: Emil du Bois-Reymond (1818–1896) — German electrophysiologist · DATA: inked nerve-membrane diagram showing sodium (Na+) and potassium (K+) ions rushing across a neuron membrane to flip the voltage from -70 mV to +40 mV (the action potential measured by du Bois-Reymond in 1848) · SCALE: -70 millivolts to +40 millivolts in one millisecond — firing up to 1,000 times a second along your nerves`
- **Ends on:** If Volta's battery runs on metal and your nerves run on salt ions, why do people still think electric eels or human nerves have tiny copper wires inside them — and what happened in 1820 when someone held a compass needle next to Volta's battery wire?

### PART 05: Why Your Brain Runs on Salt Water & the Twitching Compass (6:40–8:15)
- **Cold open (popular misconception debunked):** People often say nerves conduct electricity "like wires in a wall." Is that true?
- **No:** if your nerves worked like passive copper wires inside salty flesh, the signal would leak out and die within a couple of millimetres! Instead, every nerve in your body uses **Galvani's animal electricity** *and* **Volta's salt-water chemistry**: microscopic protein gates along the nerve membrane pump charged **sodium ($\text{Na}^+$)** and **potassium ($\text{K}^+$)** ions in and out like a line of falling dominoes, regenerating a fresh **110-millivolt pulse** every millimetre at up to **120 metres per second (270 mph)** — the exact electrical signal doctors read on an **ECG (electrocardiogram)** of your heart or an **EEG** of your brain!
- Meanwhile, **Volta's 1800 battery** immediately transformed science: within weeks of reading Volta's letter in **May 1800**, William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle used a Voltaic pile to split water into hydrogen and oxygen, Sir Humphry Davy isolated sodium and potassium, and every modern lithium-ion battery in your phone and electric car is still a direct descendant of Volta's stacked discs (and measured in **volts** in his honour!).
- **After-act hand-off (7:45–8:15):**
  - For twenty years after **1800**, everyone believed electricity and magnetism were two completely separate forces. Then, on **21 April 1820**, Danish physicist **Hans Christian Ørsted** connected a wire to a **Voltaic battery** during a university lecture in Copenhagen — and watched a nearby magnetic **compass needle** jerk sideways (`020` candidate *Electromagnetism: Ørsted's Twitching Compass & Michael Faraday's Cup of Mercury*).
