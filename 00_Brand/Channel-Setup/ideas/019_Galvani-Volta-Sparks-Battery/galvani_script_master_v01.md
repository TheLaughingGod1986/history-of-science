## PART 02: The Scalpel Spark and the Iron Balcony (1:25–3:05)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Scalpel Spark and the Iron Balcony · 1:25 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:28 — Bologna, Italy, 6 November 1780: in their home laboratory, Luigi Galvani (readable face, early forties, powdered wig, dark coat) and Lucia Galeazzi Galvani (readable face, late thirties, silk dress, notebook in hand) watch an assistant touch the inner crural nerve of a dissected frog leg with a steel scalpel just as a spark jumps from a distant electrostatic machine across the table · WHEN: Bologna, 6 November 1780 · FACT: Lucia spotted the first spark]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:58 — Bologna, 20 September 1786: on a calm autumn evening with a clear sky, Galvani hangs prepared frog legs by curved brass hooks through their spinal cords onto the black iron railing of his garden balcony; each time a brass hook presses against the iron bar, the leg kicks sharply · WHEN: 20 September 1786 · LABEL: BRASS ON IRON]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:28 — Explainer diagram on period paper: an inked comparison of Galvani's 1791 "biological Leyden jar" model (muscle storing internal animal electricity discharged through the nerve at 30 metres per second) beside a glass Leyden jar · WHEN: 1791 · DATA: inked comparison diagram of Galvani's 1791 biological Leyden jar model (nerve and muscle storing internal animal electricity) and frog sciatic nerve conduction at 30 metres per second · SCALE: 30 metres per second in a frog nerve — crossing the entire leg in less than two-thousandths of a second · LABEL: ANIMAL ELECTRICITY?]
[TEACH: On 6 November 1780 and 20 September 1786 in Bologna, Luigi and Lucia Galvani discovered that a frog's leg contracts when touched by a scalpel near a spark or when a brass hook touches an iron railing, leading to Galvani's 1791 theory of animal electricity.]

On the sixth of November, seventeen eighty, physician Luigi Galvani was working in his home laboratory in Bologna alongside his wife and collaborator, Lucia Galeazzi.

On one side of the wooden table sat a spinning glass machine that threw static electric sparks. On the other side lay a dissected frog prepared for an anatomy lesson.

Suddenly, Lucia noticed something bizarre. Whenever an assistant touched the main nerve of the frog's leg with the steel tip of a scalpel at the exact instant a spark jumped from the machine across the room, the dead leg kicked violently — as if the frog were still alive.

Remembering how storm clouds carry lightning, Galvani wondered if nature's own electricity would do the same thing. During thunderstorms, he strung a long wire from his roof down to frog legs — and every time lightning flashed across Bologna, the legs jumped.

Then came the real shock. On the twentieth of September, seventeen eighty-six, on a calm, clear evening with no storm in the sky and no spark machine in the room, Galvani hung several frog legs by brass hooks onto the iron railing of his garden balcony.

When he pressed a brass hook against the iron railing to steady it, the dead leg kicked. He pressed it again: another kick. Moving indoors, he laid the frog on an iron plate and touched the brass hook to the iron — and the muscle contracted on command, sending an impulse down the frog's nerve at thirty metres per second, crossing the leg in less than two-thousandths of a second.

In seventeen ninety-one, after eleven years of experiments, Galvani published his famous commentary. Every living creature, he declared, generates its own innate "animal electricity," stored inside muscle fibres like a living Leyden jar and discharged through the nerves.

So why did Italy's greatest physicist read Galvani's paper, applaud it at first — and then decide that the frog's leg had nothing to do with making the electricity at all?

## PART 03: The Coin on the Tongue and the Pile of Metal (3:05–4:55)

[VISUAL MUST: 4:18 — Explainer diagram on period paper: an inked cutaway diagram of Volta's March 1800 Voltaic pile showing 30 alternating pairs of silver (Ag) and zinc (Zn) discs separated by brine-soaked cardboard discs, generating ~1 volt per cell pair (~30 volts total continuous current) · DATA: inked cutaway diagram of Volta's March 1800 Voltaic pile of 30 alternating silver (Ag) and zinc (Zn) pairs separated by brine-soaked cardboard, producing ~0.76 to 1.1 volts per pair (~30 volts continuous current) · SCALE: 1 volt per silver-zinc pair — stack 30 pairs and you feel a sharp electric shock in wet hands that flows continuously instead of vanishing in one spark]
[TEACH: In 1792 in Pavia, Alessandro Volta realized that electricity came from touching two different metals through a wet conductor, and on 20 March 1800 he announced the Voltaic pile — a stack of silver, zinc and brine-soaked cardboard that became the world's first electric battery.]

At the University of Pavia in seventeen ninety-two, physics professor Alessandro Volta repeated Galvani's frog experiments and was initially dazzled.

However, as Volta tested metal after metal, he spotted a crucial clue hiding in Galvani's own notes. The frog's leg kicked strongly *only* when the arch was made of *two different metals* — such as a brass hook touching an iron railing, or silver touching tin. When Volta touched the nerve and muscle with a single clean wire of one pure metal, almost nothing happened.

Volta turned Galvani's theory upside down. What if the dead frog wasn't generating electricity at all? What if the electricity was created by the contact of two different metals through a wet substance — and the frog's leg was just a sensitive biological meter twitching in the current?

To prove you didn't need a frog to feel this "metallic electricity," Volta ran a daring test on himself. He laid a strip of tin on the tip of his tongue and a silver spoon further back. Then he touched their outer ends together.

Instantly, instead of a brief static spark, Volta tasted a sharp, sour, acidic tang that lasted for as long as the two metals touched.

Determined to banish the frog altogether, Volta spent the late seventeen-nineties hunting for a way to multiply that tiny metallic current. Finally, in early eighteen hundred, he stacked alternating discs of silver and zinc, separating each metallic pair with a disc of cardboard soaked in salt water — mimicking the stacked electric organs of a torpedo fish.

On the twentieth of March, eighteen hundred — in a letter to Royal Society President Sir Joseph Banks, read in London on the twenty-sixth of June — Volta unveiled the Voltaic pile. Now, each silver-and-zinc pair produced about one volt; stacked thirty pairs high, it delivered a continuous electric current that never died after a single spark.

Volta had invented the electric battery and seemingly crushed Galvani's theory across Europe. Case closed? Not so fast — what had Galvani quietly done in seventeen ninety-four using *zero metal at all*?

Volta's battery was a sensation, and most physicists dismissed Galvani's "animal electricity" as a delusion. Case closed? Wrong.

However, before his death, Luigi Galvani — assisted by his fiery nephew Giovanni Aldini — performed the most elegant counter-experiment of the eighteenth century.

In seventeen ninety-four, Galvani dissected two frog legs using a sharp *glass* knife so not a single speck of metal ever touched the flesh. Then he lifted the bare sciatic nerve of one leg and dropped it directly onto the cut muscle of the second leg — with no brass, no iron, no silver, and no metal of any kind.

Suddenly, the instant the bare nerve touched the muscle, the dead leg kicked violently. In seventeen ninety-seven, he even made a single frog leg twitch by touching its own nerve to its own muscle.

To defend his late uncle's honour, Giovanni Aldini toured Europe using Volta's own battery on dead bodies. On the seventeenth of January, eighteen-oh-three, at the Royal College of Surgeons in London, Aldini connected a battery of one hundred and twenty zinc-and-copper plates to the freshly executed body of a convicted murderer. When the rods touched the face and limb, the dead man's jaw quivered, his left eye opened, and his right hand rose and clenched — a gothic spectacle that helped inspire young Mary Shelley when she published *Frankenstein* in eighteen eighteen.

Who won the great war between Galvani and Volta? Both of them.

Finally, in eighteen forty-eight, German physiologist Emil du Bois-Reymond built a coil galvanometer sensitive enough to measure a living nerve impulse directly. Every nerve cell in your body maintains a resting charge of minus seventy millivolts, and flips to plus forty millivolts in a single millisecond — firing up to a thousand times a second.

If Volta's battery runs on metal and your nerves run on electricity, why do people still imagine human nerves as tiny copper wires — and what happened in eighteen twenty when a professor held a compass needle next to Volta's battery wire?

## PART 05: Why Your Brain Runs on Salt Water and the Twitching Compass (6:40–8:15)

[VISUAL MUST: 7:45 — Copenhagen, 21 April 1820: in a candlelit lecture hall, a Danish physics professor connects a platinum wire to a Voltaic battery pile directly above a glass-enclosed magnetic compass needle, and watches the needle jerk sideways · WHEN: 21 April 1820 · LABEL: THE COMPASS TWITCHES]
[TEACH: Human nerves do not conduct electricity like passive copper wires; they regenerate a 110-millivolt ion wave like falling dominoes at up to 120 metres per second, while Volta's 1800 battery unlocked modern electrochemistry and electromagnetism.]

Many people picture the nerves in your arms and brain as tiny insulated wires carrying electricity like a lamp cord in a wall. What is wrong with that picture?

If your nerves worked like passive copper wires inside wet, salty flesh, an electrical signal from your brain would leak out and die within a couple of millimetres.

Instead, every nerve in your body combines Galvani's animal electricity with Volta's salt-water chemistry. Microscopic protein gates along the nerve membrane pump charged sodium and potassium ions in and out like a line of falling dominoes, regenerating a fresh one-hundred-and-ten-millivolt wave every millimetre at up to one hundred and twenty metres per second — racing from your toe to your brain in less than a fiftieth of a second. That is the exact electrical wave doctors read on an electrocardiogram of your heart today.

Meanwhile, within weeks of Volta's letter in May eighteen hundred, scientists used his battery to split water into hydrogen and oxygen, discover new elements, and launch the electrical age — where every battery is still measured in volts.

Yet for twenty years after eighteen hundred, everyone still believed electricity and magnetism were two completely separate forces. Then, on the twenty-first of April, eighteen twenty, a Danish professor connected a wire to Volta's battery during a lecture — and watched a nearby compass needle jerk sideways.

A brass hook on an iron balcony and a stack of wet coins on a table. Together, they revealed the spark inside both our machines and ourselves.
