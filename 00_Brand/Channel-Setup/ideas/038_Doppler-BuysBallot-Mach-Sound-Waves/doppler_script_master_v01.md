When you clap your hands, what actually travels across the room to reach someone else's ear?

It isn't the air itself flying across the room — if a breeze blew from your mouth to your friend's ear every time you spoke, a quiet conversation would feel like a windstorm! Instead, your clap squeezes a thin shell of air molecules tightly together, then leaves a stretched gap behind it, passing an invisible **pressure wave** from molecule to molecule like a ripple racing across a pond.

In the sixteen thirties, French philosophers Marin Mersenne and Pierre Gassendi — followed by a grand nighttime experiment by the French Academy of Sciences in seventeen thirty-eight — fired cannons on hilltops twenty-nine kilometres apart across Paris. By timing the delay between seeing the instantaneous **muzzle flash** and hearing the distant **boom** eighty-eight seconds later, they proved that sound marches through freezing air at **three hundred and thirty-one metres per second** — or **three hundred and forty-three metres per second**, about seven hundred and sixty-seven miles an hour, on a mild spring day!

A century later in Prague, thirty-eight-year-old Austrian mathematics professor Christian Doppler was struggling with failing health and exhaustion from grading hundreds of student exams. Yet he asked a question nobody had thought to ask: what if a wave source is moving? If a ship bobbing on the sea hits more waves per minute when it steers *into* the waves than when it sails *away* from them, why wouldn't the exact same thing happen to **sound and light waves**?

On the twenty-fifth of May eighteen forty-two, in a room with barely six listeners at the Royal Bohemian Society of Sciences, Doppler unveiled that secret. When a wave source moves toward you, each new wave crest starts closer to the last one, bunching the waves together and raising the frequency; when it moves away, the crests stretch apart and the frequency drops.

Doppler's equation was pure gold — but he used it to make a famous astronomical mistake! He claimed binary stars look blue or red to the naked eye because their orbital motion shifts their starlight across the rainbow. However, stars orbit far too slowly compared to the speed of light to change their overall naked-eye colour!

Because Doppler's star-colour claim was wrong, critics attacked his entire wave equation! Why did a skeptical twenty-seven-year-old Dutch scientist in June eighteen forty-five set out to demolish Doppler's theory once and for all — using a brand-new steam railway and a carload of brass horn players?

In Utrecht in the Netherlands, twenty-seven-year-old meteorologist Christophorus Buys Ballot read Christian Doppler's paper and was convinced the Austrian professor was wrong.

To test Doppler's equation on Earth, Buys Ballot needed a machine that could move faster than any galloping horse while holding a loud, steady musical note. And in eighteen forty-three, the Dutch government had just opened the **Utrecht-to-Amsterdam steam railway**!

The railway minister lent Buys Ballot a fast steam locomotive and an open wooden flatbed wagon. After a freezing test run in February eighteen forty-five was ruined by snow and howling wind, Buys Ballot returned in warm summer weather on the third and fifth of June eighteen forty-five.

He hired professional brass horn players and stationed three teams of trained musicians with **perfect pitch** beside the tracks between Utrecht and Maarssen. First, a horn player rode on the open train car blasting a single continuous **G note** while the listeners on the ground wrote down what pitch they heard; then the musicians rode on the train while the horn player stood beside the tracks!

Buys Ballot had expected the note to stay flat. Case closed? Wrong!

As the steam locomotive thundered toward the observers at forty miles an hour, every single musician heard the horn sound **nearly a semitone higher** than the note being blown — and then, the instant the train roared past them, the pitch suddenly plunged **nearly a full musical tone lower**! Even when the horn player tried to trick the listeners by changing notes, the shift matched Christian Doppler's equation to a fraction of a semitone!

Better still, French physicist Hippolyte Fizeau in eighteen forty-eight and English astronomer William Huggins in eighteen sixty-eight solved the mystery of how to apply Doppler's law to starlight: instead of looking at a star's overall colour, Huggins looked at the hair-thin **dark barcode lines** in the spectrum of the star Sirius and found them shifted slightly toward the red — giving astronomers **redshift**, the cosmic speedometer that later revealed the expanding universe!

Buys Ballot's train travelled at barely one-twentieth the speed of sound. What if an object accelerates until it travels **just as fast as — or faster than — its own sound waves**?

Long before Chuck Yeager flew the Bell X-1 rocket plane beyond the sound barrier on the fourteenth of October nineteen forty-seven, humans had been breaking the sound barrier by hand for thousands of years.

How? Every single time someone cracks a leather **bullwhip**! When you flick your wrist, a loop of energy races down the tapering leather strap, moving faster and faster as the leather gets thinner and lighter — until the tiny tip whips past **three hundred and forty-three metres per second** and snaps through the sound barrier with a miniature **sonic boom**!

In eighteen eighty-seven, forty-nine-year-old Austrian physicist Ernst Mach — teaching in Prague, the very city where Christian Doppler had lectured — wondered what happens to the invisible air when a **rifle bullet** flies faster than sound.

Because the bullet outruns its own pressure waves, the sound waves can no longer escape ahead of it! Instead, every wave crest piles up on top of the next along a razor-sharp **cone of compressed air** trailing behind the bullet's nose.

To photograph that unknown shockwave, Mach teamed up in spring eighteen eighty-seven with physicist Peter Salcher at the Naval Academy in Fiume. In a pitch-dark room, they fired a rifle bullet at five hundred metres per second right between two tiny wires connected to a high-voltage **Leyden jar**.

At the exact instant the bullet brushed the wires, it triggered a blinding electric spark lasting **less than one-millionth of a second**! Because compressed air bends light slightly more than thin air, that microsecond flash cast a crystal-clear shadow of the invisible shockwave onto a glass photographic plate!

Now, frozen on glass for the first time in history, stood a breathtaking **V-shaped shockwave cone** — and the sharper the angle of that V, the faster the bullet was flying compared to sound, a ratio aerospace engineer Jakob Ackeret in nineteen twenty-nine named the **Mach number**!

So when a supersonic jet flies overhead at **Mach two**, why do so many people think it makes a sonic boom only once at the moment it "breaks" the sound barrier?

Many people think a supersonic jet creates a **sonic boom** only once — at the exact second it accelerates past Mach one to "break" the sound barrier. Is that true?

No — a sonic boom never stops for as long as the plane flies faster than sound! Just as a speedboat drags a V-shaped water wake behind it across an entire lake, a supersonic aircraft drags Ernst Mach's cone of compressed air continuously across the ground beneath it — so every person along its flight path hears the boom at the moment that sweeping cone of pressure passes over their ears! And since nineteen fifty-seven, doctors have used **Doppler ultrasound** to bounce gentle sound waves off the moving red blood cells inside a patient's arteries and an unborn baby's heart — turning Christian Doppler's eighteen forty-two equation into a life-saving window inside the human body!

However, every sound wave — from Buys Ballot's train horns to Ernst Mach's shockwave — is a wave of **air pressure** squeezing and stretching the atmosphere around us. What if you pump **all the air out** of a vessel to create a **vacuum** where no sound can travel at all? In Florence in sixteen forty-three and sixteen forty-four, Galileo's young successor Evangelista Torricelli inverted a four-foot glass tube of heavy liquid mercury to create the world's first barometer and vacuum, before Blaise Pascal sent a barometer up a French volcano on the nineteenth of September sixteen forty-eight and German mayor Otto von Guericke on the eighth of May sixteen fifty-four hitched **sixteen straining warhorses** to two unbolted copper bowls that the crushing weight of the air refused to let open!

Six listeners in a Prague lecture hall, a flatbed train car full of trumpet players, and a millionth-of-a-second electric spark. Together, they taught us how to read every moving wave in the universe.
