### PART 02: Cannon Flashes, Ripples in a Pond & Doppler's 1842 Prague Paper (1:25–3:05)
- **Cold open (clocking an invisible wave):** **Paris, 1630s–1738 & Prague, 25 May 1842**. When you clap your hands, what actually travels across the room to someone else's ear?
- **Not the air itself!** If air flew across the room every time you spoke, a conversation would feel like a hurricane! Instead, your clap squeezes a thin shell of air molecules together (`compression`) followed by a stretched gap (`rarefaction`), passing a **pressure wave** from molecule to molecule like a ripple racing across a pond.
- **Timing cannon flash vs boom:** In the **1630s** in France, **Marin Mersenne** and **Pierre Gassendi** (refined by the **French Academy of Sciences in 1738**) fired cannons on distant hilltops at night and timed the delay between seeing the **muzzle flash** (light arrives almost instantly) and hearing the **boom** — proving sound travels through air at **`331 metres per second` at freezing (`0 °C`), or `343 metres per second` (`767 mph`) at room temperature (`20 °C`)**!
- **25 May 1842 — Christian Doppler's insight (and his colour mistake):** In Prague, 38-year-old Austrian professor **Christian Doppler** (1803–1853) asked a question nobody in two centuries had thought to ask: if a ship bobbing on the ocean hits more waves per minute when it sails *into* the waves than when it sails *away* from them, shouldn't the same thing happen to **sound and light waves**?
  - On **25 May 1842**, speaking to just **five or six members** of the Royal Bohemian Society of Sciences, Doppler presented his paper *"On the Coloured Light of the Double Stars"*.
  - His wave equation was pure genius — yet Doppler applied it to an astronomical mistake! He claimed binary stars look blue or red to the naked eye because their orbital motion shifts visible white starlight across the rainbow. In reality, stars move far too slowly (`~30 km/s` vs light's `300,000 km/s`) to change their naked-eye colour (and emit infrared and ultraviolet light that shifts in to replace it)!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: 1738 · WHEN: 25 May 1842 · FACT: Spoke to just 6 people · DATA: inked cannon-timing & wave diagram on period paper showing the 1738 French Academy cannon experiment (muzzle flash vs acoustic boom across ~29 km = 331 m/s at 0 °C / 343 m/s at 20 °C) alongside Christian Doppler's 25 May 1842 wave-bunching formula f_obs = f_src × v / (v ∓ v_s) · SCALE: light crosses twenty-nine kilometres in less than a ten-thousandth of a second — whereas the cannon's sound wave takes a full eighty-five seconds to march across the same valley`
- **Ends on:** Because Doppler's star-colour claim was wrong, critics attacked his entire wave equation! How did a skeptical 27-year-old Dutch scientist in **June 1845** set out to demolish Doppler's theory once and for all — using a **brand-new steam railway and a carload of brass horn players**?

### PART 03: Buys Ballot's June 1845 Steam Train of Horn Players — Midpoint Twist (3:05–4:50)
- **Cold open ("Case closed? Wrong."):** **Utrecht–Maarssen Railway, Netherlands, 3–5 June 1845**. In Utrecht, 27-year-old Dutch scientist **Christophorus Buys Ballot** (1817–1890) read Doppler's paper and was convinced the Austrian professor was wrong.
- To test Doppler's equation on Earth, Buys Ballot needed something that could move at high speed while making a loud, steady musical note — and in **1843**, the Dutch government had just opened the **Utrecht-to-Amsterdam steam railway**!
- **The three-day musical train experiment (February & 3–5 June 1845):**
  - After a freezing February run ruined by snow and wind, Buys Ballot returned in warm weather on **3 and 5 June 1845**.
  - He borrowed a fast steam locomotive and an open flatbed wagon, hired **professional horn and trumpet players**, and stationed groups of trained musicians with **perfect pitch** at three posts along the tracks between Utrecht and Maarssen — AND placed musicians on the train while a stationary horn player blew the same note on the ground!
  - As the locomotive thundered past at **`~40 mph` (`~18 m/s`)**, the horn players held a single continuous **`G` note**.
- **The shock:** Every single listener on the ground — and every listener riding on the train — heard the approaching horn sound **nearly half a semitone sharper**, and the instant the train roared past, the note plunged **nearly a full musical tone lower**!
  - Even better, when **Hippolyte Fizeau (`1848`)** and **William Huggins (`1868`)** looked not at a star's overall colour, but at the hair-thin **dark spectral lines (`Fraunhofer lines`)** in starlight, they found those lines shifted toward the red or blue (**redshift and blueshift**) — proving Doppler's equation worked for light too (`and later enabling Edwin Hubble to discover the expanding universe`)!
- *Markers:* `· WHO: William Huggins (1824–1910) — English astronomer · WHEN: February 1845 · WHEN: 3 June 1845 · WHEN: 5 June 1845 · WHEN: 1848 · WHEN: 1868 · FACT: Hired a train to debunk him · DATA: inked musical-pitch & wavelength chart on period paper from Buys Ballot's June 1845 Utrecht–Maarssen train experiments showing a sustained G4 horn note (~392 Hz): approaching at ~18 m/s (~40 mph) shifts pitch up to ~414 Hz (~G#4, +1 semitone), then drops on passing to ~372 Hz (~F#4, −1 semitone) · SCALE: as the locomotive passed the station platform at forty miles an hour, the horn's note dropped by nearly a full piano step in a fraction of a second`
- **Ends on:** Buys Ballot's train travelled at one-twentieth the speed of sound. What happens if an object accelerates until it travels **just as fast as — or faster than — its own sound waves (`343 m/s`)**?

### PART 04: Cracking the Whip & Ernst Mach's 1887 Supersonic Bullet Photo (4:50–6:40)
- **Cold open:** **Prague & Fiume (Rijeka), spring 1887**. Long before anyone built a supersonic jet plane (`Chuck Yeager's Bell X-1 on 14 October 1947`), humans had been breaking the sound barrier by hand for thousands of years! How?
- **Every time someone cracks a leather bullwhip!** As a tapered whip loops forward, a wave of kinetic energy races down a thinner and thinner tip until the leather tip snaps past **`343 metres per second` (`Mach 1`)** — creating a miniature **sonic boom**!
- **1887 — Ernst Mach & Peter Salcher photograph the invisible shockwave:** In **1887**, Austrian physicist **Ernst Mach** (1838–1916, teaching in Prague — the very city where Doppler had lectured!) teamed up with physicist **Peter Salcher** (1848–1928) at the Naval Academy in Fiume to photograph something no human eye had ever seen: the invisible air waves around a **supersonic rifle bullet** travelling at **`440 to 530 metres per second`**!
  - How do you photograph invisible compressed air around a bullet moving faster than sound?
  - Using **schlieren shadow photography** invented by August Toepler, Mach and Salcher fired a rifle bullet between two tiny wires in a pitch-dark room: the passing bullet triggered a **Leyden-jar electric spark lasting less than one-millionth of a second**, casting a razor-sharp shadow of the compressed air onto a photographic plate!
  - On the plate stood a breathtaking **V-shaped cone of compressed air (`the Mach cone`)** trailing from the bullet's nose! And the angle of that cone (`sin θ = v_sound / v_object`) revealed the bullet's exact ratio to the speed of sound — the number aerospace engineer **Jakob Ackeret** in **1929** named the **Mach number (`Mach 1, Mach 2`)** in Ernst Mach's honour!
- *Markers:* `· WHO: Peter Salcher (1848–1928) — Austrian physicist · WHEN: 1887 · WHEN: 1929 · WHEN: 14 October 1947 · FACT: A whip crack is a sonic boom · DATA: inked three-stage wave-front & Mach-cone diagram on period paper from Mach & Salcher's 1887 spark photographs comparing subsonic motion (v < 343 m/s, nested circles), transonic sound barrier (v = 343 m/s = Mach 1.0, wave fronts pile up into a single wall of pressure), and supersonic flight (v = 485 m/s = Mach 1.4, V-shaped Mach cone with sin θ = 1 / M) · SCALE: a Leyden-jar spark lasting less than one-millionth of a second froze a rifle bullet moving at five hundred metres per second into a crystal-clear shadow on glass`
- **Ends on:** So when a supersonic jet flies overhead at **Mach 2**, does it make a sonic boom only at the single moment it "breaks" the sound barrier — or for its entire flight?

### PART 05: The Sonic Boom Myth, Medical Ultrasound & the 16 Horses That Couldn't Pull Two Bowls Apart (6:40–8:15)
- **Cold open (popular misconception debunked):** Many people think a supersonic jet creates a **sonic boom** only once — at the exact moment it accelerates past `Mach 1`. Is that true?
- **No — a sonic boom is a continuous wake!** Just as a speedboat drags a V-shaped water wake behind it for as long as it moves fast across a lake, a plane flying faster than `Mach 1` drags its conical **Mach shockwave** continuously across the ground beneath it — so every person along its flight path hears the double *boom-boom* as the nose and tail shockwaves sweep over their ears!
- And today, Christian Doppler's 1842 equation saves lives every day: in **Doppler ultrasound**, doctors bounce high-frequency sound waves off the moving red blood cells (`005` Harvey) inside an unborn baby's heart to watch blood flow on screen without a single cut!
- **After-act hand-off (7:45–8:15):**
  - Every sound wave — from Buys Ballot's train horns to Ernst Mach's shockwave — is a wave of **air pressure** squeezing and stretching the atmosphere around us.
  - What happens if you pump **all the air out** of a container to create a **vacuum** (where no sound can travel at all!) — and how heavy is the ocean of air pressing down on your shoulders right now?
  - How did Galileo's last assistant **Evangelista Torricelli** invert a four-foot glass tube of liquid **mercury** in Florence in **1643–1644** to create the world's first barometer and vacuum, why did **Blaise Pascal** send his brother-in-law hiking up a French volcano on **19 September 1648**, and why couldn't **sixteen straining horses** in Regensburg on **8 May 1654** pull **Otto von Guericke's** two unbolted copper bowls apart (`039` candidate *Atmospheric Pressure, the Barometer & the Vacuum*)?
