## PART 02: Sheep's Blood, the 150-Year Ban, and the Clumping Mystery (1:25–3:05)

[VISUAL MUST: 1:28 — Paris, 15 June 1667 & December 1667: Jean-Baptiste Denys (readable face, seventeenth-century wig and gown) transfuses 9 ounces of lamb's blood into a feverish 15-year-old boy, then watches in horror in December 1667 as patient Antoine Mauroy suffers acute haemolysis after calf-blood transfusions, leading to the 1668–1670 bans · WHEN: 15 June 1667 · WHEN: December 1667 · WHEN: 1670 · FACT: Animal blood banned in 1670]

[VISUAL MUST: 2:32 — Greifswald, Germany, 1875: physiologist Leonard Landois peers through a brass microscope at red blood cells clumping and bursting when mixed across species · WHO: Leonard Landois (1837–1902) — German physiologist · WHEN: 1875 · LABEL: CLUMPING AND BURSTING]
[TEACH: After Jean-Baptiste Denys's fatal December 1667 calf-blood transfusion led to a 150-year ban, James Blundell revived human-only transfusions in 1818–1829, yet nearly half of patients still suffered dangerous reactions because doctors assumed blood clumping was caused by disease.]

As soon as William Harvey proved in sixteen twenty-eight that blood circulates in a closed loop, doctors in London and Paris asked an obvious question: if a patient bleeds nearly to death, what if you top up their veins with blood from a gentle lamb or calf?

On the fifteenth of June, sixteen sixty-seven, French royal physician Jean-Baptiste Denys transfused nine ounces of sheep's blood into a fifteen-year-old boy. Because the dose was small, the boy survived.

However, in December sixteen sixty-seven, when Denys transfused calf's blood into a man named Antoine Mauroy, the patient felt a burning heat along his veins, passed black urine from shattered red blood cells, and died. Within three years, by sixteen seventy, French courts, the Royal Society in London, and the Pope banned blood transfusions outright.

Over a century later, appalled by watching new mothers bleed to death after childbirth, London obstetrician James Blundell proved that blood must never cross species: only human blood can save a human. Performing the first human-to-human transfusion on the twenty-second of December, eighteen eighteen, and publishing successful survivals by eighteen twenty-nine, Blundell saved several mothers' lives.

Yet transfusing human blood remained like flipping a coin with a patient's life: nearly half of all patients still suffered violent shock, clumped blood, and death!

In eighteen seventy-five, German physiologist Leonard Landois showed that mixing animal and human blood makes red cells clump together — *agglutinate* — and burst. And when bacteriologists in the eighteen nineties saw one human's blood clump another human's blood, everyone assumed the clumping was caused by germs or disease.

How did a thirty-one-year-old researcher in Vienna prove every doctor in Europe wrong — without touching a single sick patient?

## PART 03: Six Scientists' Blood in a 6 × 6 Grid (3:05–4:50)

[CHAPTER CARD: Six Scientists' Blood in a 6 × 6 Grid · 3:05 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:08 — University of Vienna, February 1900 – 14 November 1901: thirty-one-year-old Karl Landsteiner (readable face, thick moustache, white lab coat) draws small vials of blood from himself and five healthy lab colleagues (Dr St., Dr Plecnik, Dr Sturl, Dr Erdheim, and Zaritsch) and spins them in a centrifuge · WHEN: February 1900 · WHEN: 14 November 1901 · FACT: First printed in a footnote]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:42 — Explainer diagram on period paper: an inked molecular diagram shows red blood cell surface antigens (Type A with A markers, Type B with B markers, Type AB with both, Type O with neither) and plasma antibodies (anti-B, anti-A, none, both), alongside Alfred von Decastello and Adriano Sturli's 1902 addition of Group AB · WHO: Alfred von Decastello (1872–1960) — Austrian physician · WHO: Adriano Sturli (1873–1964) — Austrian physician · WHEN: 1902 · DATA: inked molecular diagram on period paper showing red blood cell surface antigens (A pegs, B spheres, AB both, O smooth) and Y-shaped plasma antibodies (anti-B, anti-A, none, both) demonstrating why Type O red cells pass unattacked as universal donor and Type AB accepts all red cells · SCALE: each red blood cell carries around 2 million microscopic A or B sugar markers on its skin — if the wrong antibody locks on, millions of cells glue together like Velcro]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:18 — close-up of Landsteiner's 14 November 1901 6 × 6 slide grid where Landsteiner's own Group C (O) red cells remain smooth and unclumped in every colleague's serum · LABEL: GROUP A, B, AND O]
[TEACH: In a February 1900 footnote and his 14 November 1901 paper testing six healthy scientists' blood in a 6 × 6 grid, Karl Landsteiner discovered Groups A, B, and C/O (with AB added by Decastello and Sturli in 1902), proving that clumping is caused by natural antigen-antibody lock-and-key reactions in healthy blood.]

At the University of Vienna, thirty-one-year-old pathologist Karl Landsteiner doubted that clumping blood was a symptom of illness.

To test his hunch, Landsteiner didn't use hospital patients at all. Instead, he drew small vials of blood from six completely healthy people in his own laboratory — five fellow scientists plus himself.

Spinning each vial in a centrifuge, he separated the red blood cells at the bottom from the clear golden serum at the top. Then he mixed every scientist's serum with every scientist's red cells in a six-by-six grid of thirty-six drops on glass slides — first noting the result in a seven-line footnote in February nineteen hundred, and publishing the full table on the fourteenth of November, nineteen hundred and one.

What he saw changed medicine forever. Nobody's serum ever clumped their own cells. Yet across the thirty-six squares, healthy human blood sorted itself into three distinct groups, which Landsteiner named **Group A**, **Group B**, and **Group C** — later renamed **Group O**, from the German word *Ohne*, meaning "without", which included Landsteiner's own blood! His students Alfred von Decastello and Adriano Sturli discovered the fourth group, **AB**, in nineteen hundred and two.

Each Type A or Type B red blood cell carries around two million microscopic sugar markers — antigens — on its surface, while your plasma naturally carries Y-shaped antibodies primed to attack whichever marker you lack. Give Type A blood to a Type B patient, and those antibodies lock onto the foreign cells and glue millions of them together like Velcro! Because Type O red cells carry neither A nor B markers, those antibodies slide right past them — making Type O red cells the universal donor.

Landsteiner had solved a two-hundred-year mystery in a single table! Case closed? Wrong. Why did almost no surgeon use Landsteiner's discovery for another decade — and why did doctors still have to sew a living donor's wrist artery directly to a patient's vein?

Even after Landsteiner published his blood groups in nineteen hundred and one, and New York physician Reuben Ottenberg introduced pre-transfusion cross-matching by nineteen eleven, hospitals still could not store a single bottle of blood!

Why? Because healthy blood has a second trap: the instant blood leaves a vein and touches a glass tube, calcium ions trigger a web of protein fibres that clots the blood solid in four to six minutes!

To save a dying five-day-old baby in New York on the eighth of March, nineteen hundred and eight, surgeon Alexis Carrel had to lay the baby's father on an operating table beside the infant and stitch the father's open wrist artery directly to the baby's tiny leg vein with silk thread — an achievement that won Carrel the nineteen twelve Nobel Prize, yet remained far too slow and dangerous for everyday emergencies.

Chemists knew that sodium citrate — the simple salt of citric acid found in lemons — binds calcium ions and stops blood clotting in a test tube, but doctors feared injecting it would poison the patient.

Then between nineteen fourteen and January nineteen fifteen — led by Albert Hustin in Brussels, Luis Agote in Buenos Aires on the ninth of November nineteen fourteen, and surgeon Richard Lewisohn at Mount Sinai Hospital in New York on the twenty-third of January nineteen fifteen — Lewisohn proved that adding a tiny two-tenths of one percent dose of sodium citrate kept blood liquid without harming the patient at all.

In November nineteen seventeen, on the Western Front in France, army doctor Oswald Hope Robertson packed bottles of citrated Group O blood on ice inside old wooden ammunition boxes — creating the world's first blood bank and turning transfusion from a two-surgeon operation into a bottle waiting at the front line!

Landsteiner finally won the Nobel Prize in nineteen thirty. Yet why did some patients given matched A, B, or O blood *still* suffer dangerous reactions until Landsteiner made one last discovery at age seventy-one?

Many people wonder what the plus or minus sign after their blood type actually means — or assume that once A, B, and O were matched, every blood transfusion was completely safe. Is that true?

Not until nineteen forty. Working in New York with Alexander Wiener and building on a nineteen thirty-nine clue by Philip Levine and Rufus Stetson, the seventy-one-year-old Karl Landsteiner discovered a second vital marker on red blood cells in January nineteen forty using serum tested with Rhesus monkeys: the **Rhesus factor**, or *Rh* plus and minus. Solving the Rh factor prevented fatal reactions in both transfusion patients and newborn babies. Today, one hundred and eighteen point five million blood donations are collected every year — nearly four life-saving bags every second — and Type O-negative blood stands ready in every trauma room when there isn't even a minute to test a patient's blood.

And as twentieth-century medicine and Alan Turing's early computers reshaped the world, how did two physicists at Bell Labs on the sixteenth of December nineteen forty-seven press a tiny plastic wedge wrapped in gold foil onto a crystal of germanium to invent the **transistor** — before Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce in September nineteen fifty-eight and nineteen fifty-nine shrank an entire circuit onto a sliver of silicon?

Six small vials of blood in a Vienna laboratory, a thirty-six-square table on glass slides, and half a teaspoon of citrus salt. Together, they turned a fatal gamble into a hundred million second chances at life.
