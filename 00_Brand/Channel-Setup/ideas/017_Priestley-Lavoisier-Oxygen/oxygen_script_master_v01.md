## PART 02: The Brewery Next Door and the Mouse in the Jar (1:25–3:05)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Brewery Next Door and the Mouse in the Jar · 1:25 · ~1.5 s]

[TEACH: After inventing soda water beside a Leeds brewery in 1767, Joseph Priestley used a 12-inch burning lens on 1 August 1774 to release pure oxygen from red mercuric oxide. On 8 March 1775 he proved a mouse lived twice as long in the gas, yet named it "dephlogisticated air" under Georg Ernst Stahl's 1703 phlogiston theory.]

Before he ever isolated oxygen, Joseph Priestley lived next door to a public brewery in Leeds in seventeen sixty-seven.

Curious about the heavy blanket of unknown gas hovering over the fermenting beer vats, Priestley poured water back and forth between two glasses inside the cloud. Suddenly, the water turned delightfully fizzy. He had just invented soda water — winning the Royal Society's Copley Medal on the thirtieth of November, seventeen seventy-three.

Now, to trap gases without losing them, Priestley inverted glass jars over tubs of water or liquid mercury.

Then, on the first of August, seventeen seventy-four, at Bowood House in Wiltshire, he aimed a twelve-inch burning lens at a red powder called *mercurius calcinatus per se* — red mercury oxide — floating on mercury inside a glass vial.

Focused sunlight heated the red powder beyond six hundred degrees Celsius — hot enough to split solid stone into shiny beads of liquid mercury and four to five times its volume in colourless gas.

When Priestley thrust a candle into the gas, suddenly the flame blazed with blinding brilliance.

Then, on the eighth of March, seventeen seventy-five, he slipped a live mouse under the glass. In that small volume of ordinary air, a mouse suffocated in fifteen minutes. However, in Priestley's gas, the mouse was still lively after thirty minutes.

Breathing it himself through a glass tube, Priestley wrote: "Hitherto only two mice and myself have had the privilege of breathing it."

But Priestley remained loyal to a theory framed in seventeen-oh-three by German chemist Georg Ernst Stahl, claiming burning things release phlogiston. Assuming his pure gas simply had all its phlogiston wrung out, Priestley called it "dephlogisticated air."

So why did the phlogiston theory collapse the moment someone put rusting metal on a pair of scales — and what happened when Priestley visited Paris in October seventeen seventy-four?

## PART 03: The Dinner Table in Paris and the 12-Day Retort (3:05–4:55)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Dinner Table in Paris and the 12-Day Retort · 3:05 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:08 — Paris, October 1774: at a candlelit dining table in the Paris Arsenal, Antoine Lavoisier (readable face, early thirties, powdered wig, silk waistcoat) and sixteen-year-old Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier (readable face, sixteen, sharp dark eyes, holding an English notebook and charcoal stylus) listen intently across the table to Joseph Priestley · WHO: Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier (1758–1836) — French chemistry translator · WHEN: Paris, October 1774 · LABEL: THE PARIS DINNER]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:38 — back-and-forth portrait cuts between Joseph Priestley in England and Antoine Lavoisier in Paris in April 1775 as Lavoisier reads his Easter Memoir to the Royal Academy of Sciences while a rusting bar of tin tips a brass balance downward · WHEN: 26 April 1775 · FACT: Tried to claim sole credit]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:08 — Explainer diagram on period paper: an inked two-part mass-balance diagram of Lavoisier's 12-day swan-neck retort experiment shows 4 ounces of mercury heated in 50 cubic inches of air over 12 days; the air shrinks by 8 to 9 cubic inches (~1/5th) as 45 grains of red mercury oxide form, and heating those 45 grains strongly returns 41.5 grains of liquid mercury plus 8 to 9 cubic inches (3.5 grains) of pure oxygen · WHEN: 1777 · DATA: inked mass-balance diagram of Lavoisier's 12-day swan-neck retort: 50 cubic inches of air loses 8–9 cubic inches (~1/5th) as 45 grains of red oxide gain 3.5 grains, then heating yields 41.5 grains mercury + 3.5 grains (8–9 in³) oxygen · SCALE: 8 to 9 cubic inches out of 50 — roughly 1/5th of every breath you take is oxygen]
[TEACH: After hearing of Priestley's experiment over dinner in Paris in October 1774, Antoine and Marie-Anne Lavoisier used a 12-day sealed swan-neck retort experiment to prove that rusting mercury gains the exact weight of oxygen (3.5 grains, about one-fifth of the air) that disappears from the bell jar.]

For decades, one stubborn paradox haunted the phlogiston theory.

When you burn a log of wood, the ash left behind is lighter. However, when you heat or rust a metal like tin, lead, or mercury into a flaky powder, the metal gets *heavier*. What if burning releases phlogiston — why would losing something make metal weigh more?

In Paris, wealthy Academy chemist Antoine Lavoisier and his young wife and collaborator Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier set out to weigh the secret. Marie-Anne learned English and Latin to translate foreign chemistry papers, and studied under painter Jacques-Louis David to draw every piece of apparatus in their laboratory.

Then, in October seventeen seventy-four, Joseph Priestley dined with the Lavoisiers in Paris and described the mysterious gas he had baked out of red mercury powder.

However, when Lavoisier repeated the experiment and presented it at Easter on the twenty-sixth of April, seventeen seventy-five, without giving Priestley credit, Priestley was outraged.

Yet Lavoisier looked deeper and saw what Priestley had missed. In his classic twelve-day experiment, perfected by seventeen seventy-seven, Lavoisier sealed four ounces of liquid mercury inside a curved swan-neck glass retort linked to a bell jar holding fifty cubic inches of air.

He heated the mercury just below boiling until twelve days had passed. Red flakes formed on the surface, and the air in the jar shrank by eight to nine cubic inches — about one-fifth of its volume. Worse for phlogiston, the leftover four-fifths suffocated mice and snuffed out flames.

Next, Lavoisier scraped up the forty-five grains of red flakes and heated them fiercely. Suddenly, out came forty-one point five grains of liquid mercury, plus eight to nine cubic inches of Priestley's gas — weighing exactly three point five grains.

Rusting hadn't lost phlogiston; it had *gained* three point five grains of gas from the air. In seventeen seventy-seven, Lavoisier named that gas oxygen.

Wait — if Lavoisier solved the mystery from Priestley's clue, why did a modest Swedish pharmacist have reason to be furious with both of them?

## PART 04: The Unopened Letter and the Guinea Pig in Ice (4:55–6:40)

Priestley isolated oxygen in August seventeen seventy-four, and Lavoisier weighed it. Case closed? Wrong.

Two full years earlier, between seventeen seventy-one and seventeen seventy-two in Uppsala, Sweden, a brilliant pharmaceutical chemist named Carl Wilhelm Scheele had already made pure oxygen by heating silver carbonate and red mercury oxide. He called it "fire air."

Why did Scheele lose the race? First, his printer delayed his book until seventeen seventy-seven. Second, on the thirtieth of September, seventeen seventy-four — weeks before Priestley's dinner in Paris — Scheele mailed a letter in French directly to Antoine Lavoisier, explaining step by step how to make the gas.

However, Lavoisier never replied, and kept the letter secret — until historians found Scheele's handwriting tucked inside Lavoisier's private papers in eighteen ninety.

Even so, Lavoisier pushed deeper and transformed chemistry into an exact science. Between seventeen eighty and seventeen eighty-three, working with mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace, he placed a live guinea pig inside a chamber packed with ice for ten hours.

By weighing the thirteen ounces of melted ice against the carbon dioxide the guinea pig breathed out, they proved that breathing is slow combustion: your cells burn carbon with inhaled oxygen to keep your body warm.

Then, in his landmark seventeen eighty-nine textbook *Traité Élémentaire de Chimie*, illustrated with thirteen copperplates by Marie-Anne, Lavoisier sealed fruit juice, sugar, and yeast inside airtight flasks on his balance.

Even as fermentation foamed violently into alcohol and gas, the total weight before and after did not change by a single grain. Lavoisier wrote the Law of Conservation of Mass: "Nothing is created, in the operations of art or of nature, and in every operation there is an equal quantity of matter before and after."

Now, if matter can never vanish, when you exercise and lose ten kilograms of weight, where does that lost fat actually go?

[TEACH: By Lavoisier's Law of Conservation of Mass, 84% of lost body fat (8.4 kg of every 10 kg) leaves through your lungs as carbon dioxide made with inhaled oxygen. Though Lavoisier was guillotined on 8 May 1794, Marie-Anne preserved his work and modern chemistry.]

When you diet and lose ten kilograms of fat, most people — and even many doctors — think that weight turns into heat or energy. Is that true?

No. Turning ten kilograms of matter into pure energy would level a city. As biochemists proved in twenty fourteen using Lavoisier's law, when your body burns ten kilograms of fat with oxygen, eight point four kilograms — eighty-four percent — is breathed right out of your lungs as invisible carbon dioxide, and the last one point six kilograms leaves as water. You literally exhale your lost weight into the air.

Sadly, the French Revolution tore both pioneers apart. In July seventeen ninety-one, an angry mob in Birmingham burned down Priestley's laboratory for supporting the Revolution; he fled to America and died on the sixth of February, eighteen-oh-four, still defending phlogiston to his last breath.

In Paris, on the eighth of May, seventeen ninety-four, during the Reign of Terror, a revolutionary tribunal sent fifty-year-old Antoine Lavoisier to the guillotine because he had collected taxes.

The next day, mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange whispered: "It took them only an instant to cut off that head, and one hundred years may not suffice to produce another like it." Yet Marie-Anne survived prison and published his final memoirs, securing the chemical revolution forever.

Priestley had used a glass lens to focus visible sunlight onto red powder. Twenty-six years later, in February eighteen hundred, astronomer William Herschel placed a thermometer in the dark shadow *outside* a sunlit rainbow — and discovered that the hottest light from the Sun is completely invisible to your eyes.

A twelve-inch lens, a lively mouse and a sealed glass retort. Together, they revealed the gas that keeps every cell in your body alive.
