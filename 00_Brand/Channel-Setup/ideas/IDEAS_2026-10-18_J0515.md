  1. **The Florentine well-diggers' 32-foot wall (1638):** In **1638**, well-diggers and fountain-makers in **Florence** came to an elderly, blind **Galileo Galilei** with a baffling practical failure: no matter how tightly they sealed their pistons, **no suction pump on Earth could ever lift water higher than `18 Florentine braccia` (`~32 feet` / `10.3 metres`)**! Above 32 feet, the water column simply snapped and left an empty space!
  2. **Evangelista Torricelli's 13.6× liquid-mercury shortcut (1643 → 11 June 1644):** Why 32 feet? After Galileo died in 1642, his 35-year-old successor **Evangelista Torricelli** realised that a pump doesn't *pull* water up from inside — **the weight of the outside atmosphere pushes down on the well**, balancing a 32-foot column of water like a giant seesaw! To test this indoors without a 35-foot pipe, Torricelli and **Vincenzo Viviani** in **1643–1644** switched to liquid **mercury (`quicksilver`)**, which is **13.6 times denser than water**. Dividing `32 feet` by `13.6`, Torricelli predicted the atmosphere should hold up only **`~30 inches` (`760 mm` / `1.25 Florentine braccia`) of mercury** — and when Viviani inverted a 4-foot glass tube of mercury into a dish, the mercury dropped straight to **30 inches**, leaving the **first sustained man-made vacuum in history (`the Torricellian vacuum`)** at the top of the tube! As Torricelli wrote in his famous letter to Michelangelo Ricci on **11 June 1644**: ***"We live submerged at the bottom of an ocean of the element air, which by unquestioned experiments is known to have weight."***
- **Twist (three-part):**
  1. **Blaise Pascal's 46-foot red-wine tube (1646) & the Puy-de-Dôme mountain proof (19 September 1648):** Aristotelian critics refused to believe the space at the top of Torricelli's tube was truly empty, claiming invisible "spirits" inside the glass held the mercury up. First, in **late 1646** in Rouen, 23-year-old French prodigy **Blaise Pascal** built two **40-to-46-foot glass tubes** lashed to a ship's mast and filled one with water and one with **red wine**: because alcohol is slightly *less* dense than water, Aristotelians predicted wine's stronger "spirits" would push the wine *lower* — instead, the lighter red wine rose **higher than the water (`~32.5 feet`)**!
