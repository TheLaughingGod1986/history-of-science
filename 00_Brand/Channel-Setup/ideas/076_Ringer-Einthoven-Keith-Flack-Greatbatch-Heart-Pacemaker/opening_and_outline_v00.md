### PART 02: 1882–1883 & Summer 1906 — Sidney Ringer's London Tap-Water Mistake & Keith and Flack's Farmhouse Discovery of the `SA Node` (1:25–3:10)
- **Cold open (in November 1882, London physician Sidney Ringer had to print one of the most embarrassing — and most important — corrections in medical history: his miracle salt solution only kept hearts beating because his technician had secretly filled the bottles from a London kitchen tap!):** **University College Hospital, London, 1882–1883 & Bredgar, Kent, Summer 1906 (published April 1907)**.
- **1882–1883 (*Journal of Physiology*) — Sidney Ringer, the New River Water Company & `Ringer's Solution`:**
  - In **1882**, British physician **Sidney Ringer** (1835–1910) was testing what keeps an isolated frog heart beating outside the body. He published a paper reporting that a simple solution of **0.75% table salt (`sodium chloride, NaCl`)** in pure distilled water kept a frog's heart beating strongly for hours.
  - Then Ringer mixed a fresh batch of pure distilled water and salt himself — and **the heart stopped dead within twenty minutes**!
  - Baffled, Ringer questioned his laboratory technician (`Fielder`) and uncovered the secret: to save time distilling water, the technician had been filling the "distilled water" bottles straight from the **University College Hospital pipe tap**, supplied by the **New River Water Company**!
  - Analysing that London tap water, Ringer discovered it contained **`38.3 milligrams per litre` of dissolved `calcium (Ca²⁺)`** (alongside traces of **`potassium, K⁺`** and bicarbonate). Publishing a landmark correction in **1883** (*J. Physiol.*), Ringer proved that while **sodium (`Na⁺`)** sparks the electrical wave and **potassium (`K⁺`)** resets the heart muscle (`relaxation`), **calcium ions (`Ca²⁺`)** are the physical key that unlocks heart muscle contraction (`062`) — giving every hospital on Earth **`Ringer's Solution`**!
- **Summer 1906 / April 1907 (*Journal of Anatomy and Physiology*) — Arthur Keith & Martin Flack discover the `Sinoatrial (SA) Node`:**
  - But *where* inside the heart does each beat start? Anatomists had already found the lower wiring: Czech physiologist **Jan Evangelista Purkyně (1839)** found the fast-conducting ventricular fibres (**`Purkinje fibres`**), Swiss cardiologist **Wilhelm His Jr. (1893)** found the bridge between the upper and lower chambers (**`the Bundle of His`**), and Japanese pathologist **Sunao Tawara (1906)** found the junction box that delays the spark by `0.1 seconds` so the atria can finish squeezing before the ventricles fire (**`the atrioventricular or AV node`**).
  - In the summer of **1906**, anatomist **Arthur Keith** (1866–1955) and his young medical student **Martin William Flack** (1882–1931) rented a medieval farmhouse cottage in **Bredgar, Kent**, converting a dark cupboard into a darkroom and slicing paraffin sections of mole, mouse, and human hearts.
  - One warm afternoon while Keith and his wife rode their bicycles into Maidstone, 24-year-old **Martin Flack** stained serial sections of a mole's heart and spotted a tiny, crescent-shaped nest of specialized self-firing muscle-nerve cells right where the superior vena cava enters the right atrium!
  - Published in **April 1907** (*J. Anat. Physiol.*), Keith and Flack had found the **`sinoatrial (SA) node`** — the heart's master **natural pacemaker**, whose leaky ion channels automatically fire an electrical spark **60 to 100 times every minute**!

### PART 03: May 1887 & 1901–1903 — Bulldog "Jimmy", Willem Einthoven's 600-Pound String Galvanometer & the `P–Q–R–S–T` Wave — Midpoint Twist (3:10–4:55)
- **Cold open ("How do you manufacture a wire four times thinner than a human red blood cell? - **May 1887 (*Journal of Physiology*) — Augustus Desiré Waller & his bulldog "Jimmy":**
  - Because salt water conducts electricity (`019`), the `SA node`'s spark spreads weak electrical currents (`~1 millivolt`) all the way to your skin.
  - In **May 1887** at St Mary's Hospital in London, British physiologist **Augustus Desiré Waller** (1856–1922) recorded the **first human (and canine!) electrocardiogram** using Gabriel Lippmann's mercury capillary electrometer — famously demonstrating it before the Royal Society with his beloved pet bulldog **"Jimmy"** standing calmly with his paws in glass jars of salt water (`even sparking a short debate in the British Parliament over whether standing a dog in salt water was cruelty to animals!`)!
  - **The midpoint twist:** Ironically, **Augustus Waller himself insisted the electrocardiogram would never be useful to hospital doctors** — writing in **1911**: *"I do not imagine that electrocardiography is likely to find any very extensive use in the hospital... It can at most be of rare and occasional use"!*
- **1901–1903 (*Pflügers Archiv*) — Willem Einthoven proves Waller wrong with a 600-pound electromagnet and a `2.5-micrometre` quartz string:**
  - Sitting in the audience at Waller's London demonstration in **1887** was Dutch physiologist **Willem Einthoven** (1860–1927). Einthoven realized Waller's mercury tube was too heavy and sluggish to catch the heart's fast spikes: instead of calculating math corrections (`where Einthoven first introduced the letters P, Q, R, S, T in 1895`), he needed an instrument with a moving wire of **almost zero mass**!
  - In **1901–1903** in Leiden (`building on Clément Ader's 1897 undersea telegraph receiver and Charles Vernon Boys's quartz-arrow trick`), Einthoven pulled a **quartz glass thread just `2.5 micrometres` thick** (`invisible to the naked eye`), coated it with silver so it conducted electricity, and stretched it between the poles of a **600-pound (`270-kilogram`) water-cooled electromagnet** (**`the string galvanometer`**)!
  - When a patient sat with both arms and their left leg in **three buckets of salt water (`Einthoven's Triangle`)**, the `1-millivolt` heartbeat current made the microscopic quartz thread twitch between the magnets; an arc lamp projected its magnified shadow onto a moving scroll of photographic film, tracing the crystal-clear **`P–Q–R–S–T` wave**!
  - Because the **600-pound machine** took up **two rooms** and needed **five technicians** to run, sick patients in Leiden University Hospital couldn't be carried to Einthoven's lab — so in **1906**, Einthoven wired the hospital to his lab via **`1.5 kilometres` (`nearly a mile`) of underground Leiden telephone cable**, inventing **telemedicine (` the "telecardiogram"`)** and winning the **1924 Nobel Prize**!

### PART 04: 1956 – 7 May 1958 — Wilson Greatbatch's Wrong Resistor & the First Implantable Pacemaker (4:55–6:40)
- **Cold open (in 1956 in Buffalo, New York, electrical engineer Wilson Greatbatch reached into his resistor box for a brown-black-orange 10,000-ohm resistor — his fingers accidentally pulled out a brown-black-green 1,000,000-ohm resistor instead, and when he switched on the circuit, it blinked at him like a beating human heart!):** **University of Buffalo / Chronic Disease Research Institute & Veterans Hospital, Buffalo, New York, 1956 – June 1960**.
- **1952–1957 — Why early pacemakers were tethered to wall sockets and wheeled carts:**
  - When scar tissue blocks the `AV node` or `Bundle of His` (`complete heart block`), the upper chambers (`atria`) keep firing at `70 bpm` while the lower pumping chambers (`ventricles`) drop to a sluggish `20–30 bpm` or stop altogether.
  - In **1952**, Boston cardiologist **Paul Zoll** saved lives with an external mains-powered pacemaker, and after a **31 October 1957** power blackout in Minneapolis killed a child on a mains pacemaker, **Earl Bakken** (co-founder of Medtronic) built the first wearable battery-powered transistor pacemaker for surgeon **C. Walton Lillehei**.
  - Yet external wires passing through an open hole in the chest skin always risked fatal infection (`001`). Doctors believed an **implantable** pacemaker sealed completely under the skin was impossible because vacuum tubes were too hot and bulky and batteries would die in days!
- **1956 – 7 May 1958 (*Surgery* 1960) — Wilson Greatbatch grabs the `1 MΩ` resistor & teams up with William Chardack:**
  - In **1956** in Buffalo, former Navy rear-seat radar gunner and Cornell electrical engineer **Wilson Greatbatch** (1919–2011) was building a small **silicon transistor (`029`) oscillator** to *record* fast heart sounds (`tachycardia`).
  - Reaching into his workshop bin for a **`10,000-ohm` (`10 kΩ`, colour bands brown–black–orange)** resistor, Greatbatch misread the third colour band and accidentally pulled out a **`1,000,000-ohm` (`1 MΩ`, brown–black–green — 100 times larger!)** resistor and soldered it into the circuit!
  - Instead of oscillating continuously, the transistor circuit gave a sharp **`1.8-millisecond` electrical pulse**, went completely quiet for **1 second**, and pulsed again — drawing almost zero battery current between beats!
  - Greatbatch stared at the oscilloscope in awe: he had heard two brain surgeons over lunch in 1951 describe patients dying of complete heart block, and he knew instantly that his **two-transistor blocking oscillator** could pace a human heart for years on tiny mercury-zinc batteries!
  - Every surgeon he approached told him it wouldn't work — until in **April 1958**, Chief of Surgery **William M. Chardack** (1914–2006) at the Buffalo Veterans Administration Hospital asked if Greatbatch could build one. Three weeks later, on **7 May 1958**, Greatbatch and Chardack wired a hand-cast **two-cubic-inch epoxy pacemaker** (`just 8 parts: 2 transistors, 1 transformer, 2 resistors, 1 capacitor, and mercury-zinc cells`) to a dog's heart — and watched it take immediate command of the heartbeat (`before Swedish surgeon Åke Senning and Rune Elmqvist implanted a short-lived rechargeable unit in Arne Larsson in October 1958, and Chardack and Greatbatch implanted their multi-year self-powered pacemaker into 77-year-old Frank Henefelt in Buffalo on 6 June 1960 — before Greatbatch later invented the long-life lithium-iodide pacemaker battery in 1970!`)!
