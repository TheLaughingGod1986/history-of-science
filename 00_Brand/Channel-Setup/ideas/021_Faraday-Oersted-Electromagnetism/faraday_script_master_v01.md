## PART 02: The Sideways Compass and the Cup of Mercury (1:25–3:05)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Sideways Compass and the Cup of Mercury · 1:25 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:28 — Copenhagen, 21 April 1820 (published 21 July 1820): Hans Christian Ørsted (readable face, early forties, dark coat, white cravat) closes a battery circuit above a compass needle as the needle swings to a 90-degree right angle · WHEN: Copenhagen, 21 April 1820 · WHEN: 21 July 1820 · LABEL: NINETY DEGREES SIDEWAYS]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:58 — Explainer diagram on period paper: an inked geometry comparison contrasts straight-line push-and-pull forces against Ørsted and Faraday's 90-degree concentric circular magnetic lines wrapping around a current-carrying wire · WHO: Humphry Davy (1778–1829) — Royal Society president · WHO: William Hyde Wollaston (1766–1828) — English physicist & chemist · WHEN: 1813 · FACT: Left school at age 13 · DATA: inked force-geometry diagram contrasting straight-line central forces (push/pull along the line joining two bodies) vs Ørsted and Faraday's 90-degree circular magnetic lines of force wrapping around a current-carrying wire · SCALE: a 90-degree sideways push — instead of pulling the wire in and stopping, the circular magnetic field chases the wire around in an endless circle]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:28 — Royal Institution Basement, London, 3–4 September 1821: twenty-nine-year-old Michael Faraday (readable face, bright eyes, centre-parted hair, laboratory apron) connects a battery to a glass cup of liquid mercury holding an upright bar magnet in wax; as the suspended copper wire whirls rapidly in circles around the magnet, Faraday dances around the table · WHEN: London, 3–4 September 1821 · LABEL: "THERE THEY GO!"]
[TEACH: After Hans Christian Ørsted discovered on 21 April 1820 that an electric current deflects a compass needle at right angles, former bookbinder's apprentice Michael Faraday realized the magnetic force was circular and built the first electric motor in a cup of mercury on 3–4 September 1821.]

On the evening of the twenty-first of April, eighteen twenty, during a lecture in Copenhagen, Danish professor Hans Christian Ørsted connected a thin platinum wire to a battery directly over a magnetic compass.

Suddenly, the instant the current flowed, the compass needle jerked sideways.

When Ørsted published his four-page Latin report on the twenty-first of July, eighteen twenty, physicists across Europe were stunned. For more than a century, every known force in physics — gravity, static electricity, ordinary magnetism — pulled or pushed in a straight line directly toward or away from an object. Yet Ørsted's needle didn't point *at* the wire. It turned to stand at a ninety-degree right angle across it.

In London, Royal Society President Sir Humphry Davy and chemist William Hyde Wollaston tried and failed to make a wire spin on its own axis. Watching quietly was Davy's twenty-nine-year-old laboratory assistant, Michael Faraday — a blacksmith's son who had left school at age thirteen, spent seven years as a bookbinder's apprentice, and joined the Royal Institution in eighteen thirteen after reading the science books he bound.

Because Faraday knew almost no formal algebra, he didn't think in equations; he thought in pictures. He realised the magnetic force around Ørsted's wire wasn't pushing in straight lines at all — it wrapped around the wire in endless concentric circles.

On the third and fourth of September, eighteen twenty-one, Faraday stood a bar magnet upright in wax inside a glass cup filled with liquid mercury, and hung a copper wire from a cork so its tip dipped into the mercury.

The moment he connected a battery, the circular magnetic force chased the wire around and around the magnet in non-stop circles. Faraday had built the world's first electric motor — dancing around the table shouting, *"There they go! There they go! We have succeeded at last!"*

Faraday had just invented the electric motor at age twenty-nine. So why did his greatest triumph almost ruin his career — and why did his own mentor turn against him?

## PART 03: The Blackball Vote and the Ten-Year Silence (3:05–4:55)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Blackball Vote and the Ten-Year Silence · 3:05 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:08 — back-and-forth portrait cuts between Sir Humphry Davy in his presidential chair at the Royal Society and young Michael Faraday in his basement laboratory in October 1821 holding his published Quarterly Journal of Science paper · WHEN: October 1821 · LABEL: MENTOR TURNS RIVAL]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:42 — London, 8 January 1824: Fellows of the Royal Society line up before a mahogany ballot box; a single black ball drops into the reject drawer against Faraday's election · WHEN: 8 January 1824 · FACT: One blackball vote against him]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:18 — London, 1822–1829: Faraday sits in a dusty glassworks stirring molten optical glass for a government committee, then opens his small leather pocket notebook to the handwritten line "Convert magnetism into electricity" beside a small iron cylinder and copper coil · WHEN: 1822 · WHEN: 1829 · LABEL: "CONVERT MAGNETISM INTO ELECTRICITY"]
[TEACH: When Faraday published his electromagnetic motor in October 1821, jealous mentor Sir Humphry Davy accused him of slighting William Hyde Wollaston and opposed Faraday's 8 January 1824 Royal Society election, delaying Faraday's 1822 goal to convert magnetism into electricity for nearly a decade.]

In October eighteen twenty-one, eager to share his breakthrough, Faraday rushed his paper into print — and forgot to mention an earlier conversation between Humphry Davy and William Hyde Wollaston.

Watching his former servant celebrated across Europe, Davy burned with jealousy. Rumours spread through London that Faraday had stolen Wollaston's idea.

However, Faraday went straight to Wollaston to show his laboratory notes, and Wollaston graciously agreed that Faraday's circular motor was entirely his own. Even so, Davy refused to back down. When Faraday was nominated as a Fellow of the Royal Society, Davy personally ordered him to withdraw his name.

Faraday stood his ground. When the secret ballot was held on the eighth of January, eighteen twenty-four, Faraday was elected — with one solitary blackball vote cast against him.

For the next five years, Davy steered Faraday onto exhausting government committees making optical glass, keeping him away from electromagnetism until Davy's death in eighteen twenty-nine.

Yet throughout that long silence, Faraday carried a small iron cylinder and a coil of wire in his pocket, haunted by four words he had written in his notebook back in eighteen twenty-two: *"Convert magnetism into electricity."*

Every physicist in Europe tried clamping powerful magnets next to copper coils to see if magnetism would push a current through the wire. Nothing happened.

Why did every stationary magnet fail for ten years — and what happened on the twenty-ninth of August, eighteen thirty-one, when Faraday saw a needle move for just half a second?

## PART 04: The Iron Ring and the Half-Second Twitch — Midpoint Twist (4:55–6:40)

[VISUAL MUST: 6:00 — London, 28 October 1831: Faraday turns the handle of a 12-inch copper disc mounted on an axle between the poles of the Royal Society's great horseshoe magnet, generating the world's first steady induced electric current · WHEN: 28 October 1831 · LABEL: THE FIRST GENERATOR]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once. Standing beside Faraday's hollow paper cylinder wrapped in 220 feet of copper wire, the Explorer holds a bar magnet completely still inside the coil while watching the dead galvanometer needle, then yanks the magnet briskly out and jumps back as the needle swings wide]
[TEACH: On 29 August, 17 October and 28 October 1831, Michael Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction: a stationary magnet produces zero current, but a changing or moving magnetic field induces electricity, allowing his 12-inch spinning copper disc to become the first electrical generator.]

On the twenty-ninth of August, eighteen thirty-one, Faraday sat in his basement with a solid ring of soft iron six inches wide. Around one side, he wound more than two hundred feet of copper wire connected to a battery. Around the other side — separated by cloth and air — he wound two hundred feet of wire leading to a magnetic detector needle.

He connected the battery, expecting a steady electric current to flow in the second coil. Case closed? Wrong.

The instant the battery wire touched, the needle jerked violently — and then settled straight back to zero. While the full battery current poured through the iron ring, the detector needle sat completely dead.

Baffled, Faraday disconnected the battery. Suddenly, the needle jerked again — in the exact opposite direction.

In a flash, Faraday saw what every mathematician in Europe had missed for eleven years. A stationary magnet *never* generates electricity. Electricity is born *only* while a magnetic field is moving or changing.

To prove it, on the seventeenth of October, eighteen thirty-one, Faraday wrapped two hundred and twenty feet of copper wire around a hollow paper tube and thrust a bar magnet briskly inside. As the magnet moved in, the needle swung one way; as he pulled it out, the needle swung the other way.

Finally, eleven days later, on the twenty-eighth of October, eighteen thirty-one, Faraday mounted a twelve-inch copper disc between the jaws of a giant magnet and turned the crank. Because the spinning disc cut continuously through the magnet's field, a steady stream of electricity flowed out — the world's first electrical generator.

How could a magnet push electrons inside a wire without ever touching it — and why did mathematicians laugh at Faraday's invisible "lines of force" until a young physicist calculated how fast they travel?

## PART 05: Invisible Lines of Force and the Speed of Light (6:40–8:15)

[CHAPTER CARD: Invisible Lines of Force and the Speed of Light · 6:40 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:43 — an animated timeline on period paper builds across the frame: 21 Apr 1820 (Ørsted's sideways compass) · 4 Sep 1821 (Faraday's mercury-cup motor) · 8 Jan 1824 (Royal Society ballot) · 29 Aug 1831 (Faraday's iron-ring induction) · 28 Oct 1831 (12-inch copper-disc generator) · 1852 (iron-filing magnetic field lines) · 1865 (Maxwell's electromagnetic waves), alongside an inked diagram of electromagnetic waves rippling at 300,000 km/s · WHO: James Clerk Maxwell (1831–1879) — Scottish mathematical physicist · WHEN: 1852 · WHEN: 1865 · WHEN: 1820–1865 (timeline marker) · DATA: animated timeline building 21 Apr 1820 · 4 Sep 1821 · 8 Jan 1824 · 29 Aug 1831 · 28 Oct 1831 · 1852 · 1865 alongside an inked electromagnetic wave diagram showing Faraday's electric and magnetic field lines rippling through space at 300,000 kilometres per second · SCALE: 300,000 kilometres per second — Maxwell proved Faraday's electric and magnetic lines ripple through space at the exact speed of light, seven and a half times around Earth every second · FACT: Iron filings mapped the field]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:18 — London, 1852: Faraday sprinkles fine iron filings onto a sheet of white paper waxed over a bar magnet, revealing curved spiderweb arcs filling the "empty" space around the poles · LABEL: SPACE IS NOT EMPTY]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:45 — Paris & Berlin, 1896–1938: a high-voltage induction coil sparks inside a darkened physics laboratory beside a closed wooden desk drawer holding a chunk of uranium rock and a photographic plate, dissolving into snowy woods in Sweden · WHEN: February 1896 · WHEN: December 1938 · LABEL: INSIDE THE ATOM]
[TEACH: In 1852, Michael Faraday used iron filings to show that space is filled with physical magnetic fields ("lines of force"), which James Clerk Maxwell proved in 1865 ripple through space at 300,000 kilometres per second as light itself.]

Many people think the space between a magnet and a piece of iron is completely empty, with the magnet reaching across pure nothingness. Is that true?

Faraday didn't think so. In eighteen fifty-two, sprinkling fine iron filings onto waxed paper over a magnet, he argued that "empty" space is actually packed with physical lines of magnetic and electric force, stretching like invisible rubber bands between atoms.

Mathematicians ridiculed the self-taught bookbinder's picture — until young Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell translated Faraday's lines of force into equations in eighteen sixty-five. When Maxwell calculated how fast a ripple travels along Faraday's electric and magnetic lines, the answer came out to three hundred thousand kilometres per second — seven and a half times around Earth every second, the exact speed of light. Light itself is an electromagnetic wave.

And what happened when physicists used Faraday's high-voltage induction coils to study glowing rocks and bombard heavy uranium atoms — until an exiled physicist walking through the Swedish snow in December nineteen thirty-eight realised the atomic nucleus itself had split in two?

A twitching compass in Copenhagen, a cup of mercury and an iron ring in a London basement. Together, they wired the modern world.
