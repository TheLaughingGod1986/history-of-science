### 1. Cell Theory: Robert Hooke's cork, Robert Remak & the stolen secret of how cells divide (recommended for 015)
- **Familiar thing:** the **30 trillion** microscopic cells that make up your skin, blood, muscles and bones — and the fact that every one of them came from a single cell dividing in two.
- **Person & room:** **Robert Hooke** (1635–1703), Curator of Experiments at the Royal Society in Gresham College, London, slicing a paper-thin shaving of bottle cork with a keen penknife under his red-leather compound microscope lit by a water-filled glass globe in **1663–January 1665**; paired with **Matthias Jakob Schleiden** (1804–1881) and **Theodor Schwann** (1810–1882) over dinner and in Johannes Müller's Berlin laboratory in **October 1838**, and Polish-Jewish embryologist **Robert Remak** (1815–1865) working in a tiny attic laboratory at the Charité hospital in Berlin (**1841–1852**).
- **Date & proof:** In **January 1665**, the Royal Society published Hooke's giant illustrated folio ***Micrographia*** (which diarist **Samuel Pepys** sat up until 2 a.m. reading on **21 January 1665**, calling it *"the most ingenious book that ever I read in my life"*). Looking at a shaving of dead cork Oak bark (*Quercus suber*), Hooke saw regular rows of microscopic walled boxes that reminded him of monks' bedrooms (*cella*) — coining the biological word **"cell"** — and counted **1,080 pores along a single inch**, calculating **1,259,712,000 cells in one cubic inch of cork**. Yet because cork is dead bark, Hooke thought "cells" were merely empty channels for plant sap! Over 170 years later, in **October 1838** in Berlin, botanist **Matthias Schleiden** (a former Hamburg lawyer who had shot himself in the forehead in despair before turning to science) told physiologist **Theodor Schwann** how plant cells form around a dark central spot (the **nucleus**, named by **Robert Brown** in **1831**). Schwann realised he had seen the exact same nucleated boxes inside frog notochord and pig cartilage — proving in **1839** that all plants and animals are built of cells.
- **Twist (two-part):**
  1. **The Newton rivalry:** After Hooke died on **3 March 1703**, his bitter rival **Isaac Newton** became President of the Royal Society later that year, moved the Society's headquarters from Gresham College to Crane Court in **1710**, and **Hooke's only portrait at the Royal Society vanished without trace** — leaving the man who named the cell without a single confirmed face in history.
  2. **The Berlin crystal blunder & credit theft ("Case closed? Wrong."):** Even as Schleiden and Schwann founded cell theory in **1838–1839**, they got the biggest question completely backwards: they insisted new cells crystallised out of formless fluid (*blastema*) like rock candy in sugar water! Between **1841 and 1852**, Polish-Jewish scientist **Robert Remak** — who was barred from a full Prussian professorship because he refused to renounce his Jewish faith — hardened fertilised frog eggs and chick red blood cells in Berlin and proved that **every new cell comes only from an existing cell dividing in two**. Three years later (**1855**), Remak's colleague **Rudolf Virchow** (1821–1902) published Remak's breakthrough under the famous Latin motto ***"Omnis cellula e cellula"*** ("All cells come from cells") without citing Remak — setting off a bitter lifelong rift!

### 2. DNA & Photo 51: Rosalind Franklin's 100-hour X-ray & the race for the double helix
- **Familiar thing:** a cheek-swab DNA test, or the twisted ladder that carries the code for every living thing.
- **Person & room:** Physical chemist **Rosalind Franklin** (1920–1958) and PhD student **Raymond Gosling** in a damp basement X-ray diffraction room at **King's College London** on **2–6 May 1952**, paired in back-and-forth portrait cuts with **James Watson** and **Francis Crick** building tin-and-cardboard molecular models at the **Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge** (**January–February 1953**), **Maurice Wilkins** at King's, and **Linus Pauling** in California.
- **Date & twist:** In **May 1952**, Franklin beamed X-rays for **100 hours** through a single strand of wet calf-thymus DNA no thicker than a spider's silk, hydrated by bubbling hydrogen through a salt solution, and captured **Photograph 51** — a stark black cross of spots that spelled out a helix with **10 rungs per turn** and a **34-ångström repeat**. In **January 1953**, Linus Pauling published a broken **three-stranded** DNA model with the phosphates on the inside. Then on **30 January 1953**, Maurice Wilkins showed Franklin's unpublished *Photo 51* to James Watson without her knowledge, and **Max Perutz** handed Crick an MRC committee report containing Franklin's exact crystal-symmetry measurements — letting Watson and Crick realise the two sugar-phosphate backbones run in **opposite directions** on the outside and lock the base pairs (`A–T`, `G–C`) together on **28 February 1953**. Franklin died of ovarian cancer in **1958** at age 37; when Watson, Crick and Wilkins received the **1962 Nobel Prize**, none of them explained how much their model owed to her unpublished data until Watson's 1968 memoir inadvertently gave the game away.

### 3. Invisible Light: William Herschel's thermometer past the rainbow & Ritter's ultraviolet
- **Familiar thing:** the warmth of sunlight on your face, a thermal camera, or sunscreen blocking ultraviolet rays.
- **Person & room:** **William Herschel** (1738–1822) and his sister **Caroline Herschel** in a darkened room in Slough, England, on **11 February 1800**, with a glass prism in a window shutter casting a rainbow across mercury thermometers; and 24-year-old **Johann Wilhelm Ritter** (1776–1810) in Jena, Germany, in **February 1801**.
- **Date & twist:** Herschel wasn't hunting for invisible light — he was testing coloured glass filters to protect his eyes while observing sunspots. On **11 February 1800**, he placed thermometers in the red, green and violet bands, and set a control thermometer in the dark shadow *just past the red end* of the rainbow. The thermometer in the dark rose **9°F (5°C) above room temperature in 16 minutes** — hotter than any visible colour — proving the Sun shines in invisible **infrared** light. One year later (**February 1801**), Ritter reasoned there must be an invisible opposite beyond violet, placed paper soaked in white **silver chloride** in the dark past violet, and watched it turn jet black faster than anywhere in the visible spectrum — discovering **ultraviolet**!

### 4. Penicillin & Antibiotics: Fleming's mouldy dish & the Oxford bedpan factory
- **Familiar thing:** a course of antibiotic pills that cures a throat or wound infection in days.
- **Person & room:** **Alexander Fleming** (1881–1955) in his cluttered turret lab at St Mary's Hospital, Paddington, on **3 September 1928**; then **Howard Florey** (1898–1968), **Ernst Chain** (1906–1979) and **Norman Heatley** (1911–2004) in the Dunn School of Pathology at Oxford (**May 1940 – February 1941**) and **Mary Hunt** at the USDA lab in Peoria, Illinois (**1943**).
- **Date & twist:** Everyone knows Fleming returned from holiday on **3 September 1928** and saw a stray blob of *Penicillium notatum* mould dissolving *Staphylococcus* bacteria on an unwashed Petri dish (`"That's funny"`). The twist: Fleming couldn't purify the unstable broth and **gave up on penicillin as a medicine by 1931**! It sat on the shelf for a decade until **Ernst Chain**, **Howard Florey** and **Norman Heatley** in wartime Oxford grew gallons of mould in **ceramic hospital bedpans** during the Blitz, injected **8 mice on 25 May 1940**, treated Oxford policeman **Albert Alexander** in **February 1941** (even recycling penicillin from his urine until the supply ran out!), and smeared spores into their own coat linings in case Britain was invaded — before laboratory aide **Mary Hunt** ("Moldy Mary") brought in a rotting **cantaloupe melon from a Peoria grocery market in 1943** whose golden mould (*Penicillium chrysogenum*) boosted yields **200-fold** in time for D-Day!

### 5. Electromagnetism: Ørsted's twitching compass & Michael Faraday's cup of mercury
- **Familiar thing:** every electric motor in your phone, fan and train, and every power-station generator on Earth.
- **Person & room:** **Hans Christian Ørsted** (1777–1851) in a Copenhagen lecture theatre on **21 April 1820**, and former bookbinder's apprentice **Michael Faraday** (1791–1867) in the basement laboratory of the Royal Institution in London on **3–4 September 1821** and **29 August 1831**.
- **Date & twist:** On **21 April 1820**, Ørsted connected a battery during a lecture and watched a compass needle twitch sideways. In London, **Michael Faraday** — who left school at 13 and knew almost no algebra — suspended a wire over a bar magnet fixed with wax in a cup of liquid mercury on **3–4 September 1821**, saw the wire spin continuously in circles (the first electric motor), and danced around the table shouting *"There they go! There they go!"* His famous mentor **Sir Humphry Davy** grew so jealous that he accused Faraday of plagiarism and cast the **sole blackball vote against** Faraday's election to the Royal Society in **1824** — only for Faraday, ten years later (**29 August 1831**), to push a magnet through a coil of wire and invent the electric generator!
