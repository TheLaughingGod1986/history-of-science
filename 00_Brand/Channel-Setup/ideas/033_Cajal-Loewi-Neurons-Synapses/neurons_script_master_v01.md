Even after Robert Hooke and Robert Remak proved that every muscle, bone, and organ in your body is built of individual cells, nineteenth-century anatomists insisted the brain was the one grand exception.

Why? Because when you slice brain tissue and dip it in ordinary dye, every single nerve fibre stains at once! Under the microscope, all you see is an impenetrable, solid tangle — like a bowl of millions of microscopic wires melted together.

So leading anatomists, including Italian physician Camillo Golgi, championed the **Reticular Theory**. They argued that nerve fibres fused together into a single unbroken net, like a city's water pipes soldered into one continuous grid.

Then, in eighteen seventy-three, money troubles forced thirty-year-old Camillo Golgi to take a post as chief doctor at a quiet hospital for the incurably ill in Abbiategrasso, near Milan. Refusing to give up research, Golgi set up a tiny laboratory in his apartment kitchen and worked by candlelight.

On the second of August eighteen seventy-three, Golgi announced a miraculous breakthrough he called ***la reazione nera*** — *"the black reaction"*. After hardening brain slices in potassium dichromate, he dropped them into silver nitrate.

Suddenly, for reasons chemists still debate today, black crystals of silver chromate formed inside only one to five percent of the brain cells at random — leaving the other ninety-five percent completely transparent! Instead of a solid black smudge, single nerve cells stood out from root to tip like dark trees in a sunlit clearing!

Yet why did Golgi himself still look through his microscope and swear all those nerve fibres were fused together — until a thirty-five-year-old Spanish artist saw the same stain in eighteen eighty-seven and proved the exact opposite?

Growing up in northern Spain, Santiago Ramón y Cajal wanted only to be an artist, until his surgeon father dragged him to sketch bones in a graveyard and steered him into medicine.

In eighteen eighty-seven in Madrid, a colleague showed thirty-five-year-old Cajal a brain slice stained with Golgi's black silver method. Cajal wrote that he couldn't tear his eyes from the eyepiece: the black threads looked like a sketch in Chinese ink on transparent Japanese paper!

Back at his kitchen table in Barcelona, however, Cajal asked a brilliant question: what if Golgi's stain still looked tangled in adult brains because adult nerve cells are wrapped in thick fatty insulation and overgrown like an ancient jungle? If the full-grown forest is impenetrable, why not study the young saplings?

In May eighteen eighty-eight, Cajal stained the brains of chick embryos and newborn mammals before that insulation grew in. Immediately, the truth jumped out! The nerve fibres wrapped closely around neighbouring cells like ivy around a tree trunk, but every single branch ended freely — they never fused!

Even better, Cajal deduced the **Law of Dynamic Polarization**: signals flow through each nerve cell on a one-way street — arriving at the bushy **dendrites**, passing through the cell body, and racing out down the single long **axon**! In eighteen ninety-one, Wilhelm Waldeyer coined the word **neuron** for Cajal's independent cell, and in eighteen ninety-seven, English physiologist Charles Scott Sherrington named the tiny junction between two cells the **synapse**.

Yet when Golgi and Cajal shared the Nobel Prize in Stockholm in December nineteen hundred and six, Golgi refused to back down! On the eleventh of December, Golgi used his Nobel speech to attack Cajal's Neuron Doctrine to his face! The next day, on the twelfth of December, Cajal took the same stage with giant charts of his drawings to defend separate neurons — a bitter clash finally settled in nineteen fifty-four when the electron microscope photographed the twenty-nanometre gap directly!

Cajal had proved your brain cells are separate islands. Case closed? Not quite. If neurons never touch, how does a signal cross the gap from one cell to the next — by an electric spark, or by a chemical soup?

For twenty years after Sherrington named the synapse, scientists fought what became known as **the War of the Soups and the Sparks**. Electrical physiologists — the Sparks — insisted the electrical nerve pulse simply jumped across the gap like a spark across a spark plug. Pharmacologists — the Soups, led by Henry Hallett Dale in Britain and Otto Loewi in Austria, who first wondered about chemical messengers back in nineteen hundred and three — suspected nerve endings squirted out a tiny drop of chemical, but nobody knew how to prove it!

Then came Easter weekend in March nineteen twenty-one in Graz, Austria. On Easter Saturday night, forty-seven-year-old Otto Loewi fell asleep reading a novel — and woke in the pitch dark with a sudden flash of inspiration! Grabbing a pencil and a scrap of paper, he scribbled down an experiment and went back to sleep.

At six in the morning on Easter Sunday, Loewi woke up thrilled, reached for the paper — and to his horror, couldn't read a single word of his own midnight scrawl! He spent all day pacing in agony trying to remember what he had dreamed.

That night, at three in the morning, he woke from the exact same dream! Refusing to trust his handwriting twice, Loewi got dressed at three a.m. and rushed straight to his laboratory!

He placed two beating frog hearts into separate glass cups filled with clear salt water. Heart Number One still had its **vagus nerve** attached; Heart Number Two had every nerve stripped away.

First, Loewi electrically stimulated the vagus nerve of Heart Number One for a few minutes until its heartbeat slowed right down. Then he sucked up a few drops of the salt water around Heart Number One with a glass pipette and dripped it onto nerve-free Heart Number Two!

Instantly, Heart Number Two slowed down too — and when he stimulated the first heart's accelerator nerve and transferred the fluid, the second heart sped up! The nerve hadn't jumped the gap with an electrical spark; it had released an invisible chemical messenger into the water! Loewi called it ***Vagusstoff*** — which he and Henry Dale proved was **acetylcholine**, earning them the nineteen thirty-six Nobel Prize!

So how do electrical sparks and chemical puffs team up inside your head every millisecond as you listen to this sentence?

People often say the human brain runs on pure electricity like a copper computer chip — or repeat the old myth that you only use ten percent of your brain! In truth, you use virtually all of your brain every day, and whenever you feel a sudden emotion or recall a memory, both the Sparks and the Soups are working together!

Inside each neuron, an electrical wave races down the long fibre at up to a hundred metres per second. Then, at the tip, that electrical spark triggers tiny bubbles to dump thousands of chemical molecules — acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin — across the twenty-nanometre synaptic gap in less than a thousandth of a second, sparking a brand-new electrical wave in the next cell!

And because your eighty-six billion firing neurons burn a fifth of all your body's energy, every breath you exhale releases carbon dioxide gas into the sky. Thirty-two years before Cajal stained his first chick brain, in August eighteen fifty-six in New York, a pioneering scientist named Eunice Newton Foote sealed carbon dioxide inside a glass cylinder in the summer sun — launching a chain of discoveries with John Tyndall in eighteen fifty-nine and Svante Arrhenius in eighteen ninety-six that revealed why four molecules in ten thousand keep Earth from freezing into a solid ball of ice!

A silver stain in an Italian kitchen, an artist's pen in Barcelona, and two frog hearts in the middle of the night. Together, they revealed the microscopic canyons where every human thought is born.
