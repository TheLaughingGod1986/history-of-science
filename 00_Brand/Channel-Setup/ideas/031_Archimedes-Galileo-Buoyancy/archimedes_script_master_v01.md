## PART 02: The King's Wreath and Vitruvius's Bathtub Myth (1:25–3:05)

Our only ancient story of Archimedes running naked from the bath shouting *"Eureka!"* does not come from Archimedes' own books. Instead, it was written down around twenty-five BC by a Roman architect named Vitruvius — more than two hundred years after Archimedes died!

According to Vitruvius, around two hundred and fifty BC, King Hiero the Second of Syracuse gave a royal craftsman a weighed bar of pure gold to fashion a sacred laurel wreath for the gods. When delivered, the crown weighed the exact right amount. However, a whistleblower reported that the smith had stolen part of the gold and mixed in an equal weight of cheaper silver.

Why did that matter physically? Because pure gold — at nineteen point three grams per cubic centimetre — is nearly twice as dense as silver, at ten point five grams per cubic centimetre! Atom for atom, gold packs almost twice as much mass into the same space as silver.

So what if a thief replaces three hundred grams of gold with three hundred grams of silver inside a one-kilogram crown? On a dry scale, the weight stays one thousand grams. But because silver is puffier, the fake crown must take up more space — sixty-four point eight cubic centimetres instead of fifty-one point eight!

Stepping into a full public bath in Syracuse, Archimedes noticed what you feel every time you climb into a tub: as his body sank in, water spilled over the rim, and his own limbs felt lighter underwater. Shouting ***"Eureka! Eureka!"*** — meaning *"I have found it!"* — he raced home.

Vitruvius assumed Archimedes tested the crown by dipping pure gold, pure silver, and the crown one by one into a pot filled to the brim and measuring how much water overflowed. That story sounded so neat that everyone repeated it for sixteen centuries — until in fifteen eighty-six, a broke twenty-two-year-old medical dropout in Florence named Galileo Galilei did the arithmetic and proved Vitruvius's method could never have worked! In fifteen eighty-six — three years before he became a professor at Pisa — twenty-two-year-old Galileo Galilei wrote his very first scientific treatise: a short manuscript called ***La Bilancetta***, or *"The Little Balance"*.

Galileo pointed out the fatal flaw in Vitruvius's splash-and-spill story. A golden wreath wide enough for a king's head needs a water basin at least thirty centimetres across. Lowering a one-kilogram block of pure gold into that wide bowl raises the water level by zero point seven three millimetres. Lowering a one-kilogram crown mixed with thirty percent silver raises it by zero point nine two millimetres — a difference of less than one fifth of a millimetre!

Worse still, water surface tension makes water cling to the rim of a pot in a curved bulge two to three millimetres high before a single drop spills over — ten times bigger than the difference you are trying to measure! And when you lift the wet, leafy wreath out, water droplets cling to the golden leaves and ruin the test.

Instead, Galileo had read Archimedes' own surviving treatise, ***On Floating Bodies***. There, in Proposition Seven, Archimedes proved **Archimedes' Principle**: when any object is submerged in water, the water pushes *upward* on it with a buoyant force equal to the exact weight of the water it displaces — one gram of upward lift for every cubic centimetre of volume!

So Archimedes didn't measure spilled water at all! He hung the one-kilogram crown from one side of a beam scale and one kilogram of pure gold from the other. In the air, they balanced dead level.

Then he dunked both hanging pans underwater! Suddenly, because the silver-mixed crown was thirteen cubic centimetres bulkier, it pushed aside thirteen extra grams of water — so the water pushed *up* on the fake crown with thirteen extra grams of buoyant force! Now the pure gold sank and the fake crown rose before the King's eyes without spilling a single drop! And by winding fine brass wire along the scale arm and counting the coils with his thumbnail, Galileo measured the exact fraction of stolen gold by ear.

Archimedes' law caught a thief in a bowl of water. Case closed? Wrong. How did Archimedes use that exact same law to float an ancient palace ship weighing four thousand tonnes — and how was his book nearly erased forever by a thirteenth-century monk?

Once Archimedes proved that a floating object sinks only until the weight of the water it pushes aside equals its own weight, he realised that any ship — no matter how heavy — will float if its hollow hull is wide enough to displace that weight of water!

Around two hundred and forty BC, King Hiero put that law to the test by commissioning the ***Syracusia*** — the *Titanic* of the ancient world! Supervised by Archimedes, she stretched over a hundred metres long and weighed up to four thousand tonnes, carrying mosaic floors, flower gardens, a gymnasium, a library, a marble temple, eight watchtowers, a stone-hurling catapult, and a bilge hull kept dry by one man turning **Archimedes' screw**!

After a Roman soldier killed the seventy-five-year-old Archimedes during the fall of Syracuse in two hundred and twelve BC, how did his Greek treatise *On Floating Bodies* — and his lost book *The Method*, where he weighed geometric slices two thousand years before calculus — survive to reach us?

By a thread! In April twelve twenty-nine, a monk named Johannes Myronas needed parchment for an Easter prayer book. Taking the only surviving tenth-century Greek manuscript of Archimedes' works, he scraped the ink off with pumice stone, washed it with weak acid, folded the pages in half sideways, and wrote medieval prayers right over Archimedes' diagrams!

In nineteen hundred and six, Danish scholar Johan Ludvig Heiberg spotted the ghostly geometric figures under a magnifying glass in Constantinople — before the book vanished again and had four pages painted over with forged gold-leaf icons! Finally, in May two thousand and five, physicists placed the prayer book inside the synchrotron particle accelerator at Stanford. Firing a hair-thin X-ray beam across the parchment, they made the microscopic traces of iron left in Archimedes' ancient ink glow right through the medieval prayers — recovering his erased Greek proofs line by line!

So why does a heavy rock weigh less underwater even when it sinks all the way to the bottom — and does water pressure push downward or upward?

Many people think buoyancy only works on things that float at the surface, or that deep water crushes a sinking rock downward faster. Is that true?

Not at all! Buoyancy comes from one simple rule: water pressure increases with depth. Because the bottom of any submerged object sits slightly deeper than the top, the water underneath always pushes *upward* harder than the water on top pushes downward! Whether it is a one-kilogram crown on Galileo's scale, a hundred-thousand-tonne steel ship on the sea, or a granite continent floating on Earth's mantle, every object is lifted by the exact weight of the fluid it pushes aside.

Galileo tied a golden crown to a thread and dipped it into a jar of water to test what was hidden inside without cutting it open. Two hundred and thirty-nine years later, in May eighteen twenty-five on a remote island in Michigan, US Army surgeon William Beaumont tied a piece of beef to a silk string and lowered it through a healed musket-shot keyhole straight into a living man's stomach — launching a medical detective story that ended in July nineteen eighty-four when Australian doctor Barry Marshall drank a glass beaker teeming with a billion bacteria to prove what really causes stomach ulcers!

A brimming bath in Syracuse, a brass wire wound by twenty-two-year-old Galileo, and an X-ray beam reading a medieval prayer book. Together, they showed why ships float and how a single gram of water can unmask a royal lie.
