### PART 02: The King's Wreath and Vitruvius's Bathtub Myth (1:25–3:05)
- **Cold open (a Roman writer two centuries after the fact):** **Syracuse, Sicily, ~250 BC & Rome, ~25 BC**. Our only ancient account of Archimedes running naked from the bath shouting *"Eureka!"* comes not from Archimedes' own writings, but from Roman architect **Vitruvius** (~80–15 BC), writing in Book IX of *De Architectura* around **25 BC** — **more than two hundred years after Archimedes' death**!
- According to Vitruvius, **King Hiero II of Syracuse** (~308–215 BC) gave a royal craftsman a weighed bar of pure gold to fashion a sacred votive wreath crown for the immortal gods.
- When delivered, the crown had the exact right weight. Yet a whistleblower reported that the smith had stolen part of the gold and alloyed the crown with an equal weight of cheaper **silver**.
- Why did that matter physically? Because **pure gold (`density 19.32 g/cm³`)** is nearly **twice as dense as silver (`density 10.49 g/cm³`)**: atom for atom (`002`, `004`), gold atoms (`atomic mass 197`) pack almost twice as much mass into the same space as silver atoms (`atomic mass 108`)!
- So if you replace 300 grams of gold with 300 grams of silver inside a 1,000-gram crown, the weight on a dry scale stays **1,000 grams**, but the crown must take up **more volume (`64.8 cm³` instead of `51.8 cm³`)**!
- Stepping into a full public bath in Syracuse, **Archimedes** (~287–212 BC) noticed two things simultaneously: as his body sank in, water spilled over the edge **and** his own body felt lighter, lifted up by the water. Shouting ***"Eureka! Eureka!"* ("I have found it!")**, he raced home.
- Vitruvius assumed Archimedes measured the crown by dipping pure gold, pure silver, and the crown one by one into a vessel filled to the brim and **measuring how much water overflowed**.
- *Markers:* `· WHO: Vitruvius (~80–15 BC) — Roman architect and engineer · WHEN: ~250 BC · WHEN: ~25 BC · FACT: Written 200 years later · DATA: inked atomic density and volume comparison on period paper showing 1,000 g of pure gold (density 19.32 g/cm³, volume 51.76 cm³) vs 1,000 g of a 70% gold / 30% silver crown (silver density 10.49 g/cm³, total volume 64.83 cm³) — identical 1,000 g weight in air, 13.07 cm³ extra volume · SCALE: pure gold is almost twice as dense as silver — so hiding 30% silver inside a one-kilogram crown swells its volume by 13 cubic centimetres, less than a single tablespoon`
- **Ends on:** Vitruvius's overflowing-water story sounded so neat that everyone repeated it for sixteen centuries. Until in **1586**, a broke twenty-two-year-old medical dropout in Florence named **Galileo Galilei** did the arithmetic — and proved Vitruvius's method could never have worked! Why?

### PART 03: Twenty-Two-Year-Old Galileo and *The Little Balance* (3:05–4:50)
- **Cold open:** **Florence, Italy, 1586**. Three years before he became a professor at Pisa (`009`), 22-year-old **Galileo Galilei** (1564–1642) wrote his very first scientific treatise: a short manuscript titled ***La Bilancetta* ("The Little Balance")**.
- Galileo pointed out the glaring flaw in Vitruvius's splash-and-spill story:
  - A ceremonial golden wreath wide enough for a king's head requires a water basin at least **`30 centimetres` (`12 inches`) in diameter** (`area = π × 15² = 707 cm²`).
  - Lowering a `1,000-gram` pure gold block (`51.8 cm³`) into that basin raises the water level by **`0.73 millimetres`**. Lowering a `1,000-gram` crown mixed with 30% silver (`64.8 cm³`) raises it by **`0.92 millimetres`** — a difference of **just `0.18 millimetres`**!
  - Even worse, **water surface tension** makes water cling to the rim of a bronze or clay pot in a curved bulge (**meniscus**) up to **`2 to 3 millimetres` high** before a single drop spills over — and when you pull the wet, leafy wreath out to refill the pot, water droplets cling to the golden leaves and ruin the measurement!
- **How Archimedes really did it — Proposition 7 of *On Floating Bodies*:** Galileo had read Archimedes' own brilliant treatise ***On Floating Bodies* (*Peri ton ochoumenon*)**, where Archimedes proved **Archimedes' Principle**:
  - When any object is submerged in water, the water pushes **upward** on it with a **buoyant force exactly equal to the weight of the water it displaces** (`1 gram of upward lift for every 1 cubic centimetre of volume`)!
  - So Archimedes didn't measure spilled water at all! He hung the **1,000 g crown** from one end of a precision beam balance and **1,000 g of pure gold** from the other end. In the air, the beam sat **perfectly horizontal**.
  - Then he **submerged both hanging pans into a tub of water**:
    - The `51.8 cm³` block of pure gold displaced `51.8 g` of water, so it weighed **`948.2 g` underwater**;
    - The bulkier `64.8 cm³` silver-mixed crown displaced `64.8 g` of water, so the water pushed up on it with **13 grams more buoyant force**, making it weigh only **`935.2 g` underwater**!
    - The instant they touched the water, the pure gold sank and the fake crown rose — exposing the thief before the King's eyes without spilling or measuring a single drop!
- **Galileo's 1586 wire-wrapped balance:** To make the measurement even more precise, Galileo wrapped **fine brass wire** tightly along the balance arm and slid the counterweight along the coils, scraping his fingernail across the wire to **count the exact number of turns by ear** and read off the exact percentage of silver mixed into the gold!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: 1586 · FACT: Meniscus hides 0.18 mm rise · DATA: inked schematic of Archimedes' Proposition 7 underwater balance and Galileo's 1586 wire-wrapped La Bilancetta showing 1,000 g gold (51.8 cm³ → −51.8 g buoyancy = 948.2 g apparent weight in water) vs 1,000 g 70/30 crown (64.8 cm³ → −64.8 g buoyancy = 935.2 g in water), tipping by 13.1 g · SCALE: instead of trying to spot a 0.18-millimetre water rise swamped by 3 millimetres of surface tension, dunking the balance turns that tiny volume difference into a 13-gram weight difference any scale can feel`
- **Ends on:** Archimedes' law of buoyancy caught a royal thief in a bowl of water. Case closed? **Wrong.** How did Archimedes use that exact same equation to float an ancient palace ship weighing **four thousand tonnes** — and how was his masterpiece nearly erased from history forever by a thirteenth-century monk?

### PART 04: The 4,000-Tonne Super-Ship and the Erased Prayer Book — Midpoint Twist (4:50–6:40)
- **Cold open ("Case closed? Wrong."):** **Syracuse Harbour, ~240 BC; Jerusalem, April 1229; Constantinople, 1906; & Stanford, California, May 2005**. Once Archimedes proved in Book I of *On Floating Bodies* that **a floating object sinks only until the weight of the water it displaces equals the total weight of the object**, he realised that *any* material — no matter how heavy — will float if you shape it into a hollow hull wide enough to displace its weight in water!
- **The *Syracusia* (~240 BC) — the *Titanic* of the ancient world:** King Hiero II put Archimedes' law to the ultimate test by commissioning the ***Syracusia*** (~240 BC), supervised by Archimedes and shipwright Archias of Corinth:
  - Nearly **110 metres long** and carrying **~1,600 to 4,000 tonnes** of cargo and passengers, it held **three decks, mosaic floors telling the *Iliad*, a gymnasium, a library, flower-lined garden promenades, a marble temple to Aphrodite, eight defensive watchtowers, a stone-hurling catapult built by Archimedes**, and a bilge hull kept dry by a single man turning **Archimedes' revolving water-screw**!
  - Because only two harbours in the Mediterranean were deep enough to berth her, Hiero sailed her loaded with grain to **Alexandria, Egypt**, as a gift to Pharaoh **Ptolemy III**!
- **The erased parchment & the 2005 X-ray beam (1229 → 1906 → 2005–2008):** After a Roman soldier killed the 75-year-old Archimedes during the fall of Syracuse in **212 BC**, how did the Greek text of *On Floating Bodies* (and his secret treatise *The Method*, where he weighed geometric slices on an imaginary balance to calculate volumes two millennia before calculus!) survive?
  - By the tenth century AD (`~975 AD`), a scribe in **Constantinople** copied Archimedes' treatises onto goatskin parchment.
  - Then, in **April 1229**, a Christian monk named **Johannes Myronas** needed parchment for an Easter prayer book (*Euchologion*): he **scraped the Archimedes parchment with a pumice stone, washed it with weak acid, cut the pages in half, rotated them 90 degrees, and wrote medieval prayers right over Archimedes' diagrams** (**the Archimedes Palimpsest**)!
  - In **1906**, Danish scholar **Johan Ludvig Heiberg** (1854–1928) spotted the faint geometric spirals under a magnifying glass in Constantinople and photographed what he could — before the book vanished for decades and had four pages painted over with fake gold-leaf portraits by a twentieth-century forger!
  - Finally, in **May 2005 – August 2008**, physicists and imaging scientists brought the palimpsest to the **Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource**: firing a hair-thin **X-ray fluorescence beam (`003`)** across the parchment, they caused the microscopic traces of **iron (`Fe`)** left in Archimedes' tenth-century oak-gall ink to glow brightly right through the medieval prayers and modern forgeries — recovering the original Greek text of *On Floating Bodies*!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: ~240 BC · WHEN: 212 BC · WHEN: April 1229 · WHO: Johan Ludvig Heiberg (1854–1928) — Danish philologist and historian · WHEN: 1906 · WHEN: May 2005 · FACT: Scraped into a prayer book · DATA: inked diagram on period paper showing (1) buoyancy of the 4,000-tonne ship Syracusia displacing 4,000 m³ of seawater vs a sinking solid metal bolt, and (2) Stanford's 2005–2008 synchrotron X-ray fluorescence beam exciting iron atoms in Archimedes' erased 10th-century ink beneath Johannes Myronas's April 1229 prayer text · SCALE: a 4,000-tonne palace ship floats effortlessly because its hollow hull pushes aside 4,000 tonnes of seawater — while a solid one-ounce bolt sinks because it pushes aside only a thimbleful`
- **Ends on:** Archimedes' law explains why a four-thousand-tonne wooden super-ship and a hundred-thousand-tonne modern steel freighter float on the sea. So why does an object weigh less underwater even when it sinks all the way to the bottom — and does water pressure push downward or upward?

### PART 05: Why Heavy Steel Ships Float & the Keyhole Into a Living Stomach (6:40–8:15)
- **Cold open (popular misconception debunked):** Many people think buoyancy only works on things that float at the surface, or that deep water crushes a sinking rock downward faster. Is that true?
- **No:** Buoyancy comes from a simple fact of fluid pressure (`CrashCourse Fluids at Rest`, `b5SqYuWT4-4`): **water pressure increases with depth**! Because the bottom of any submerged object sits slightly deeper than the top, the water underneath pushes **upward harder than the water on top pushes downward** — and that net upward difference equals the exact weight of the displaced fluid (`F_b = ρ_fluid × V × g`), whether the object is a floating steel ship, a rising helium balloon in air, a continent floating on Earth's mantle (`030`), or a golden crown hanging on a scale at the bottom of a tank!
- **After-act hand-off (7:45–8:15):**
  - Galileo tied a golden crown to a thread and dipped it into a jar of water to test what was hidden inside without cutting it open.
  - Two hundred and thirty-nine years later, in **May 1825** on a remote island in Michigan, US Army surgeon **William Beaumont** tied a piece of beef to a **silk thread** and lowered it through a healed **musket-shot keyhole straight into a living man's stomach** — launching a 160-year medical detective story that ended in **July 1984** when Australian doctor **Barry Marshall** drank a glass beaker teeming with **a billion bacteria** to prove what really causes stomach ulcers (`032` candidate *Stomach Acid & the Ulcer Myth: William Beaumont & Barry Marshall*)!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: May 1825 · WHEN: July 1984 · DATA: animated timeline building ~250 BC (Archimedes discovers the law of buoyancy in Syracuse, unmasks the adulterated golden crown, and writes On Floating Bodies) · ~240 BC (Archimedes designs the 4,000-tonne super-ship Syracusia) · ~25 BC (Vitruvius records the Eureka bathtub story in De Architectura) · April 1229 (scribe Johannes Myronas scrapes Archimedes' parchment into a prayer book) · 1586 (22-year-old Galileo Galilei writes La Bilancetta, proving Archimedes used an underwater hydrostatic balance) · 1906 & May 2005–2008 (Johan Ludvig Heiberg rediscovers the Archimedes Palimpsest; Stanford synchrotron X-rays recover the erased Greek text) · SCALE: whether it is a 1-kilogram crown in a bowl, a 100,000-tonne steel ship in the ocean, or a continent floating on the mantle, every submerged object is lifted by the exact weight of the fluid it pushes aside · FACT: Deeper water pushes up harder`
