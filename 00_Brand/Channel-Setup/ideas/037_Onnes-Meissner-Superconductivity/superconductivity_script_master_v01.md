You can heat matter as hot as you like — millions of degrees inside a star — so why is there a hard floor to how cold something can get?

Because heat is simply the jiggling motion of atoms! In seventeen-oh-two, French physicist Guillaume Amontons noticed that air pressure in a sealed glass bulb drops steadily as it cools, pointing toward a temperature where pressure hits zero; and in eighteen forty-eight, William Thomson — Lord Kelvin — calculated the exact bottom of the scale: **Absolute Zero**, minus two hundred and seventy-three point one five degrees Celsius, where thermal motion stops!

Across Europe, a fierce race erupted to cool and liquefy every gas on Earth. In eighteen ninety-eight at the Royal Institution in London, Scottish chemist Sir James Dewar — who invented the silvered vacuum **Dewar flask**, the ancestor of every Thermos bottle in your kitchen — liquefied hydrogen at twenty point four Kelvin!

Only one gas on Earth refused to turn into a liquid: **helium**. At Leiden University in the Netherlands, Heike Kamerlingh Onnes built an industrial-scale cooling factory with a simple motto on the wall: *"Door meten tot weten"* — *"Through measurement to knowledge."*

Starting before dawn at five forty-five in the morning on the tenth of July nineteen-oh-eight, Onnes used liquid air and twenty litres of liquid hydrogen to chill pure helium gas through four cascading cooling stages. Fourteen hours later, at half past seven that evening, he peered into the central glass flask and saw a shimmering teacupful — sixty millilitres — of **liquid helium** at four point two two Kelvin! By pumping the vapour away, he reached one point zero four Kelvin — making a room in Holland more than twice as cold as deep outer space!

Now that Kamerlingh Onnes controlled the coldest liquid in the universe, what happened on the eighth of April nineteen eleven when he dipped a frozen wire into it?

In nineteen eleven, physicists were split over what electricity would do near Absolute Zero. Lord Kelvin had predicted in nineteen-oh-two that electrons would freeze solid onto their atoms, causing electrical resistance to shoot up to infinity! Others thought impurities in the metal would leave a flat floor of resistance.

When Kamerlingh Onnes tested gold and platinum wires in liquid helium, trace impurities muddied the results. Then he had a brilliant idea: what is the only metal on Earth that sits liquid at room temperature, so you can purify it to near-perfection by distilling it over and over in glass? **Mercury**!

On the eighth of April nineteen eleven, Onnes, Cornelis Dorsman, and research assistant Gilles Holst froze a hair-thin glass capillary of quadruple-distilled mercury in liquid helium. Down in a vibration-free basement room, Holst watched the mirror galvanometer.

At four point three Kelvin, the mercury's resistance fell steadily. Then, at four o'clock in the afternoon, as the helium bath crossed **four point two zero Kelvin**, the galvanometer needle dropped dead to zero!

At first, they assumed a wire had touched and short-circuited the apparatus! They warmed the helium bath slightly: the resistance popped right back. They cooled it below four point two Kelvin: the resistance vanished to less than a ten-millionth of an ohm! Onnes wrote three Dutch words in his blue notebook: ***"Kwik nagenoeg nul"*** — *"Mercury practically zero"* — and in nineteen thirteen named this new phenomenon **superconductivity**!

In nineteen fourteen, Onnes started an electric current flowing inside a closed ring of superconducting lead wire, then **unplugged the battery**. Hour after hour, a compass needle beside the flask never budged — and modern measurements prove a current inside a superconducting ring will keep flowing without a battery for more than a hundred thousand years!

For twenty-two years, physicists thought a superconductor was simply a wire with zero resistance. Case closed? Not yet. If a superconductor were merely an ordinary wire with zero resistance, then by Michael Faraday's laws of electromagnetism, a stationary magnetic field already inside a warm metal cylinder should stay trapped inside when you cool it down.

In October nineteen thirty-three in Berlin, German physicists Walther Meissner and Robert Ochsenfeld tested that assumption on cylinders of tin and lead — and got the shock of their lives! The instant the metal crossed into superconductivity, it **actively kicked the entire magnetic field out of its interior**!

That active magnetic expulsion — **the Meissner Effect** — proved superconductivity is a brand-new quantum state of matter! Because a superconductor refuses to let magnetic field lines pass freely through it, a magnet placed over a superconductor sits on an invisible cushion of repelled magnetic lines, **floating and locked in mid-air**, first photographed in nineteen forty-five!

Yet why did electrons stop colliding inside the metal? Einstein, Bohr, Heisenberg, and Feynman all tried to solve it — and for forty-six years, every one of them failed!

Finally, in February nineteen fifty-seven, John Bardeen — who had just won the Nobel Prize for co-inventing the silicon transistor — teamed up with Leon Cooper and twenty-five-year-old student Robert Schrieffer to publish **BCS Theory**, earning Bardeen a second Nobel Prize in nineteen seventy-two and making him the only person in history to win two Nobel Prizes in Physics!

Their secret? In extreme cold, as one negative electron glides past positive metal ions, it pulls those positive ions slightly inward — creating a tiny ripple of positive charge that attracts a second electron into a bound **Cooper pair**, like two skaters gliding in lockstep as a single quantum wave that cannot scatter off individual atoms!

So why can a brittle ceramic coffee-mug material superconduct at temperatures warmer than liquid nitrogen — and how do superconductors see inside your body in hospital MRI scanners today?

Many people assume only shiny pure metals can conduct electricity without resistance, or that every superconductor needs rare liquid helium near Absolute Zero. Is that true?

Not anymore! In January nineteen eighty-six at IBM Zurich, Georg Bednorz and Alex Müller tested a brittle copper-oxide ceramic — normally an insulator like a coffee mug — and watched its resistance vanish at thirty-five Kelvin! Within a year, in February nineteen eighty-seven, Maw-Kuen Wu and Paul Chu pushed a ceramic called **YBCO** to **ninety-three Kelvin** — sixteen degrees warmer than the **seventy-seven Kelvin** boiling point of liquid nitrogen, a coolant cheaper than milk! Today, superconducting coils generate the powerful magnetic fields inside forty thousand hospital **MRI scanners** and levitate Japanese maglev trains at six hundred and three kilometres an hour!

And superconductivity happens when sound-like waves of vibration inside a crystal lock electrons into step. What happens when sound waves travel through the air all around you — and why does the pitch of an ambulance siren or a speeding train drop in a sudden *"NEEEE-owww"* the instant it passes you? On the twenty-fifth of May eighteen forty-two in Prague, Austrian physicist Christian Doppler predicted that wave shift — prompting a skeptical Dutch scientist named Christophorus Buys Ballot on the third of June eighteen forty-five to hire a steam locomotive and a flatbed car full of brass trumpet players to prove Doppler wrong, before Ernst Mach photographed a bullet breaking the **sound barrier** in eighteen eighty-seven!
