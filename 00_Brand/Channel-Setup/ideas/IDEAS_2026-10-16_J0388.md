### 1. DNA & Photo 51: Rosalind Franklin's 100-hour X-ray & the race for the double helix (recommended for 016)
- **Familiar thing:** a cheek-swab DNA test, or the **two metres** of microscopic genetic thread coiled inside every single cell of your body (**60 billion kilometres** across all 30 trillion cells — enough to stretch from Earth to Pluto and back five times).
- **Person & room:** Physical chemist **Rosalind Franklin** (1920–1958) and PhD student **Raymond Gosling** (1926–2015) in a damp basement X-ray diffraction laboratory at **King's College London** on **2–6 May 1952**, cut back-and-forth against **James Watson** (born 1928) and **Francis Crick** (1916–2004) bolting together tin-and-cardboard molecular models at the **Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge** (**November 1951 – February 1953**), **Maurice Wilkins** (1916–2004) at King's, and **Linus Pauling** (1901–1994) at Caltech.
- **Date & proof:** After **Oswald Avery** (1944) showed that DNA — not protein — carries heredity, the world's sharpest scientists raced to find its 3D shape. At King's College London, **Rosalind Franklin** discovered why earlier X-ray images of DNA were a blurry mess: DNA shifts between two distinct forms depending on humidity — a dry crystalline **A-form** (at 75% relative humidity) and a wet paracrystalline **B-form** (at 92% relative humidity) when water molecules cling to its outer phosphate backbone. Between **2 and 6 May 1952**, Franklin and Gosling bubbled hydrogen gas through a salt solution to hold a single hair-thin fibre of calf-thymus DNA at 92% humidity and beamed X-rays through it for **100 hours** in a microcamera tilted at 68 degrees. The resulting plate — **Photograph 51** — showed a stark black cross (`X`) of diffraction spots with a missing fourth layer line, proving B-DNA is a regular helix **20 ångströms (2 nm) wide** with **10 rungs per turn** repeating every **34 ångströms (3.4 nm)** (`3.4 Å` per rung).
- **Twist (three-part):**
  1. **The inside-out mistakes (Cambridge 1951 & Caltech Jan 1953):** In **November 1951**, Watson and Crick built a humiliatingly wrong **three-stranded inside-out model** with the sugar-phosphate backbones on the inside and the bases sticking out; Franklin took one look on **21 November 1951** and demolished it in minutes because the hydrophilic phosphates *had* to sit on the outside in water, prompting Cavendish chief **Sir Lawrence Bragg** to ban Watson and Crick from DNA model-building. Fourteen months later (**January 1953**), Nobel-bound chemist **Linus Pauling** published the exact same inside-out three-stranded blunder!
  2. **The unapproved hand-off (30 January – mid-February 1953):** On **30 January 1953**, without Franklin's knowledge or permission, **Maurice Wilkins** opened a drawer at King's and showed *Photograph 51* to James Watson (*"The instant I saw the picture my mouth fell open and my pulse began to race"*). Days later, **Max Perutz** handed Crick an unpublished December 1952 Medical Research Council (MRC) report containing Franklin's exact unit-cell crystal measurements ($C2$ monoclinic symmetry) — from which Crick immediately deduced that the two DNA strands run in **opposite directions** (antiparallel). Combined with **Erwin Chargaff's** rule ($A=T, G=C$) and **Jerry Donohue's** keto-tautomer tip on **27–28 February 1953**, Watson and Crick locked the base pairs together.
  3. **The final twist (17 March 1953 & 1962):** Franklin was never "anti-helix": before she even saw Watson and Crick's model, she drafted her own manuscript on **17 March 1953** concluding that DNA is a two-chain coaxial helix with its phosphates on the outside! When the three back-to-back papers appeared in *Nature* on **25 April 1953**, Franklin's *Photo 51* paper was printed third, looking as if it merely confirmed Watson and Crick's model. Franklin died of ovarian cancer on **16 April 1958** at age 37; because Nobel rules barred posthumous awards, Watson, Crick and Wilkins shared the **1962 Nobel Prize** without ever telling her how much their breakthrough owed to her unpublished data.

### 2. Oxygen & the Phlogiston Myth: Joseph Priestley's bell jar, the mouse that refused to die & Antoine Lavoisier's balance
- **Familiar thing:** every breath of air you take (21% oxygen), a burning candle flame, or why rusting iron gets *heavier* when it oxidises.
- **Person & room:** English dissenting minister **Joseph Priestley** (1733–1804) at Bowood House, Wiltshire, on **1 August 1774** (and **8 March 1775**), focusing sunlight through a **12-inch burning lens** onto red mercury calx under a glass vessel inverted over mercury; and **Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier** (1743–1794) and **Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier** (1758–1836) at their Paris dinner table in **October 1774** and in the Paris Arsenal laboratory (**1775–1789**).
- **Date & twist:** For nearly a century, chemists swore that burning things grew lighter because they released an invisible fire-element called **phlogiston**. On **1 August 1774**, Priestley used a 12-inch burning lens to heat red mercury calx (*mercurius calcinatus per se*) trapped under a glass bell jar over mercury. A colourless gas bubbled out in which a candle blazed like fireworks, a **mouse** survived **twice as long** as in ordinary air (**8 March 1775**), and Priestley's own lungs felt *"peculiarly light and easy."* Yet Priestley was so devoted to phlogiston that he named his gas **"dephlogisticated air"** and died in **1804** still denying that oxygen was an element! Meanwhile, after Priestley described his experiment over dinner in Paris in **October 1774**, **Antoine Lavoisier** (assisted by **Marie-Anne Paulze**, who translated Priestley's English papers and drew the laboratory plates) sealed mercury and air inside a glass retort for **12 days**, weighed everything to **1/400th of a grain**, proved that burning and rusting *absorb* a real gas making up **one-fifth of the air** — naming it **oxygen** (**1777**) and proving the **Law of Conservation of Mass** (**1789**) — and tried to claim independent discovery without crediting Priestley, before being sent to the **guillotine** on **8 May 1794** during the Reign of Terror!

### 3. Invisible Light: William Herschel's thermometer past the rainbow & Ritter's ultraviolet
- **Familiar thing:** the warmth of sunlight on your face, a thermal camera, or sunscreen blocking ultraviolet rays.
- **Person & room:** **William Herschel** (1738–1822) and his sister **Caroline Herschel** in a darkened room in Slough, England, on **11 February 1800**, with a glass prism in a window shutter casting a rainbow across mercury thermometers; and 24-year-old **Johann Wilhelm Ritter** (1776–1810) in Jena, Germany, in **February 1801**.
- **Date & twist:** Herschel wasn't hunting for invisible light — he was testing coloured glass filters to protect his eyes while observing sunspots. On **11 February 1800**, he placed thermometers in the red, green and violet bands, and set a control thermometer in the dark shadow *just past the red end* of the rainbow. The thermometer in the dark rose **9°F (5°C) above room temperature in 16 minutes** — hotter than any visible colour — proving the Sun shines in invisible **infrared** light. One year later (**February 1801**), Ritter reasoned there must be an invisible opposite beyond violet, placed paper soaked in white **silver chloride** in the dark past violet, and watched it turn jet black faster than anywhere in the visible spectrum — discovering **ultraviolet**!

### 5. Electromagnetism: Ørsted's twitching compass & Michael Faraday's cup of mercury
- **Familiar thing:** every electric motor in your phone, fan and train, and every power-station generator on Earth.
- **Person & room:** **Hans Christian Ørsted** (1777–1851) in a Copenhagen lecture theatre on **21 April 1820**, and former bookbinder's apprentice **Michael Faraday** (1791–1867) in the basement laboratory of the Royal Institution in London on **3–4 September 1821** and **29 August 1831**.
- **Date & twist:** On **21 April 1820**, Ørsted connected a battery during a lecture and watched a compass needle twitch sideways. In London, **Michael Faraday** — who left school at 13 and knew almost no algebra — suspended a wire over a bar magnet fixed with wax in a cup of liquid mercury on **3–4 September 1821**, saw the wire spin continuously in circles (the first electric motor), and danced around the table shouting *"There they go! There they go!"* His famous mentor **Sir Humphry Davy** grew so jealous that he accused Faraday of plagiarism and cast the **sole blackball vote against** Faraday's election to the Royal Society in **1824** — only for Faraday, ten years later (**29 August 1831**), to push a magnet through a coil of wire and invent the electric generator!
