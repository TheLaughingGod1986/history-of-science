## PART 01: The Cross in the Basement (0:00–1:25). No chapter card.

Why did the sharpest minds of the twentieth century have no idea how life copies itself? And how did a single X-ray photograph — exposed for one hundred hours in a damp London basement — reveal the twisted ladder inside every cell of your body?

[VISUAL MUST: 0:00 — in a dim basement room at King's College London, a narrow beam of X-rays passes through a single glistening fibre of DNA no thicker than a spider's thread inside a brass microcamera; on the square photographic film behind it, a stark black cross of ten stepped spots burns into view as a glowing 3D double helix uncoils beside it (ANSWER IMAGE by 0:03, TITLE QUESTION by 0:07) · WHEN: London, May 1952 · LABEL: 100-HOUR X-RAY]

By the end of this film, you'll see why three famous scientists first built the molecule of life completely inside out, how an unpublished photograph shown from a drawer broke the deadlock in Cambridge, and how a forgotten draft dated the seventeenth of March proved its creator had nearly solved the puzzle on her own.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:12 — split composition: on the left, Rosalind Franklin adjusts a brass collimator lens in London; on the right, James Watson and Francis Crick bolt brass and tin plates onto a six-foot metal stand in Cambridge (PROMISE by 0:15) · LABEL: TWO LABS, ONE SECRET]

Picture yourself in nineteen fifty-one. Biologists know that a chemical called DNA sits inside the nucleus of every cell and passes traits from parent to child. What would you see if you purified a tube of it? Only a sticky white paste of fibres, with no clue how a dead chemical could copy a living blueprint.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:21 — a glass vial of chalky white calf-thymus DNA fibres sits beside a question-marked blueprint of a dividing cell nucleus (STAKES by 0:25) · WHEN: 1951 · LABEL: HOW DOES IT COPY?]

Then, between the second and sixth of May nineteen fifty-two, thirty-one-year-old physical chemist Rosalind Franklin and her student Raymond Gosling beamed X-rays through a single wet strand of DNA to capture "Photograph 51" — a stark black cross proving DNA is a spiral staircase twenty ångströms wide with ten rungs per turn.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:34 — an inked diagram on period paper traces the X-shaped diffraction pattern of Photograph 51 into a helical staircase 20 ångströms (2 nanometres) wide, with 10 rungs per turn rising 3.4 ångströms per step and repeating every 34 ångströms (FIRST CONCRETE ANSWER by 0:30, FIRST REAL FACT before 0:45) · WHO: Rosalind Franklin (1920–1958) — King's College physical chemist · WHO: Raymond Gosling (1926–2015) — King's College PhD student · WHEN: 2–6 May 1952 · DATA: inked diagram of Photograph 51's X-cross showing 10 rungs per turn, 3.4 Å step rise, 34 Å helix pitch, and 20 Å diameter · SCALE: 20 ångströms wide — 50,000 times thinner than a human hair]

That spiral is fifty thousand times thinner than a human hair. Yet every nucleated cell in your body packs two full metres of it — tens of billions of kilometres across your body's trillions of cells, enough to stretch past Pluto and back.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:52 — an inked scale comparison on period parchment unspools 2 metres of DNA from a single cell nucleus and multiplies across trillions of human cells to tens of billions of kilometres spanning the solar system · DATA: inked comparison of 2 metres of DNA per human cell nucleus vs tens of billions of km across trillions of body cells · SCALE: tens of billions of km of DNA in you — enough to stretch past Pluto and back · LABEL: PAST PLUTO AND BACK]

## PART 02: The Coal Chemist and the Wet Thread (1:25–3:05)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Coal Chemist and the Wet Thread · 1:25 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:28 — Paris, 1947–1950: in a bright French laboratory, young Rosalind Franklin (readable face, late twenties, dark wavy hair, crisp white lab coat) places black lumps of coal and charred graphite inside an X-ray camera · WHEN: Paris, 1947–1950 · FACT: Studied coal before DNA]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:52 — King's College London, January 1951: in a subterranean corridor, biophysicist Maurice Wilkins (readable face, mid-thirties, tall, spectacles, tweed jacket) returns from holiday to find Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling aligning a fine-focus X-ray tube over a bundle of DNA fibres · WHO: Maurice Wilkins (1916–2004) — King's College biophysicist · WHEN: London, January 1951 · LABEL: CROSSED WIRES]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:18 — Explainer diagram on period paper: an inked two-state humidity diagram shows a needle-thin 1.54-ångström X-ray beam hitting a DNA fibre; at 75% relative humidity the fibre sits in the short crystalline A-form, and at 92% relative humidity water molecules bind the outer phosphate groups, stretching the fibre by 25% into the wet helical B-form · DATA: inked two-state humidity diagram showing dry A-DNA (75% relative humidity) stretching by ~25% into wet B-DNA (92% relative humidity) under a 1.54 Å X-ray beam · SCALE: 1.54-ångström X-rays — 3,500 times shorter than green visible light]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:45 — close view of Photograph 51 developing in a darkroom tray: ten horizontal layer lines form a bold black X, with the fourth layer line conspicuously blank · LABEL: THE MISSING FOURTH LINE]
[TEACH: Rosalind Franklin discovered that DNA shifts between two shapes depending on humidity: a dry A-form at 75% humidity and a wet, stretched B-form at 92% humidity. Separating them produced Photograph 51's sharp helical cross.]

Before touching DNA, Rosalind Franklin spent four years in Paris from nineteen forty-seven to nineteen fifty studying coal. Using X-rays, she measured microscopic pores in carbon — explaining why some charcoals work as gas-mask filters while others turn into graphite.

However, when she arrived at King's College London in January nineteen fifty-one, she walked into a trap. The lab chief assigned Franklin and student Raymond Gosling to X-ray DNA fibres originally gathered by biophysicist Maurice Wilkins.

Wilkins was away on holiday. Returning, he assumed Franklin was his assistant. But Franklin had been told in writing that the X-ray work was hers alone. Soon the two barely spoke.

Now, to see DNA's shape, Franklin used X-rays with a wavelength of one point five four ångströms — three thousand five hundred times shorter than green light, scattering off atoms onto film.

Why had earlier X-rays looked blurry? By autumn nineteen fifty-one, Franklin discovered the secret. DNA changes shape when it drinks water.

At seventy-five percent humidity, DNA sits in a short, dry crystal shape she named the A-form. But when she bubbled hydrogen through salt water to reach ninety-two percent humidity, water clung to the strand and stretched the fibre by twenty-five percent into a wet spiral — the B-form.

Earlier researchers had photographed both shapes mixed together. Holding a single B-form fibre at ninety-two percent humidity in May nineteen fifty-two, Franklin and Gosling caught Photograph 51. That black cross proved a helix — and the missing fourth line of spots showed how its strands were spaced.

If Franklin held the sharpest X-ray of DNA on Earth, why did she put Photograph 51 aside — while two rivals in Cambridge tried to guess the structure with tin and wire?

## PART 03: The Inside-Out Blunder (3:05–4:55)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Inside-Out Blunder · 3:05 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:08 — Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge, November 1951: American biologist James Watson (readable face, early twenties, lanky, tousled hair) and British physicist Francis Crick (readable face, mid-thirties, animated expression, brass calipers in hand) stand before a tall metal retort stand bolting brass rods together · WHO: James Watson (born 1928) — American postdoctoral biologist · WHO: Francis Crick (1916–2004) — Cavendish doctoral physicist · WHEN: Cambridge, November 1951 · LABEL: TIN AND WIRE]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:35 — back-and-forth portrait cuts between Rosalind Franklin at the chalkboard in King's College on 21 November 1951 and Watson and Crick unveiling their lopsided three-stranded model in Cambridge on 28 November 1951 · WHEN: 21 November 1951 · WHEN: 28 November 1951 · LABEL: COMPLETELY INSIDE OUT]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:02 — Cavendish Laboratory, December 1951: laboratory director Sir Lawrence Bragg (readable face, sixties, white moustache, dark suit) points sternly to the dismantled brass model as crates of metal moulds are packed for London · WHO: Lawrence Bragg (1890–1971) — Cavendish laboratory director · FACT: Bragg banned their model-building]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:28 — Pasadena & Cambridge, January 1953: chemist Linus Pauling (readable face, fifties, grey hair, bright smile) sits beside a three-stranded model at Caltech while Watson and Crick in Cambridge read his advance manuscript in disbelief · WHO: Linus Pauling (1901–1994) — Caltech chemistry professor · WHEN: January 1953 · LABEL: THE SAME BLUNDER]
[TEACH: In November 1951, Watson and Crick built a flawed three-stranded model of DNA with its sugar-phosphate backbones on the inside. Rosalind Franklin refuted it in minutes using her water-content measurements, and Linus Pauling made the exact same mistake in January 1953.]

In November nineteen fifty-one at Cambridge's Cavendish Laboratory, twenty-three-year-old American biologist James Watson and thirty-five-year-old British physicist Francis Crick were impatient.

That spring, California chemist Linus Pauling had solved the spiral shape of proteins simply by building metal scale models like a puzzle. Watson and Crick wanted to crack DNA the same way.

On the twenty-first of November, nineteen fifty-one, Watson went to London to hear Franklin lecture on her X-ray numbers. Taking no written notes, he misremembered her water figures by nearly a factor of ten.

One week later, on the twenty-eighth of November, Watson and Crick invited Wilkins and Franklin to Cambridge to unveil their model: a three-stranded helix with the sugar-phosphate chains jammed on the inside, and the bases sticking out.

Franklin demolished it in minutes. Negatively charged phosphates would push each other apart in the core. Moreover, her measurements proved wet DNA holds ten times more water than Watson remembered. Because phosphates love water, the backbones had to sit on the outside, with the bases tucked inside.

Worse still, Cavendish director Sir Lawrence Bragg banned Watson and Crick from building any more DNA models.

For over a year, they were stopped cold — until January nineteen fifty-three, when word arrived that Linus Pauling had written a paper solving DNA.

When Watson and Crick opened Pauling's manuscript, they gasped. Suddenly they saw that Pauling had built a three-stranded helix with the phosphates on the inside — the exact same blunder Franklin had crushed fourteen months earlier.

They knew Pauling would spot his error within weeks. So what happened on the thirtieth of January, nineteen fifty-three, when James Watson took the train down to London?

## PART 04: The Drawer Opens (4:55–6:40)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Drawer Opens · 4:55 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:58 — King's College London, 30 January 1953: in a quiet laboratory room, Maurice Wilkins pulls open a wooden desk drawer, lifts out the glass-mounted print of Photograph 51, and hands it to a wide-eyed James Watson · WHEN: London, 30 January 1953 · FACT: Shown without Franklin's permission]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:26 — Cambridge, mid-February 1953: molecular biologist Max Perutz (readable face, late thirties, spectacles, dark jumper) hands Francis Crick a buff-covered December 1952 Medical Research Council committee report; beside it, an inked diagram on period paper shows Franklin's C2 monoclinic crystal unit cell with two antiparallel chains running in opposite directions (one up, one down) · WHO: Max Perutz (1914–2002) — Cambridge molecular biologist · WHEN: February 1953 · DATA: inked crystal-symmetry diagram of Franklin's C2 monoclinic space group showing two antiparallel backbones running in opposite directions with 3.4 Å rung spacing · SCALE: 3.4 ångströms between rungs — 3 million rungs stacked in a single millimetre]

[EXPLORER ACTS: once. Sitting on the corner of Watson's wooden desk in Cambridge, the Explorer slides the two cardboard cut-outs of Adenine and Thymine together until their notched edges click into a level rung, then grins]
[TEACH: After seeing Photograph 51 on 30 January 1953 and Franklin's crystal symmetry in an MRC report in February 1953, Watson and Crick used Erwin Chargaff's 1:1 ratios and Jerry Donohue's chemical advice on 28 February 1953 to lock the A–T and G–C base pairs inside an antiparallel double helix.]

Rosalind Franklin held the X-ray proof, and Watson and Crick were barred from experiments. Case closed? Wrong.

Preparing to leave King's for Birkbeck College, Franklin was finishing her calculations on dry A-DNA while Gosling handed Photograph 51 to Maurice Wilkins.

On the thirtieth of January, nineteen fifty-three, Watson visited King's. Without Franklin's permission, Wilkins opened a drawer and showed Watson Photograph 51.

Watson wrote: "The instant I saw the picture my mouth fell open and my pulse began to race."

On the train to Cambridge, Watson sketched the cross on his newspaper. It proved B-DNA was a helix repeating every thirty-four ångströms, with ten rungs per turn spaced three point four ångströms apart — three million rungs in a single millimetre.

Then, in February nineteen fifty-three, Crick's supervisor Max Perutz handed him a December nineteen fifty-two Medical Research Council report containing Franklin's measurements.

Franklin had found that her DNA crystals belonged to a symmetry group called C2 monoclinic. To Crick, that geometry meant DNA had two chains running in opposite directions — one up, one down.

Now they needed the rungs. In nineteen fifty, biochemist Erwin Chargaff had proved that in any species, the letter A always equals T, and G always equals C.

Yet Watson couldn't make the shapes fit — until the twenty-seventh of February, nineteen fifty-three, when American chemist Jerry Donohue pointed out that Watson's textbook drew the bases with their hydrogen atoms in the wrong positions.

On the twenty-eighth of February, Watson slid four cardboard cut-outs across his desk. Suddenly, A locked with T, and G locked with C, forming rungs of the exact same width. Unzip the two strands, and each half is a direct mould for copying the other.

Yet when the discovery appeared in print, why did the world think Rosalind Franklin had merely confirmed someone else's work?

[VISUAL MUST: 6:43 — King's College London, 17 March 1953: close view of Rosalind Franklin's typed draft manuscript dated 17 March 1953 beside Photograph 51, where the words "two-chain coaxial helix" and "phosphates on the outside" are underlined in ink · WHEN: 17 March 1953 · WHEN: 25 April 1953 · LABEL: 17 MARCH 1953]

[VISUAL MUST: 7:42 — Stockholm, December 1962 & Bowood House, 1774: three Nobel medals gleam beside a quiet tribute portrait of Rosalind Franklin (1920–1958); the camera pans across the workbench to a glass bell jar inverted over mercury with a burning candle and a 12-inch magnifying lens · WHEN: 16 April 1958 · WHEN: December 1962 · LABEL: WHAT BURNS THE FUEL?]
[TEACH: An unpublished manuscript dated 17 March 1953 proved Rosalind Franklin had independently deduced that B-DNA is a two-chain helix with outer phosphates before seeing the Cambridge model. Every dividing cell in your body unzips and copies six billion DNA letters using that geometry.]

For decades after James Watson's memoir, a popular myth claimed that Rosalind Franklin was so opposed to helices that she couldn't read her own photograph. Was that true?

No. Ten years after her death, historians found Franklin's own unpublished draft manuscript, dated the seventeenth of March, nineteen fifty-three — written before she knew the details of the Cambridge model.

Working strictly from Photograph 51, Franklin had already deduced on her own that B-DNA is a two-chain helix with its phosphates on the outside and ten steps per turn.

However, when the three papers appeared in *Nature* on the twenty-fifth of April, nineteen fifty-three, Watson and Crick's model came first, thanking the King's group only for "a knowledge of the general nature" of their unpublished results. Franklin and Gosling's paper printing Photograph 51 was placed third — making her photograph look like an afterthought.

Rosalind Franklin died of ovarian cancer on the sixteenth of April, nineteen fifty-eight, at thirty-seven. Four years later, in December nineteen sixty-two, Watson, Crick, and Wilkins shared the Nobel Prize, while Franklin had gone to her grave without ever being told how much their model owed to her data.

From isolating DNA in eighteen sixty-nine and proving it carries genes in nineteen forty-four, to Photograph 51 in May nineteen fifty-two, every time one of your cells divides, it unzips and copies six billion chemical letters along the staircase Franklin caught on film.

Copying those six billion letters takes energy — burned using a gas you breathe seventeen thousand times a day. Yet when an English experimenter first trapped pure oxygen under a glass bell jar with a lens and a mouse, why did he go to his grave insisting oxygen didn't exist?

A wet strand of DNA, a hundred-hour X-ray and four cardboard cut-outs. Together, they revealed the twisted ladder of life.
