In eighteen fifty, a twenty-eight-year-old friar sat in a Vienna exam room, handed in his biology paper, and failed his teaching certificate.

Seven years earlier, in eighteen forty-three, Johann Mendel had entered St Thomas's Abbey in Brünn, taking the name Gregor. For a poor farmer's son, the monastery was the only place to study science.

Fortunately, Abbot Cyrill Napp sent Mendel to the University of Vienna from eighteen fifty-one to eighteen fifty-three.

There, Mendel studied physics under Professor Christian Doppler. Doppler taught a habit biologists rarely used: don't just look at one specimen. Run large experiments, measure thousands of results, and count the numbers.

In May eighteen fifty-six, Mendel sat the teaching exam a second time, fell ill during the first question, and walked out. He never became a certified teacher. Instead, he returned to the abbey garden.

Rumour says he first bred mice in his room until the bishop objected. Soon, Mendel switched to garden peas.

From eighteen fifty-four to eighteen fifty-six, he tested thirty-four pea varieties — enough packets to line a shed shelf — and selected twenty-two pure lines.

Why peas? Because a pea flower is sealed shut like a tiny tent, so wind and bees rarely mix the pollen.

From those lines, Mendel picked seven clear either-or traits. What happened when he crossed them?

## PART 03: The Trait That Vanished (3:05–5:00)

[TEACH: Each parent passes down one instruction per trait. A recessive trait can hide behind a dominant one in the first generation and reappear unchanged in one quarter of the next.]

In spring eighteen fifty-six, Mendel knelt beside his trellises with tweezers and a paintbrush.

To cross two plants, he opened a green bud before its pollen ripened, snipped out the pollen sacs, dusted the flower with pollen from the other parent, and tied a calico bag over the blossom so no beetle could ruin the count. He made two hundred and fifty-three crosses on seed shape alone.

When he crossed pure smooth peas with pure wrinkled peas, the paint-blending idea predicted every new pea would be slightly wrinkled.

Instead, every first-generation pea was completely smooth. The wrinkled trait had vanished.

Where had it gone? The next summer, Mendel let those smooth hybrids self-pollinate, then counted every seed.

Out of seven thousand three hundred and twenty-four peas, five thousand four hundred and seventy-four were smooth, and one thousand eight hundred and fifty were wrinkled. Two point nine six to one — almost three smooth peas for every wrinkled one.

Across eight thousand and twenty-three seeds checked for colour — a pile heavy enough to tip a kitchen scale at two kilograms — six thousand and twenty-two were yellow, and two thousand and one were green. Three point zero one to one.

How could a vanished trait return unchanged in one quarter of the grandchildren? Mendel realised each plant carries two invisible elements for every trait: one from the pollen, one from the egg cell. He called the trait that shows "dominant", and the one that hides "recessive". When two hybrids cross, four combinations are equally likely: three carry a dominant smooth instruction, and one carries two recessive wrinkled ones.

On the eighth of February and eighth of March, eighteen sixty-five, Mendel read his paper to about forty members of the Natural Science Society in Brünn, and published it in eighteen sixty-six.

He had cracked the code of heredity. Case closed? Wrong.

[TEACH: Munich botanist Carl von Nägeli urged Mendel to test hawkweed. Because hawkweed makes seeds asexually without pollen, Mendel's 3-to-1 ratio seemed to fail.]

Why did forty people sit in that hall in eighteen sixty-five and ask not a single question? Because biologists then described plants with words, not algebra. They thought Mendel had merely found a quirk of peas.

Mendel sent forty copies of his eighteen sixty-six paper to leading scientists. Most were never even cut open.

The one famous botanist who wrote back was Professor Carl von Nägeli in Munich, corresponding with Mendel from December eighteen sixty-six until eighteen seventy-three. Yet Nägeli doubted pea rules governed wild nature, and challenged Mendel to repeat his crosses on hawkweed.

It was a cruel trap. For years, Mendel strained his eyes with a needle and lens, crossing tiny hawkweed blossoms.

Worse still, when the rare seeds grew, Mendel's three-to-one law failed every time. The offspring looked just like their mothers.

What neither man knew — until nineteen hundred and three — is that hawkweed reproduces by apomixis. It makes seeds without pollen, turning out maternal clones even after you snip the stamens!

In March eighteen sixty-eight, Mendel was elected Abbot, bogged down in a tax dispute, and stopped his plant work. Near the end of his life, he is said to have told a friend: "My time will come."

When he died on the sixth of January, eighteen eighty-four, the monastery burned his private papers.

If his notes were ashes and his law seemed broken, how did the world ever find out he was right?

Thirty-five years passed — longer than a human generation — while Mendel's paper gathered dust, cited only about three times.

Then, in spring nineteen hundred, three botanists — Hugo de Vries in Amsterdam, Carl Correns in Tübingen, and Erich von Tschermak in Vienna — independently counted the same three-to-one ratio in their own plants, searched the journals, and found the eighteen sixty-six paper from Brünn.

In nineteen hundred and five, Cambridge biologist William Bateson coined a new word for the science built on Mendel's peas: genetics.

But here is a question that puzzles almost everyone. If Mendel's rule is so simple, why doesn't everything in your family follow a three-to-one box? Why aren't people simply six feet tall or one foot tall, like Mendel's pea stems?

What would you see inside our cells today? Most human traits, like height or skin tone, aren't controlled by one pair of genes. They are shaped by dozens of gene pairs working together, each adding a tiny step, like instruments playing one chord.

That is why human families looked like blended paint. Mendel succeeded because he chose seven pea traits where a single gene acts like an on-off switch.

Why was this so groundbreaking? Because long before anyone saw DNA, a friar with a paintbrush proved that life is passed down in unbroken digital packets.

And while Mendel counted peas in Brünn, Charles Darwin in England was agonising over the one great flaw in his theory of evolution — why natural selection wasn't washed away by blended inheritance. The answer was already in print in German, and Darwin never knew it existed.

A paintbrush, a walled garden and twenty-eight thousand pea plants. Together, they revealed the code of life.
