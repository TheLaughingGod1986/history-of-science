## Outline (Parts 02–06)

Each part opens cold on a scene, quote or question, and ends on a question the next part answers (*Reference lessons* §1). Music shifts between ambient wonder (`awe`) and light period plucked/arpeggiated explain cues (`explain`) at the part cards (*Polish pass* §2).

### PART 02: The Friar Who Failed Twice (1:20–2:35) — `awe` → `explain`
- **Cold open:** A Vienna examination room in 1850. A nervous twenty-eight-year-old friar hands in his biology paper — and fails his state teaching certificate.
  - Mendel (born Johann Mendel on a small farm in Silesia) enters St Thomas's Abbey in Brünn (now Brno) in **1843** because he cannot afford university fees.
  - Wait — Abbot **Cyrill Napp** sends him to the University of Vienna (**1851–1853**), where physicist **Christian Doppler** teaches him to look for simple mathematical laws in nature through large numbers of repeated trials.
  - In **May 1856**, Mendel sits his teaching exam a second time, walks out ill after the first question, and never becomes a certified teacher. Instead, he goes back to the abbey garden.
  - *Markers:* `· WHO: Cyrill Napp (1792–1867) — Abbot of Brünn · WHO: Christian Doppler (1803–1853) — Vienna physics professor · WHEN: Vienna, 1851–1853 · WHEN: May 1856 · FACT: Failed teacher exam twice`
- **Ends on:** If he wanted to test how traits pass from parent to child, why did he switch from mice to garden peas?

### PART 03: Twenty-Eight Thousand Plants (2:35–4:00) — `explain`
- **Cold open:** Rumour says Mendel started by breeding mice in his abbey room until the bishop objected; whether or not the bishop knew, garden peas (*Pisum sativum*) had three huge advantages.
  - First, pea flowers are sealed shut, so wild wind or bees rarely cross them by accident.
  - Second, Mendel spent **two full years (1854–1856)** testing **34 varieties** from seed merchants to pick **22 pure-breeding lines** and **7 clear either-or traits** (round vs wrinkled seed, yellow vs green albumens, purple vs white flowers, tall 6-foot vs dwarf 1-foot stems, etc.) — no murky in-betweens.
  - Third, to cross two plants, he opened a bud before its own pollen ripened, snipped out the anthers with fine forceps, dabbed pollen from the other parent with a camel-hair brush, and tied a small calico bag over the flower so no beetle could ruin the count.
  - **The First Generation ($F_1$):** Cross a pure round-seed pea with a pure wrinkled-seed pea. Blending says every child should be slightly wrinkled. Instead, **100% are completely smooth and round**. The wrinkled trait has vanished!
  - *Markers:* `· WHEN: Brünn, 1854–1856 · DATA: inked diagram of the 7 either-or pea traits and the 253 hybrid fertilisations across 7,324 seeds · SCALE: 7,324 peas — enough to fill a two-litre glass jar · FACT: Tested 34 seed varieties`
- **Ends on:** Where did the wrinkled trait go — was it destroyed forever, or hiding inside?

### PART 04: Three to One (4:00–5:20) — `explain`
- **Cold open:** In the second summer, Mendel lets those smooth first-generation plants self-pollinate, splits open the pods, and starts counting.
  - Out of **7,324 seeds**, **5,474 are round** and **1,850 are wrinkled** — a ratio of **2.96 to 1**, or **3 to 1**.
  - Across **8,023 seeds** checked for colour, **6,022 are yellow** and **2,001 are green** — **3.01 to 1**.
  - The wrinkled and green traits hadn't been diluted at all; they had hidden unchanged for an entire generation and stepped back out in one-quarter of the grandchildren!
  - **The mechanism (Explainer diagram on period paper):** Each plant carries **two elements** (what we now call alleles) for each trait — one from the pollen, one from the egg cell. Mendel names the one that shows **dominant** (*dominirend*) and the one that hides **recessive** (*recessiv*). A simple 2×2 grid inked on period paper shows why $Aa \times Aa$ produces $1\ AA : 2\ Aa : 1\ aa$ — three showing the dominant trait for every one showing the recessive.
  - *Markers:* `· DATA: animated 2×2 inked grid and tally table (5,474 : 1,850 round/wrinkled; 6,022 : 2,001 yellow/green = 3:1) · SCALE: almost exactly 3 in every 4 peas · FACT: Coined "dominant" and "recessive"`
- **Ends on:** He had solved a mystery as old as farming. When he stood up to tell the world in February 1865, why did nobody clap?

### PART 05: The Hawkweed Trap — Midpoint Twist (5:20–6:45) — `awe`
- **Cold open:** Cold evening, **8 February 1865** (and **8 March 1865**). About forty members of the Natural Science Society gather in Brünn as Mendel reads *Experiments in Plant Hybridization*.
  - The room is polite — and completely silent. Biologists in 1865 didn't use algebra or probability to study living things; they thought he had only found a quirk of garden peas.
  - **Twist ("Case closed? Wrong."):** Published in **1866**, Mendel sends **40 reprint copies** to leading scientists across Europe. Almost none are even cut open.
  - The one famous botanist who writes back, **Carl von Nägeli** in Munich (**1866–1873**), doubts that pea rules apply to nature and challenges Mendel to repeat his crosses on **hawkweed (*Hieracium*)** — Nägeli's own favourite plant.
  - For years Mendel strains his eyesight dissecting tiny hawkweed florets with a needle and lens — and his 3:1 law fails every single time! Neither man knew that hawkweed reproduces asexually without pollen (**apomixis**), making maternal clones even after you snip the stamens.
  - In **1868** Mendel is elected Abbot, bogged down in a monastery tax dispute, and stops his plant work. He tells a friend: *"My time will come."* (Softened in script as "he is said to have told a friend" unless primary-sourced in `SOURCES_v00.md`). When he dies on **6 January 1884**, the monastery burns his private papers.
  - *Markers:* `· WHEN: Brünn, 8 February & 8 March 1865 · WHEN: 1866 · WHO: Carl von Nägeli (1817–1891) — Munich botany professor · WHEN: 6 January 1884 · DATA: animated timeline building 1856 → 1865 → 1866 → 1884 → 1900 · SCALE: 35 years between Mendel's 1865 lecture and its 1900 rediscovery — longer than a human generation · FACT: Hawkweed clones its own seeds`
- **Ends on:** If his notes were burned and his law seemed broken, how did the world ever find out he was right?

### PART 06: Three Letters in the Spring of 1900 (6:45–7:45) — `awe`
- **Cold open:** Spring **1900** — thirty-five years after Mendel stood in that hall in Brünn.
  - Within three months of each other, three botanists in three countries — **Hugo de Vries** in Amsterdam, **Carl Correns** in Tübingen, and **Erich von Tschermak** in Vienna — independently count the same 3:1 ratio in their own plants, search the old library journals, and find the 1866 paper from Brünn that had been cited roughly **three times** in thirty-four years!
  - In **1905–1906**, British biologist **William Bateson** coins the word **genetics** for the new science built on Mendel's peas.
  - **The misconception explained away:** Do single pairs of genes explain everything, like height or eye shade in a simple 3:1 box? No — most human traits use many genes working together, which is why people looked blended to the naked eye; Mendel won because he picked seven pea traits where a single gene acts like an on-off switch.
  - *Markers:* `· WHEN: Spring 1900 · WHO: Hugo de Vries (1848–1935) — Dutch botanist · WHO: Carl Correns (1864–1933) — German botanist · WHO: Erich von Tschermak (1871–1962) — Austrian botanist · WHO: William Bateson (1861–1926) — Cambridge biologist · WHEN: 1905–1906 (timeline completes)`
