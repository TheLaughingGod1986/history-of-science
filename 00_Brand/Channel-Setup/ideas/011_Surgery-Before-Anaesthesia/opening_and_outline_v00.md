## PART 01: The Fastest Knife (0:00–1:20). No chapter card.

Before 1846, the best surgeon in the world was simply the fastest one. Because the patient was awake for every second of it.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:00 — a crowded, steep wooden operating theatre lit by tall windows; students lean over the rail; a surgeon in a dark frock coat checks a pocket watch, then looks up (ANSWER IMAGE by 0:03) · WHEN: London, 1840s · LABEL: THE FASTEST KNIFE]

In London, one surgeon was famous for taking off a leg in a few minutes. His students timed him with their watches.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:10 — close on a row of students' pocket watches opening one after another; the second hands sweep · DATA: a brass stopwatch dial that sweeps round once and stops short of three minutes · WHO: Robert Liston (1794–1847) — London surgeon]

Then, in one week in the autumn of 1846, all of that changed. A dentist in Boston held a glass globe to a young man's face, and the young man felt nothing.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:22 — Boston, a tiered hall under a dome; a dentist holds a glass globe with a mouthpiece to a young patient, whose eyes slowly close (FIRST CONCRETE ANSWER by 0:30) · WHEN: Boston, 16 October 1846 · LABEL: ETHER]

So how do you put a person to sleep, and bring them back? Who worked it out first? And why did the men who found it spend the rest of their lives fighting about it?

[VISUAL MUST: 0:38 — three portrait frames on a dark panelled wall, side by side, their faces still in shadow; a candle flickers below them · LABEL: WHO WAS FIRST?]

By the end of this film, you'll know how a party trick, a country doctor and a hall full of doubters turned surgery from a race into something you could sleep through.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:50 — three objects appear on a wooden table one after another: a balloon of laughing gas, a doctor's leather bag, a glass ether globe · LABEL: A GAS · A BAG · A GLOBE]
[TEACH: Before 1846 most operations were done on patients who were wide awake. Speed was the only mercy a surgeon could give.]

Picture yourself on that table in 1840. What would you want most from your surgeon? Not skill. Speed.

## Outline (Parts 02–06)

### PART 02: A Gas That Made People Laugh (1:20–2:40)
- **Davy's line, 1800:**
  - Humphry Davy breathes nitrous oxide at the Pneumatic Institution in Bristol and writes that it "may probably be used with advantage during surgical operations".
  - Nobody acts on it for over 40 years. Laughing-gas and "ether frolics" become party entertainment.
  - · WHO: Humphry Davy (1778–1829) — young chemist · WHEN: Bristol, 1800 · FACT: Used at parties first
- **Ends on:** if the answer was written down in 1800, why did surgeons keep cutting people awake?

### PART 03: The Doctor Who Didn't Tell Anyone (2:40–3:50)
- **Long's operation, 1842:**
  - Crawford Long, a young doctor in Jefferson, Georgia, has seen guests at ether frolics bruise themselves and feel nothing.
  - On 30 March 1842 he removes a tumour from James Venable's neck with ether. Venable feels nothing.
  - Long doesn't publish until 1849.
  - · WHO: Crawford Long (1815–1878) — country doctor · WHEN: Georgia, 1842 · the timeline starts here (DATA: a timeline that builds 1800 → 1842 → 1845 → 1846 → 1847, one marker per date as it's said)
- **Ends on:** so who would show the world?

### PART 04: The Day It Failed (3:50–4:50)
- **Wells's failure, 1845:**
  - Horace Wells, a Hartford dentist, has his own tooth pulled under laughing gas in December 1844.
  - In January 1845 he demonstrates at Massachusetts General Hospital. The patient cries out, and the room calls it a humbug.
  - · WHO: Horace Wells (1815–1848) — Hartford dentist · WHEN: Boston, January 1845 · timeline marker
- **Ends on:** his former partner was in the room. What did he do next?

### PART 05: "This Is No Humbug" (4:50–6:10). The midpoint twist is at the end of this part.
- **Ether Day, 16 October 1846:**
  - William Morton gives ether to Edward Gilbert Abbott in the hall now called the Ether Dome. The surgeon, John Collins Warren, removes a growth from Abbott's neck and tells the room: "Gentlemen, this is no humbug."
  - · WHO: William T. G. Morton (1819–1868) — Boston dentist · WHO: John Collins Warren (1778–1856) — senior surgeon · WHEN: Boston, 16 October 1846 · timeline marker
- **The name:**
  - Oliver Wendell Holmes suggests the word "anaesthesia" in a letter to Morton on 21 November 1846.
  - · WHO: Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. (1809–1894) — doctor and poet · FACT: A poet named it
- **Twist ("Case closed? Wrong."):**
  - Morton tries to keep the gas secret, calls it "Letheon", and patents it.
  - Charles Jackson, Wells and Long all claim it first.
  - The fight lasts decades, and Boston's 1868 Ether Monument names nobody (TO VERIFY).
  - · FACT: The monument names nobody

### PART 06: Across the Ocean (6:10–7:30)
- **Liston in London:**
  - On 21 December 1846 Robert Liston amputates a leg under ether at University College Hospital in London: "This Yankee dodge beats mesmerism hollow."
  - The fastest knife no longer needs to be fast.
  - · WHEN: London, December 1846 · timeline marker
- **Chloroform and a queen:**
  - In November 1847 James Young Simpson in Edinburgh tries chloroform.
  - In 1853 John Snow gives it to Queen Victoria for the birth of Prince Leopold, and ordinary people stop fearing it.
  - · WHO: James Young Simpson (1811–1870) — Edinburgh obstetrician · WHO: John Snow (1813–1858) — London anaesthetist · WHEN: 1853 · DATA: the timeline completes
- **The scariest idea, explained away:** "Is going under still dangerous?" Give the modern safety figure (a sourced number, TO FIND) and say why a trained anaesthetist watches every breath.
