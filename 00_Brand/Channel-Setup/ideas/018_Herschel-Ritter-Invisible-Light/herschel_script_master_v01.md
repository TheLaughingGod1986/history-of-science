Picture yourself in the winter of eighteen hundred. For more than a century since Isaac Newton split sunlight with a glass prism in sixteen sixty-six, every scientist on Earth has believed that the seven colours of the rainbow *are* the entire spectrum of light. What would you see if you looked beyond red and violet? Only empty darkness.

Then, on the eleventh of February eighteen hundred, sixty-one-year-old astronomer William Herschel slid a mercury thermometer half an inch into the dark shadow past the red end of a sunlit rainbow, and watched the temperature rise nine degrees Fahrenheit in sixteen minutes — hotter than in any visible colour — until German physicist Johann Wilhelm Ritter placed chemical paper beyond the violet end on the twenty-second of February eighteen-oh-one and discovered ultraviolet.

## PART 02: The Musician's Burned Filters and the Three Thermometers (1:25–3:05)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Musician's Burned Filters and the Three Thermometers · 1:25 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:28 — Bath, England, 1773–1781: in a Georgian parlour turned workshop, William Herschel (readable face, early forties, powdered wig, rolled shirt sleeves) grinds a gleaming speculum-metal telescope mirror by hand while his sister Caroline Herschel (readable face, late twenties, mob cap, notebook and spoon in hand) feeds him a spoonful of supper and reads aloud · WHO: Caroline Herschel (1750–1848) — Hanoverian-British astronomer · WHEN: Bath, 1773–1781 · WHEN: 13 March 1781 · FACT: Composed 24 symphonies first]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:58 — Slough, England, early 1800: in his garden observatory, Herschel peers through a telescope at the blazing Sun through a dark red glass filter that cracks from intense heat, then swaps it for a dark green glass filter that stays cool · LABEL: RED BURNS, GREEN COOLS]
[VISUAL MUST: 2:28 — Slough, 11 February 1800 (read 27 March 1800): close view of Herschel's wooden prism stand; sunlight through a narrow slit in a cardboard screen falls on Thermometer No. 1 (blackened bulb) while Thermometers No. 2 and No. 3 sit in the shade as controls, rising +2°F in violet, +3.25°F in green, and +6.875°F in red · WHEN: 27 March 1800 · LABEL: THREE THERMOMETERS]
[TEACH: After composing 24 symphonies and discovering Uranus with his sister Caroline on 13 March 1781, William Herschel tested coloured solar telescope filters on 11 February 1800 and found that red light heated a blackened thermometer more than three times as much as violet (+6.875°F vs +2°F).]

Before he became King George the Third's personal astronomer, William Herschel was a German oboist, organist, and composer who wrote twenty-four symphonies.

However, after moving to Bath, England, he grew obsessed with the night sky. Unable to afford a large telescope, William and his sister Caroline Herschel turned their house into a workshop. William ground metal mirrors by hand for sixteen hours straight while Caroline spoon-fed him supper, until on the thirteenth of March, seventeen eighty-one, he discovered the planet Uranus.

By eighteen hundred in Slough, Herschel had turned his telescopes on the Sun.

But looking at the Sun through a telescope without protection blinds you in seconds. To survive the glare, Herschel tested dark glass filters of different colours — and noticed a strange mystery.

Dark red glass let through scorching heat even when the Sun looked dim. Yet dark green glass blocked the heat while keeping the image sharp.

Why would different colours of light carry different amounts of heat?

On the eleventh of February, eighteen hundred — in a paper read to the Royal Society on the twenty-seventh of March — Herschel mounted a glass prism in his sunny window shutter. He cut a narrow slit in a cardboard screen so only one colour at a time fell onto three mercury thermometers whose bulbs he had painted black with Indian ink to absorb heat.

In ten minutes, violet light raised the mercury by just two degrees Fahrenheit. Green raised it by three and a quarter degrees. Suddenly, in deep red light, the thermometer climbed nearly seven degrees — more than three times hotter than violet.

Now, if the temperature kept climbing all the way from violet to the very edge of red, why would it suddenly stop just because human eyes stop seeing red?

## PART 03: Into the Dark — Infrared and Ritter's Ultraviolet Twin (3:05–4:55)

Herschel suspected that an invisible stream of rays continued past the rainbow into the dark.

For his second paper, read on the twenty-fourth of April, eighteen hundred, he drew five parallel lines spaced half an inch apart into the dark shadow beyond the red edge of the spectrum.

Then he slid Thermometer Number One onto the first line — half an inch past the last trace of visible red, the width of a thumb into complete darkness.

In sixteen minutes, the thermometer in the dark rose nine degrees Fahrenheit — hotter than in red itself. Even a full inch into the shadow, it climbed more than five degrees. Herschel had discovered infrared radiation — what he called "calorific rays," proving that the greatest heating power of sunlight is completely invisible.

When news reached the University of Jena in Germany, twenty-four-year-old experimenter Johann Wilhelm Ritter was electrified.

Ritter believed that nature is built on symmetry: just as every magnet has a north and south pole, if invisible *warming* rays sit past red, then invisible *chemical* rays must sit past violet.

How could he catch them if a thermometer felt nothing there? Back in seventeen seventy-seven, Swedish chemist Carl Wilhelm Scheele had noticed that white paper coated with silver chloride — "horn silver" — turns black faster in violet light than in red.

On the twenty-second of February, eighteen-oh-one, Ritter laid damp silver-chloride paper in the dark shadow *beyond* the violet end of a sunlit rainbow. Suddenly, in the dark, the white paper turned jet-black faster than anywhere in the visible spectrum. Ritter had discovered ultraviolet light — waves twice as short as red light, energetic enough to snap chemical bonds and sunburn your skin.

With invisible rays found on both sides of the rainbow, did William Herschel celebrate discovering two new kinds of light?

Herschel had proved that his invisible heat rays reflect off mirrors and bend through lenses just like light. Case closed? Wrong.

Between May and November eighteen hundred, Herschel ran more than two hundred experiments — and talked himself into believing the exact opposite.

Why? Because he walked into a glass trap. When Herschel held a pane of clear crown glass in front of a candle or a hot iron poker, nearly a hundred percent of the visible light shone straight through, but the glass blocked seventy to ninety percent of the invisible heat.

Worse still, when he plotted the eye's brightness curve — peaking in yellow-green — beside the thermometer's heating curve — peaking in the dark beyond red — the two peaks didn't match. On the sixth of November, eighteen hundred, Herschel told the Royal Society that radiant heat and light were two completely different kinds of rays.

How was the paradox solved? Just twelve months later, in November eighteen-oh-one, English physician Thomas Young passed sunlight through two pinholes and proved light is a wave. He measured deep red waves at one thirty-sixth-thousandth of an inch — about seven hundred nanometres, so tiny that fourteen hundred waves fit across the edge of a dime — and violet at one sixty-thousandth of an inch, suggesting Herschel's and Ritter's rays were simply longer and shorter waves.

Then, between eighteen thirty-one and eighteen thirty-five, Italian physicist Macedonio Melloni replaced glass lenses with prisms carved from clear rock salt. Unlike glass, rock salt lets long-wave infrared pour straight through — proving heat rays behave identically to light.

Finally, in eighteen sixty-five, Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell proved that radio, infrared, visible light, and ultraviolet are all one electromagnetic wave travelling at three hundred thousand kilometres per second.

If glass blocks long-wave infrared while letting visible sunlight through, what happens every time the Sun shines on your car window — or on a telescope a million miles out in space?

[VISUAL MUST: 7:45 — Jena & Pavia, 1800–1801: on Ritter's workbench beside his silver-chloride paper sits a tall column of stacked zinc and silver coins soaked in salt water, sparking as a brass wire touches a dissected frog's leg on an iron balcony railing · WHEN: March 1800 · LABEL: ANIMAL OR METAL?]
[TEACH: Herschel's 1800 glass trap explains why windows trap long-wave infrared heat (~10,000 nm, the wavelength your own 37°C body radiates) and why the James Webb Space Telescope uses chilled gold mirrors to see infrared light from the early universe.]

Many people think infrared is just a dim shade of red — or wonder why a car parked in winter sunshine gets roasting hot inside while the glass window stays cool. What is really happening?

Herschel's glass trap from November eighteen hundred is the exact culprit. Short-wave sunlight shines straight through clear window glass and warms the seats inside. Those warm seats then radiate long-wave infrared at around ten thousand nanometres — fifteen times longer than red light — which glass blocks from escaping.

In fact, at thirty-seven degrees Celsius, your own body glows day and night at ten thousand nanometres in the mid-infrared, letting thermal cameras see you in pitch darkness. And a million miles from Earth, the James Webb Space Telescope — chilled below minus two hundred and twenty-three degrees Celsius — uses gold mirrors to catch stretched infrared light from thirteen point five billion light-years away, doing across the cosmos what William Herschel did on a table in Slough.

Yet how did twenty-four-year-old Johann Wilhelm Ritter pay for his experiments in Jena? Just months earlier, in March eighteen hundred, Italian physicist Alessandro Volta had stacked zinc and silver coins soaked in salt water to build the first electric battery — trying to win a furious argument over why a dead frog's leg kicked when touched by a metal scalpel.

A glass prism, a blackened thermometer and a strip of silver-coated paper. Together, they opened our eyes to the invisible universe.
