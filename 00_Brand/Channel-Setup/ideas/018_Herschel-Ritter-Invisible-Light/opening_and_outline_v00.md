### PART 02: The Musician's Burned Filters and the Three Thermometers (1:25–3:05)
- **Cold open (quirky anecdote before the optics):** **Bath, England, 1773–1781**. Before he was King George III's personal astronomer, **William Herschel** (1738–1822) was a Hanoverian oboist, organist and composer who wrote twenty-four symphonies. Obsessed with the night sky and unable to afford a large telescope, he and his sister **Caroline Herschel** (1750–1848) turned their Bath house into a foundry — grinding speculum-metal mirrors by hand for sixteen hours straight while Caroline fed him mouthfuls of food and read aloud, leading to his discovery of the planet **Uranus** on **13 March 1781**!
- By **early 1800** in **Slough**, Herschel had turned his telescopes on the **Sun** to study sunspots and solar granules.
- **The practical problem:** Looking at the Sun through a telescope without protection blinds you instantly, and Herschel noticed that dark coloured glass filters cracked or burned his eye differently depending on their colour:
  1. Dark **red** glass let through glaring heat even when the image looked dim.
  2. Dark **green** glass blocked the heat while keeping the image sharp.
- Why would different colours of light carry different amounts of heat? On **11 February 1800** (read to the Royal Society on **27 March 1800**), Herschel mounted a prism in his south-facing window shutter, laid a cardboard screen with a narrow slit on a slanted board so only one colour at a time could pass through, and placed **three matched mercury thermometers** (with their bulbs painted black with Indian ink to absorb heat) on the board: one in the colour band, and two to the side as room-temperature controls.
- In ten minutes, **violet** light raised Thermometer No. 1 by **2°F**, **green** by **3.25°F**, and **full red** by **6.875°F (nearly 7°F)** — more than three times hotter than violet!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: Bath, 1773–1781 · WHO: Caroline Herschel (1750–1848) — Hanoverian-British astronomer · WHEN: 13 March 1781 · FACT: Composed 24 symphonies first · WHEN: 27 March 1800`
- **Ends on:** If the temperature kept climbing all the way from violet to the very edge of red, why would it suddenly stop just because human eyes stop seeing red?

### PART 03: Into the Dark — Infrared and Ritter's Ultraviolet Twin (3:05–4:55)
- **Cold open:** **Slough, March 1800**. Herschel asks himself the decisive question: does the heating power of sunlight stop at the red edge of the rainbow, or does an invisible stream of rays continue into the dark?
- In his second paper (read to the Royal Society on **24 April 1800**), Herschel marks **five parallel lines** spaced **half an inch apart** into the dark shadow beyond the red end of the spectrum.
- When he places Thermometer No. 1 **half an inch past the last trace of visible red**, the mercury rises **9°F (5°C) in 16 minutes** — higher than in red itself! Even **one full inch** into the dark, the thermometer still climbs **5.25°F**; the heat only fades out **1.5 inches** beyond the visible rainbow.
- Herschel calculates that the **maximum heating power** of sunlight lies completely outside the visible spectrum — naming these invisible waves **"calorific rays"** (what we now call **infrared**, meaning "below red").
- **Rivalry / symmetry beat (Jena, Germany, 22 February 1801):** When twenty-four-year-old German experimenter **Johann Wilhelm Ritter** (1776–1810) reads of Herschel's discovery at the University of Jena, he is electrified. Guided by *Naturphilosophie* — the belief that nature is built on symmetric polarities like north and south magnetic poles or positive and negative electricity — Ritter predicts that if invisible *warming* rays exist past red, invisible *cooling* or *chemical* rays must exist past **violet**!
- Back in **1777**, Swedish chemist **Carl Wilhelm Scheele** (1742–1786) had noticed that white paper coated with **silver chloride ($\text{AgCl}$, "horn silver")** turns black faster in violet light than in red. On **22 February 1801**, Ritter lays strips of damp silver-chloride paper in the dark shadow *beyond the violet end* of a prism spectrum — and watches the paper blacken furiously in the dark, discovering **ultraviolet radiation** ("chemical rays")!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: 24 April 1800 · WHEN: 1777 · WHO: Carl Wilhelm Scheele (1742–1786) — Swedish pharmaceutical chemist · WHEN: Jena, 22 February 1801 · DATA: inked dual-spectrum diagram on period paper showing Herschel's thermometer peak 0.5 inches beyond the 750 nm red edge (+9°F) and Ritter's silver-chloride (AgCl) darkening peak beyond the 380 nm violet edge · SCALE: 380 nm violet to 750 nm red — ultraviolet waves are twice as short as red light, carrying enough energy per photon to break silver-chloride bonds and sunburn human skin · FACT: Found ultraviolet with silver salt`
- **Ends on:** With invisible rays discovered on both sides of the rainbow, did William Herschel celebrate discovering two new kinds of light?

### PART 04: The Discoverer's Doubt — Midpoint Twist (4:55–6:40)
- **Cold open ("Case closed? Wrong."):** **Slough, May to November 1800**. You would expect William Herschel to tell the world that his invisible heat rays and visible light were the exact same thing, just at different wavelengths. Instead, Herschel spent the summer of **1800** running more than **two hundred experiments** — and talked himself into believing the exact opposite!
- Why? First, using mirrors and lenses, Herschel proved that invisible heat rays obey the exact same laws of reflection and refraction as visible light.
- **The trap that fooled Herschel:** Next, Herschel tested how much heat vs how much light passed through dozens of sheets of glass and liquid cells. A pane of clear crown glass let through nearly **100% of the visible light** from a candle or a red-hot iron poker, yet blocked **70% to 90% of the radiant heat**! Even more baffling to Herschel, plotting the brightness curve of the rainbow (which peaked in yellow-green) next to the heating curve (which peaked in the dark past red) showed two completely separate peaks.
- On **6 November 1800**, Herschel stood before the Royal Society and concluded that radiant heat and light were **fundamentally different kinds of rays**!
- **How the mystery was solved (1801–1865):**
  1. Just twelve months later (**November 1801 – 1803**), English physician and polymath **Thomas Young** (1773–1829) passed sunlight through two pinholes and proved that light is a **wave**, measuring the wavelength of deep red light at about **1/36,000th of an inch (~700 nanometres)** and extreme violet at **1/60,000th of an inch (~420 nanometres)**.
  2. In **1831–1835**, Italian physicist **Macedonio Melloni** (1798–1854) replaced glass lenses (which absorb mid-infrared) with prisms made of **rock salt ($\text{NaCl}$)** and an ultra-sensitive electric **thermopile**, proving that heat rays polarise, interfere and refract identically to light — glass simply happens to be "opaque" to longer infrared wavelengths just as red glass is opaque to blue light!
  3. In **1865**, Scottish physicist **James Clerk Maxwell** (1831–1879) unified electricity, magnetism and light into a single wave travelling at **300,000 kilometres per second**.
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: 6 November 1800 · FACT: Herschel thought heat wasn't light · WHEN: November 1801 · WHO: Thomas Young (1773–1829) — English physician & physicist · WHEN: 1831–1835 · WHO: Macedonio Melloni (1798–1854) — Italian physicist · WHEN: 1865 · WHO: James Clerk Maxwell (1831–1879) — Scottish theoretical physicist · DATA: inked comparison curve of Herschel's November 1800 illumination peak (yellow-green, ~555 nm) vs radiant-heat curve (>750 nm infrared) alongside clear glass transmission vs rock-salt transmission · SCALE: 700 nanometres (red light) vs 400 nanometres (violet light) — 1,400 waves of red light fit across the thickness of a single dime`
- **Ends on:** If glass blocks long-wave infrared heat while letting visible sunlight straight through, what happens every time the Sun shines on your car windshield — or on a telescope floating a million miles out in space?

### PART 05: The Greenhouse Trap, James Webb & the Frog-Leg Spark (6:40–8:15)
- **Cold open (popular misconception debunked):** People often call night-vision thermal cameras "infrared cameras" and assume infrared is just a dim shade of red — or wonder why a car parked in winter sunshine gets scorching hot inside even though the glass window feels cool.
- **The truth:** Herschel's "glass trap" from November 1800 is the exact reason! Short-wave visible and near-infrared sunlight (`400–2,000 nm`) passes straight through clear window glass and warms the seats inside; the warm seats then radiate long-wave thermal infrared (`~10,000 nm`), which glass blocks from escaping! And because your own body at **37°C** glows brightly at **10,000 nanometres (10 micrometres)** in the mid-infrared, a thermal camera can see you in pitch darkness with zero visible light — while the **James Webb Space Telescope**, chilled to below **-223°C (50 K)**, uses gold-coated mirrors to capture the stretched **infrared light** of the earliest galaxies in the universe, doing across **13.5 billion light-years** what William Herschel did on a wooden table in Slough in **1800**!
- **After-act hand-off (7:45–8:15):**
  - How did twenty-four-year-old **Johann Wilhelm Ritter** in Jena pay for his silver-chloride experiments in **1801**? Just months earlier, an Italian physicist named **Alessandro Volta** had stacked zinc and silver coins soaked in salt water to build the world's first **electric battery** — trying to win a furious argument over why a dead **frog's leg** kicked when touched by a scalpel (`019` candidate *Sparks of Life & the First Battery: Luigi Galvani's Frog Legs & Alessandro Volta's Pile of Coins*).
