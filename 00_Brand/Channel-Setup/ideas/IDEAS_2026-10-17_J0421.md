### 1. Penicillin & Antibiotics: Alexander Fleming's mouldy dish & the Oxford bedpan factory (recommended for 020)
- **Familiar thing:** a course of antibiotic pills that cures a life-threatening infection in days — and the greenish-blue mould that grows on an old loaf of bread or a forgotten piece of fruit.
- **Person & room:** Scottish bacteriologist **Alexander Fleming** (1881–1955) in his tiny, cluttered second-floor turret laboratory at **St Mary's Hospital, Paddington, London**, on **3 September 1928**; then Australian pathologist **Howard Florey** (1898–1968), German-Jewish refugee biochemist **Ernst Chain** (1906–1979), and ingenious biochemist **Norman Heatley** (1911–2004) in the **Sir William Dunn School of Pathology at Oxford** (**May 1940 – February 1941**); and microbiologist **Mary Hunt** ("Moldy Mary") at the USDA Northern Regional Research Laboratory in **Peoria, Illinois** (**July 1943**).
- **Date & proof:** Before **1941**, a scratch from a rose thorn, an infected blister, or a strep throat could kill a healthy adult in days by blood poisoning (sepsis). In **August 1928**, **Alexander Fleming** stacked his glass Petri dishes of golden *Staphylococcus* bacteria in a corner of his bench at St Mary's Hospital and left for a summer holiday in Suffolk. When he returned on **3 September 1928** and chatted with former assistant **Merlin Pryce**, Fleming picked up a dish from the top of the pile before submerging it in disinfectant, paused, and muttered two famous words: **"That's funny."** A stray spore of blue-green mould — ***Penicillium notatum*** — had drifted onto the agar, and all around the mould sat a crystal-clear halo where the deadly *Staphylococcus* colonies had dissolved into transparent droplets! Even diluted **1 part in 800**, the mould's golden "mould juice" (which Fleming named **penicillin** on **7 March 1929**) stopped bacteria dead without harming human white blood cells.
- **Twist (three-part):**
  1. **Fleming gives up for a decade (1929–1939):** Textbooks often pretend Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928 and doctors immediately started curing patients. **Wrong!** Penicillin was so chemically unstable and hard to extract from the watery broth that Fleming couldn't purify it; by **1931**, he largely abandoned penicillin as a human medicine and used it only as a laboratory filter to isolate picky bacteria!
  2. **The Oxford bedpans, the 8 mice & the policeman's urine (25 May 1940 – 12 February 1941):** Nine years later, as Nazi bombers threatened Britain, **Ernst Chain** and **Howard Florey** at Oxford unearthed Fleming's forgotten **1929** paper, while **Norman Heatley** invented a back-extraction tower using amyl acetate and water, and commissioned **hundreds of rectangular ceramic hospital bedpans** to grow acres of mould skin during the Blitz! On Saturday **25 May 1940**, they injected **eight mice** with a lethal dose of *Streptococcus* (**110 million bacteria**): the **four untreated mice died within 17 hours**, while all **four penicillin-treated mice survived** (*"A miracle,"* Florey said). Yet it took **2,000 litres** of mould broth just to treat one human patient! When they treated 43-year-old Oxford policeman **Albert Alexander** (dying from a scratch on his face) on **12 February 1941**, he rallied dramatically within 24 hours — even as Heatley extracted and recycled unabsorbed penicillin from the policeman's own **urine** — until the tiny Oxford supply ran out after 5 days and Alexander relapsed and died on **15 March 1941**.
  3. **Spores in their coat linings & the mouldy Peoria cantaloupe (1941–1943):** Determined never to run out again — and after smearing *Penicillium* spores into the cloth linings of their own jackets in case Hitler invaded Britain — Florey and Heatley flew to **Peoria, Illinois** in **July 1941**, where USDA scientists fed the mould **corn steep liquor** in deep aerated fermentation tanks and sent laboratory assistant **Mary Hunt** to local grocery markets for mouldy fruit. In **July 1943**, Mary Hunt brought back a rotting **cantaloupe melon** carrying a golden strain (***Penicillium chrysogenum* NRRL 1951**) that — after X-ray and UV mutation — produced **over 1,000 times more penicillin** than Fleming's original dish, manufacturing **2.3 million doses** in time for the **D-Day landings on 6 June 1944**!

### 2. Electromagnetism: Hans Christian Ørsted's twitching compass & Michael Faraday's cup of mercury (recommended for 021, or 020 right after 019)
- **Familiar thing:** every electric motor spinning inside your fan, washing machine, blender and electric train, and every power-station turbine generating the electricity in your wall socket.
- **Person & room:** Danish physics professor **Hans Christian Ørsted** (1777–1851) in a candlelit university lecture room in **Copenhagen** on the evening of **21 April 1820**; cut to 29-year-old former bookbinder's apprentice **Michael Faraday** (1791–1867) and his jealous mentor **Sir Humphry Davy** (1778–1829) in the basement laboratory of the **Royal Institution on Albemarle Street, London**, on **3–4 September 1821** and **29 August 1831**.
- **Date & proof:** For twenty years after **Alessandro Volta** invented the battery (`019`), scientists were sure electricity and magnetism were two completely unrelated forces. Then, during an evening lecture on **21 April 1820**, **Hans Christian Ørsted** connected a platinum wire across a Voltaic battery directly above a magnetic compass needle — and saw the needle jerk sideways! Strange still, the needle didn't point *toward* the wire or *away* from it, but stood **perpendicular** to it, proving an electric current wraps itself in a **circular magnetic whirlwind**. In London on **3–4 September 1821**, self-taught laboratory assistant **Michael Faraday** — who left school at thirteen and knew almost no algebra — stuck a bar magnet upright in a wax-bottomed cup of liquid **mercury**, hung a free-swinging copper wire from a cork above it, and connected a battery: instantly, the wire spun in rapid, non-stop circles around the magnet (**the world's first electric motor**), prompting Faraday to dance around the table shouting, *"There they go! There they go! We have succeeded at last!"*
- **Twist (three-part):**
  1. **The jealous mentor & the sole blackball vote (October 1821 – January 1824):** When Faraday published his spinning-wire motor in **October 1821**, his famous patron **Sir Humphry Davy** — President of the Royal Society — grew consumed with jealousy, accused Faraday of stealing the idea of circular rotation from **William Hyde Wollaston**, and even campaigned against Faraday's election as a Fellow of the Royal Society on **8 January 1824**, where a single anonymous blackball vote was cast against him!
  2. **Ten years of failure & the half-second twitch (1821–29 August 1831):** Silenced for years on electromagnetism, Faraday kept a small iron cylinder and coiled wire in his pocket, asking one question: if an electric current can make magnetism, why won't a magnet make an electric current? Everyone who clamped a strong magnet next to a wire got **zero current**. Finally, after Davy's death, on **29 August 1831** Faraday wound two separate coils of copper wire (`203 feet` and `203 feet`) around opposite sides of a **6-inch iron ring**. The instant he connected the first coil to a battery, the galvanometer needle on the second, unconnected coil **jumped — and immediately fell back to zero**! When he disconnected the battery, it jumped in the **opposite direction**!
  3. **Motion is the secret (17 October & 28 October 1831):** A stationary magnet never makes electricity — only a **changing or moving magnetic field** induces a current (**electromagnetic induction**)! On **17 October 1831**, Faraday plunged a bar magnet in and out of a hollow paper cylinder wrapped in **220 feet of copper wire**, watching the needle swing back and forth; eleven days later (**28 October 1831**), he cranked a copper disc between the poles of a horseshoe magnet to generate the first continuous dynamo current — the grandfather of every power station on Earth!

### 3. Splitting the Atom: Lise Meitner's walk in the Swedish snow & Otto Hahn's barium puzzle
- **Familiar thing:** nuclear power plants generating carbon-free electricity, atomic energy, and Einstein's famous equation $E = mc^2$.
- **Person & room:** Radiochemist **Otto Hahn** (1879–1968) and **Fritz Strassmann** (1902–1980) at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Chemistry in **Berlin** on **17–19 December 1938**, writing a baffled midnight letter to exiled Austrian-born physicist **Lise Meitner** (1878–1968) and her nephew **Otto Robert Frisch** (1904–1979) walking on cross-country skis through the snow-covered woods of **Kungälv, Sweden**, on **24 December 1938**.
- **Date & twist:** For four years after **Enrico Fermi** bombarded uranium (element 92) with neutrons in **1934**, the world's greatest physicists believed hitting a heavy uranium nucleus with a slow neutron merely chipped off tiny alpha/beta particles or created slightly heavier "transuranic" elements (93, 94). Nobody thought a slow neutron could split a giant nucleus in half — because physicists treated the atomic nucleus like a solid brittle rock. In **July 1938**, **Lise Meitner** (who had worked side-by-side with **Otto Hahn** in Berlin for 30 years) had to flee Nazi Germany in secret with two small suitcases and ten marks in her purse. Five months later (**17–19 December 1938**), Hahn and Fritz Strassmann bombarded uranium with slow neutrons in Berlin and tested the products chemically: instead of radium (element 88), their crystals tested as **barium (element 56)** — an element nearly **half the weight of uranium**! Baffled, Hahn wrote to Meitner in Sweden on **19 December**: *"Perhaps you can come up with some sort of fantastic explanation. We knew ourselves that it can't actually burst into barium."* On **Christmas Eve, 24 December 1938**, walking through the snowy woods of **Kungälv, Sweden** with her physicist nephew **Otto Frisch** on skis, Meitner sat on a fallen tree trunk and calculated on scraps of paper that a uranium nucleus behaves like an **electrically charged drop of liquid** (Bohr's liquid-drop model): adding one gentle neutron stretches the drop into a waisted dumbbell until the mutual electrostatic repulsion of its **92 protons** snaps it violently in two, releasing **200 million electron-volts (200 MeV)** of energy from the missing mass ($\Delta m \cdot c^2$, about one-fifth of a proton's mass)! **The double twist:** Frisch coined the term **"nuclear fission"** (after biological cell division in `015`) in their **11 February 1939** *Nature* paper, yet because Meitner was a Jewish refugee in exile, Hahn published the chemical finding without her name and was awarded the **sole 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry** — before element **109 (Meitnerium, Mt)** was finally named in her honour!

### 5. Thermodynamics & Conservation of Energy: Count Rumford's cannon-boring in Munich & James Prescott Joule's falling-weight paddle wheel
- **Familiar thing:** calories on every food label, an engine or brake disc getting scorching hot from friction, and why no perpetual-motion machine can ever work.
- **Person & room:** American-born adventurer and military engineer **Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford** (1753–1814) at the Electoral Arsenal in **Munich, Bavaria**, in **January 1798**, watching horses turn a blunt steel borer inside a brass cannon barrel submerged in a wooden box of water; and twenty-something Manchester brewer's son **James Prescott Joule** (1818–1889) in his basement laboratory in **Salford/Manchester** (**1843–June 1845**), dropping lead weights on strings to spin a brass **paddle wheel** inside an insulated copper cylinder of water.
- **Date & twist:** At the end of the eighteenth century, the world's leading chemists — including **Antoine Lavoisier** (`017`) — believed heat was an invisible, weightless fluid called **"caloric"** trapped inside matter and squeezed out like water from a sponge when you rubbed or hammered a solid. In **January 1798** at the Munich military arsenal, **Count Rumford** noticed that boring out brass cannon barrels made the metal hotter than boiling water. If heat were a finite fluid squeezed out of the brass shavings, a blunt borer that cut almost *no* shavings should produce almost no heat — and the cannon should eventually run out of heat! Instead, Rumford surrounded a brass cylinder with **18.77 pounds (`8.5 kg`) of cold water**, pressed a blunt steel borer against it turned by two horses, and after **2 hours and 30 minutes** the water **boiled violently with no fire at all** (and never ran out of heat!), proving heat is **motion**, not a fluid! Yet scientists still ignored him for forty years until Manchester brewer **James Prescott Joule** built a brass paddle wheel churned by falling weights (**1843–1845**) and measured with thermometers sensitive to **1/200th of a degree Fahrenheit** that **772 foot-pounds** of mechanical work always raises **1 pound of water by 1°F** (`4.184 joules per calorie`) — only to be ignored by the Royal Society until a 23-year-old **William Thomson (Lord Kelvin)** stood up at a noisy **June 1847** Oxford meeting and later bumped into Joule on his **honeymoon in the Swiss Alps**, where Joule was carrying a giant thermometer to try to measure the temperature rise at the bottom of a waterfall!
