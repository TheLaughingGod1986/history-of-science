For more than a thousand years, the greatest minds of antiquity — including **Euclid** and **Ptolemy** — taught a theory of vision that sounds like a comic-book superpower: **`extramission`**. They claimed you see the world because invisible beams of fire shoot *out* of your eyeballs, reach across space like a walking stick to touch a tree or a star, and report its shape back to your soul!

Then between **ten eleven and ten twenty-one AD** in **Cairo**, Arab physicist **Ibn al-Haytham** — locked under house arrest after admitting he could not dam the flooding Nile — wrote his seven-volume ***Kitāb al-Manāẓir* (`The Book of Optics`)**. First, he demolished eye-beams with one question: if beams shoot *out* of our eyes, **why does staring at the bright midday sun hurt**? And when you close your eyes in a dark room after glancing at a flame, why does a glowing **`afterimage`** linger inside your vision? That pain and ghost image prove that light physically **enters the eye (`intromission`)**!

Critics still objected: if light reflects off a tree in all directions, why don't all those rays scramble in mid-air? To prove how light travels, Ibn al-Haytham hung **five oil lamps** outside a darkened room (**`al-bayt al-muzlim`** — the **`camera obscura`**) with a single tiny pinhole in the shutter. On the white wall inside sat **five sharp spots of light** in the **exact opposite order**! Blocking one lamp outside made only its matching spot vanish — proving that light rays travel in **straight lines** without mixing!

**Ibn al-Haytham's** darkened room proved that light enters the eye — yet it created a baffling new paradox: in a pinhole camera, the picture on the back wall is **upside down**! Why would your eye flip the world on its head — and how did **Johannes Kepler in sixteen hundred and four** and a **peeled ox eye in sixteen nineteen** solve the mystery?

In **sixteen hundred and four** in Prague and **sixteen nineteen** in Ingolstadt, two scientists proved that you really *do* see the entire world **upside down** — and scraped open an eyeball to show the inverted picture with their own eyes!

For six hundred years after Ibn al-Haytham — even after Italian glassmakers around **twelve eighty-six** ground the first **`spectacles`** and Swiss anatomist **Felix Platter** in **fifteen eighty-three** realized that the **`retina`** is the true light sensor — scientists feared an upside-down image.

Then in **sixteen hundred and four**, German astronomer **Johannes Kepler** traced the geometry of light bending (**`refraction`**) through the curved **`cornea`** and **`crystalline lens`**. A lens lets your pupil open wide to gather a bright cone of rays and bends them to a sharp point on your **`retina`**. Because top and bottom rays cross at the lens, the picture painted on your retina is **upside down and backwards** — and your **brain** flips it upright! Better still, Kepler explained how **glasses** work: in a **short-sighted (`myopic`)** eye, rays focus *too soon* in front of the retina, so a **concave lens** spreads them out; in a **far-sighted** eye, rays focus *too late* behind the retina, so a **convex lens** bends them in!

In **sixteen nineteen**, Jesuit physicist **Christoph Scheiner** pared away the white coat at the back of a fresh **ox eye** until only the translucent **`retina`** remained, held it before a candle in a dark room, and watched a **tiny, crystal-sharp, upside-down flame** glowing on the back of the eye!

**Kepler and Scheiner** proved that the eye is a living camera — but a rigid glass camera lens has to slide back and forth to focus near and far! As people pass age forty, the lens inside the eye stiffens (**`presbyopia`**) — which drove **Benjamin Franklin** in **May seventeen eighty-four** to saw his distance and reading lenses in half to invent **`bifocals`**. Yet nobody knew *how* a young eye switches focus between far and near (**`accommodation`**): did muscles squeeze the eyeball longer, did the **`cornea`** bulge steeper, or did the **`crystalline lens`** flex inside the eye?

On **twenty-seven November eighteen hundred** (`published in eighteen hundred and one`), twenty-seven-year-old British polymath **Thomas Young** tested all three ideas on his own living eye! First, he plunged his open eye under water — cancelling out the cornea's bending power — and proved his eye could *still* switch focus! Next, Young turned his eye sharp inward and wedged a blunt **iron ring** behind the back of his eyeball right against the retina! When he focused from far to near, the pressure ring on his retina didn't budge — proving the eyeball never lengthens! Instead, a ring of muscle inside your eye lets the elastic **`crystalline lens`** spring **fatter and rounder** to bend close-up light! In that same **eighteen hundred and one** paper, Young also discovered **`astigmatism`** (`when the cornea is curved like a spoon rather than a sphere, corrected in eighteen twenty-five`).

Finally, in **Utrecht in eighteen sixty-two** (`with Franciscus Donders's eighteen sixty-four laws of refraction`), Dutch eye doctor **Herman Snellen** designed block letters (**`optotypes`**) on a **`five-by-five` grid**, led by the **Big "E"**, where each stroke subtends **one arcminute** at **twenty feet (`six metres`)** — giving humanity **`20/20` vision**!
