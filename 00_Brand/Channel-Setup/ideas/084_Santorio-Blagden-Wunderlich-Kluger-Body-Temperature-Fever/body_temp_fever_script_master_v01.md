For two thousand years, doctors judged a fever solely by laying a hand on a patient's forehead. Then in **sixteen twelve** in Padua, Venetian physician **Santorio Santorio** curved a glass thermoscope tube so a patient could hold the bulb inside their mouth and added a numbered scale — inventing the **first clinical thermometer**! For thirty years (**sixteen fourteen**), Santorio also ate, worked, and slept on a giant **hanging weighing chair**, proving that over half the weight we eat and drink vanishes invisibly from our skin and breath as water vapour!

Yet Europe's leading authorities still taught that breathing air hotter than human blood would instantly cook a person alive. On **twenty-three January seventeen seventy-five**, Royal Society physician **Charles Blagden**, **George Fordyce**, and **Joseph Banks** walked into a London brick chamber heated by red-hot cast-iron flues to **`one hundred and twenty-six degrees Celsius` (`260 °F`)** — **twenty-six degrees hotter than boiling water**!

Inside that furnace, their metal watch-chains burned their fingers, **eggs roasted hard in twenty minutes**, and a **raw beefsteak cooked in thirty-three minutes**! Yet Blagden and his friends stood inside for eight minutes while thermometers under their tongues stayed at **`37.8 °C` (`100 °F`)** — and breathing onto the hot wall thermometer actually *cooled* the glass down! To prove how living skin beat the oven, they set out two flasks of water, one open and one sealed with a film of **oil** to block **evaporation**: the oiled flask boiled furiously, while the open flask stayed cool because **evaporating water (`and evaporating sweat!`) carries away `580 calories` of heat per gram**!

**Blagden** proved that evaporating sweat shields us from extreme heat — yet what *is* the exact temperature of a healthy human body, and how did one German doctor in **Leipzig in eighteen sixty-eight** take **one million armpit measurements** to find out?

Before British physician **Thomas Clifford Allbutt** in **eighteen sixty-six** invented the six-inch pocket thermometer, hospital thermometers were a foot long (`30 centimetres`) and took **twenty minutes** in a patient's armpit.
