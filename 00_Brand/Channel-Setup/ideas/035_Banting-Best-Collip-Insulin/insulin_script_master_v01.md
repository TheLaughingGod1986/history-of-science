## PART 02: Starving in a Sea of Sugar & the 2:00 a.m. Notebook (1:25–3:05)

[TEACH: After Paul Langerhans spotted pancreatic islets in 1869 and Oskar Minkowski proved in 1889 that removing the pancreas causes diabetes, Frederick Banting realised on 31 October 1920 that tying off the pancreatic duct causes the 98% enzyme-making cells to degenerate before they can digest the 1%–2% insulin-producing islets.]

For over three thousand years — including English physician Thomas Willis in sixteen seventy-four, who noted that diabetic urine tasted *"wonderfully sweet as if imbued with honey"* — severe diabetes was a swift death sentence.

Why? Because even when patients ate huge meals, their bodies couldn't move glucose sugar out of the bloodstream and into their muscle and fat cells! Their blood turned thick with unburned sugar while their cells literally starved to death — forcing doctors to put dying children on brutal four-hundred-calorie starvation diets just to buy them a few extra months of life.

In eighteen sixty-nine, German medical student Paul Langerhans spotted tiny clusters of cells scattered like islands — barely one percent of the organ — inside the pancreas, later named the **Islets of Langerhans**. Twenty years later, in eighteen eighty-nine, Oskar Minkowski and Joseph von Mering removed the pancreas from a dog and watched flies swarm around its sugar-filled urine! Clearly, those tiny pancreatic islands made a life-saving hormone. Yet for thirty years, whenever scientists ground up pancreases to extract it, the mixture failed!

Enter twenty-nine-year-old Canadian war surgeon Frederick Banting in London, Ontario. His new medical practice was going so badly that in his entire first month he saw one patient and earned just four dollars!

On the night of the thirtieth of October nineteen twenty, while preparing a student lecture on the pancreas, Banting read a case report about a gallstone blocking the main pancreatic duct. Ninety-eight percent of the pancreas makes harsh digestive enzymes like trypsin that dissolve meat in your gut — so when scientists ground up a whole pancreas, its own digestive enzymes chewed the delicate islet hormone to pieces!

At two in the morning on the thirty-first of October nineteen twenty, Banting bolted upright in bed and scribbled twenty-five famous, misspelled words: tie off the pancreatic ducts in living dogs, wait weeks until the ninety-eight percent enzyme cells shrivel away, and extract the intact islets!

Banting had the idea on paper, but no laboratory and no money. How did a coin toss between two students and a stray terrier in a sweltering Toronto attic turn those twenty-five words into a living cure?

Banting took his two-in-the-morning notebook to John Macleod, the world-renowned physiology professor at the University of Toronto. Macleod knew dozens of elite researchers had failed, yet before sailing to Scotland for the summer, he lent Banting a tiny, baking-hot attic operating room, ten dogs, and a student assistant to measure blood and urine sugar.

Two twenty-two-year-old students — Charles Best, whose favourite aunt had just died of diabetes, and Clark Noble — flipped a coin to see who would take the first summer shift with Banting! Best won the toss, and when Banting and Best clicked as partners, Noble let Best stay all summer — putting Charles Best into the history books on the flip of a coin!

Beginning on the seventeenth of May nineteen twenty-one, Banting and Best worked in stifling attic heat, frying eggs and sausages over a Bunsen burner because Banting was so broke he sold his furniture and slept in the lab.

Finally, on the thirtieth of July nineteen twenty-one, they ground up the shrivelled, duct-tied pancreas of a dog in chilled salt water and injected four millilitres — less than a teaspoon — into the vein of a comatose diabetic terrier, **Dog number four-oh-eight**. Within one hour, the dog's blood sugar dropped by forty percent, from two hundred milligrams per decilitre down to a normal one hundred and twenty! The dying dog lifted its head and stood up!

Soon they kept their favourite diabetic dog, **Marjorie**, alive and playful for seventy days on daily shots of the extract Macleod named **insulin** — from the Latin word for "island." And in November nineteen twenty-one, using acidified alcohol on slaughterhouse calf pancreases, they brewed enough extract to test on a human child!

They had saved diabetic dogs all autumn. Case closed? Not yet. When doctors injected Banting and Best's brownish extract into dying fourteen-year-old Leonard Thompson on the eleventh of January nineteen twenty-two, it failed! Why did the first human shot nearly end in disaster — and how did a visiting biochemist unlock the ninety-percent alcohol secret twelve days later?

On the eleventh of January nineteen twenty-two at Toronto General Hospital, fourteen-year-old Leonard Thompson — weighing sixty-five pounds and drifting toward death — received fifteen millilitres of Banting and Best's brownish calf extract.

It was a heartbreaking failure. His blood sugar dipped by only a quarter, the toxic ketones in his urine didn't budge, and the impure beef proteins caused a painful abscess on his hip! Doctors halted the trial at once.

Fortunately, Macleod had invited a brilliant twenty-nine-year-old visiting biochemist named James Collip to join the Toronto team. Working late at night in mid-January, Collip discovered the chemical secret of **fractional alcohol precipitation**: at eighty percent alcohol, almost all the toxic meat proteins drop out of solution while insulin stays dissolved — and when you raise the alcohol past ninety percent, pure insulin precipitates to the bottom of the flask like clean white snow!

On the twenty-third of January nineteen twenty-two, Leonard Thompson received Collip's crystal-clear purified insulin. Within twenty-four hours, his blood sugar plunged from five hundred and twenty milligrams per decilitre down to one hundred and twenty, his urine sugar fell from seventy-one grams to eight, and the dying boy sat up in bed, gained weight, and lived another thirteen years!

Weeks later, Banting, Best, and Collip walked down a Toronto ward where up to fifty children lay dying in diabetic comas, injecting bed after bed — and before they reached the last child at the end of the room, the first children were waking from their comas to the cries of their parents!

On the twenty-third of January nineteen twenty-three, Banting, Best, and Collip sold the patent for insulin to the University of Toronto for just one dollar each, with Banting declaring: *"Insulin does not belong to me, it belongs to the world."* And when the nineteen twenty-three Nobel Prize went only to Banting and Macleod, Banting immediately split his prize money fifty-fifty with Charles Best, and Macleod split his half with James Collip!

So if diabetes fills the blood with sugar, does eating too much candy actually cause Type 1 diabetes in children — and how do doctors make human insulin today without a single animal pancreas?

Many people still think eating too much candy causes Type 1 diabetes in children, or that Type 1 and Type 2 are the same thing. What is the real difference?

In Type 1 diabetes — what Leonard Thompson had — the child's own immune system mistakenly destroys the beta cells in the Islets of Langerhans, leaving the body with zero insulin keys, which has nothing to do with eating sweets! In Type 2 diabetes, the pancreas still makes insulin keys, but the body's cell doors have become resistant, like rusted locks! And after Frederick Sanger mapped all fifty-one amino acids of insulin in nineteen fifty-five, scientists in nineteen seventy-eight inserted the human insulin gene into harmless bacteria — brewing pure human insulin in steel tanks rather than extracting it from farm animals!

And insulin proved something even bigger: a fatal disease can be caused not by an invading germ, but by a single missing molecule your body needs to survive! On the twentieth of May seventeen forty-seven, aboard the warship HMS *Salisbury* in the English Channel, a thirty-year-old Scottish surgeon named James Lind divided twelve dying sailors into six pairs for the world's first controlled clinical trial and cured **scurvy** in six days with two oranges and a lemon — before Dutch doctor Christiaan Eijkman watched paralysed chickens stand up after eating unpolished brown rice in Java in eighteen ninety to reveal **vitamins**!

Twenty-five misspelled words at two in the morning, a summer coin toss in a sweltering attic, and a patent sold for a single dollar. Together, they turned a death sentence into life for millions.
