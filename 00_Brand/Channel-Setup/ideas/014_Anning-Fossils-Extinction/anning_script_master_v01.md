## PART 01: The Monster in the Cliff (0:00–1:25). No chapter card.

Why did scientists two hundred years ago believe it was impossible for any kind of animal to go extinct? And what if the secret that proved whole worlds of creatures had vanished forever was chipped out of a crumbling beach cliff by a twelve-year-old girl?

Picture yourself in the late seventeen-hundreds. Almost every naturalist believes every species was made once and can never die out. What would you see if a giant bone turned up in the ground? You would assume that creature was still alive somewhere, hiding in an unknown forest or a deep ocean.

[VISUAL MUST: 0:21 — a candlelit Paris museum hall: a huge curved mammoth tusk sits on a table beside a globe with vast blank oceans marked UNKNOWN (STAKES by 0:25) · WHEN: Late 1700s · LABEL: STILL HIDING SOMEWHERE?]

But in eighteen eleven and eighteen twelve, on the storm-washed cliffs of Lyme Regis in southern England, twelve-year-old Mary Anning and her brother Joseph uncovered a seventeen-foot skeleton — longer than a family rowing boat — belonging to a mysterious sea reptile unlike anything alive on Earth.

Then, twelve years later, she uncovered a second mystery: a monster with a neck of thirty-five bones — five times as many neck bones as a giraffe — so bizarre that the greatest anatomist in Europe suspected it was a fake.

## PART 02: Curiosities on the Beach (1:25–3:05)

[CHAPTER CARD: Curiosities on the Beach · 1:25 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:28 — Paris, 1796: young Georges Cuvier (readable face, late twenties, high collar, dark coat) stands before an anatomical table comparing an Indian elephant skull, an African elephant skull, and a giant fossil mammoth jaw · WHO: Georges Cuvier (1769–1832) — Paris anatomist · WHEN: Paris, 1796 · LABEL: LOST SPECIES?]
[VISUAL MUST: 1:48 — Lyme Regis, 19 August 1800: dark storm clouds gather over a green meadow and a tall elm tree; inside a cottage moments later, neighbours revive fifteen-month-old Mary in a basin of warm water · WHEN: Lyme Regis, 19 August 1800 · FACT: Survived a lightning strike]

[VISUAL MUST: 2:38 — November 1810: a small wooden table outside the Anning cottage displays coiled shells for passing coach travellers; an animated timeline builds along the lower edge as 23 gold sovereigns stack beside a ledger · WHEN: November 1810 (timeline marker) · DATA: timeline that builds as each date is said (1796 · 1800 · 1810 · 1811–1812) beside a 23-pound coin stack · SCALE: twenty-three pounds — half a year's labourer wages]
[TEACH: French anatomist Georges Cuvier argued in 1796 that mammoths were extinct, but critics thought strange creatures still lived in unexplored seas. Lyme Regis's crumbling Jurassic cliffs exposed fossils after every winter storm.]

In seventeen ninety-six, a young French anatomist called Georges Cuvier compared the skulls of living elephants with giant fossil jaws from Siberia, and argued they belonged to lost species.

But many scholars still refused to believe him. What if mammoths still walked in unknown forests, and strange sea creatures still swam in the deep ocean?

Then, three years later, in May seventeen ninety-nine, Mary Anning was born in the seaside town of Lyme Regis in Dorset.

On the nineteenth of August, eighteen hundred, when Mary was fifteen months old, a sudden lightning strike hit an elm tree where a neighbour was holding her. The three women under the tree died instantly, until baby Mary was revived in a basin of warm water.

Her father, Richard Anning, was a cabinetmaker who took Mary and her brother Joseph along the beach beneath the crumbling Blue Lias cliffs. Whenever winter storms tore away the clay, strange stones tumbled out: coiled shells locals called snake-stones, and tapered rods called devil's fingers.

With Britain cut off from Europe by the Napoleonic Wars, wealthy tourists flocked to the south coast and bought Richard's curiosities from a table outside his door.

But in November eighteen ten, Richard died after a cliff fall and illness, leaving the family a hundred and twenty pounds in debt.

Then, one winter day in late eighteen eleven, fourteen-year-old Joseph spotted a four-foot stone skull sticking out of the shale. What mystery was waiting inside the cliff behind it?

## PART 03: The Seventeen-Foot Fish-Lizard (3:05–4:55)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Seventeen-Foot Fish-Lizard · 3:05 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:08 — Lyme Regis cliffs, 1811–1812: twelve-year-old Mary Anning chips carefully around ribs and vertebrae embedded in wet grey shale while waves break behind her; quarrymen help lift the heavy stone blocks onto a cart · WHEN: 1811–1812 (timeline marker) · LABEL: CHIPPED FROM SHALE]
[VISUAL MUST: 3:38 — Explainer diagram on period paper: an inked anatomical drawing of the Ichthyosaurus highlights four parts in turn: a dolphin-like snout, crocodile-like teeth, a bony eye ring the size of a dinner plate, and fish-like vertebrae with four whale-like paddles · DATA: inked anatomy diagram of Ichthyosaurus with eye-ring scale comparison · SCALE: eye rings as wide as dinner plates]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:10 — London, 1814: manor lord Henry Hoste Henley counts out gold coins; in the Royal Society library, surgeon Sir Everard Home reads his printed 1814 paper aloud while the title page shows no mention of Mary or Joseph Anning · WHO: Henry Hoste Henley — lord of the manor · WHO: Sir Everard Home (1756–1832) — London surgeon · WHEN: London, 1814 · FACT: Not named in the paper]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:38 — London, 15 May 1820: an auctioneer's gavel falls before wealthy collectors as Colonel Thomas Birch sells his fossil cabinet to save the Anning family from eviction · WHO: Thomas Birch — fossil collector · WHEN: 15 May 1820 · LABEL: £400 TO SAVE THEM]
[TEACH: The 1811–1812 Ichthyosaurus combined features of dolphins, crocodiles and fish, proving it matched no living animal. Wealthy collectors bought Anning's fossils and published them without crediting her.]

Over the winter of eighteen eleven and into eighteen twelve, twelve-year-old Mary Anning chipped away the shale around that four-foot skull until quarrymen helped haul out a seventeen-foot skeleton.

When scientists examined it in London, they were stunned by the paradox in the stone. Its jaws held teeth like a crocodile's. Its eye sockets held bony rings as wide as dinner plates, built for deeper, darker water. But its backbone was shaped like a fish's, and its limbs were four swimming paddles.

It was later named *Ichthyosaurus* — the fish-lizard. No living whale, fish or crocodile matched it.

A local manor lord, Henry Hoste Henley, paid the Annings twenty-three pounds for the skeleton — about half a year's wages for a farm labourer — and sold it to a London museum.

Then, in eighteen fourteen, the Royal Society surgeon Sir Everard Home published the first of six scientific papers describing the unknown creature. Not once did he print Mary or Joseph Anning's name.

Worse still, for Mary, fossil hunting remained a dangerous struggle against landslides and the tide. By eighteen twenty, after a year without a big find, her family was about to sell their furniture to pay the rent, until a kind friend, Colonel Thomas Birch, auctioned his own fossil collection in London on the fifteenth of May, eighteen twenty, raising four hundred pounds for the Annings.

But three years later, in December eighteen twenty-three, Mary uncovered a skeleton so unbelievable that Georges Cuvier himself thought she had faked it.

## PART 04: The Fake That Proved Extinction (4:55–6:40)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Fake That Proved Extinction · 4:55 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 4:58 — Lyme Regis, 10 December 1823: twenty-four-year-old Mary Anning kneels over a flat stone slab containing a complete nine-foot skeleton with four paddles and an astonishing serpent-like neck of thirty-five vertebrae · WHEN: 10 December 1823 (timeline marker) · LABEL: PLESIOSAURUS]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:25 — back-and-forth portrait cuts between Georges Cuvier in his Paris study examining an inked sketch of the 35-vertebra neck through a lens, and geologists William Conybeare and William Buckland in England comparing the slab's unbroken rock seams · WHO: William Conybeare (1787–1857) — English geologist · WHO: William Buckland (1784–1856) — Oxford geologist · FACT: Cuvier suspected a forgery]
[VISUAL MUST: 5:58 — Geological Society of London, 20 February 1824: William Conybeare holds up the wide folding engraving of the Plesiosaurus before rows of top-hatted gentlemen; outside the closed oak doors, a brass plaque reads FELLOWS ONLY · WHEN: London, 20 February 1824 (timeline marker) · LABEL: BARRED FROM THE ROOM]
[TEACH: When Mary Anning found the first complete Plesiosaurus in December 1823, Cuvier initially suspected its 35-bone neck was a forgery. Once verified in February 1824, it sealed the case for extinction — though women were barred from the Geological Society.]

On the tenth of December, eighteen twenty-three, twenty-four-year-old Mary Anning uncovered her masterpiece: the first complete skeleton of a *Plesiosaurus*.

It was nine feet long, with four broad flippers and a tiny head at the end of a neck made of thirty-five vertebrae. Almost every mammal on Earth, from a mouse to a giraffe, has just seven neck bones. Why did this creature have five times as many — like a snake threaded through the body of a turtle?

She had found the proof of a lost world. Case closed? Wrong.

When a drawing of the skeleton reached Georges Cuvier in Paris, the great anatomist suspected a fraud. Collectors in coastal towns sometimes cemented bones from different animals together to raise the price. Cuvier wondered if someone had stitched a sea-serpent spine onto another reptile.

In England, geologists William Conybeare and William Buckland rushed to inspect the slab and sent detailed drawings to Paris, showing every vertebra locked in undisturbed stone.

Then, on the twentieth of February, eighteen twenty-four, Conybeare presented the *Plesiosaurus* to a packed hall at the Geological Society of London. Cuvier admitted openly that he had been wrong, and soon became one of Mary Anning's customers.

But on that triumphant night in London, the woman who found the skeleton could not even step inside the room. Why? Because the Geological Society barred women even as guests.

Skeletons showed how these creatures looked when they died. How did Mary Anning unlock the secret of what they ate when they were alive?

## PART 05: Stones That Were Ancient Meals (6:40–8:20)

[CHAPTER CARD: Stones That Were Ancient Meals · 6:40 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:43 — Anning's workbench, 1824–1829: Mary taps open a twisted grey stone found inside an ichthyosaur's ribs; inside glint tiny dark fish scales and crushed reptile bones · WHEN: 1824–1829 (timeline marker) · LABEL: ANCIENT DROPPINGS]
[EXPLORER ACTS: once. At the corner of the workbench, the Explorer peers through his magnifying glass at the cracked stone, spots a tiny fish scale inside, and recoils with a delighted grin]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:05 — Mary grinds a dried black fossil ink sac from a belemnite with a drop of water in a porcelain dish and dips a quill pen to draw a crisp sepia sketch of an ichthyosaur skull · FACT: Fossil ink still wrote]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:25 — Explainer diagram on period paper: an inked prehistoric food chain builds from left to right (belemnite squid → Jurassic fish → small ichthyosaur → 17-foot ichthyosaur), while the timeline along the bottom completes: 1796 · 1800 · 1811–1812 · 1823 · 1824 · 1829 · 1847 · WHEN: 9 March 1847 (timeline marker) · DATA: inked Jurassic food chain and completed 1796 → 1847 timeline · SCALE: 200-million-year-old ink still dark enough to draw with]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:50 — side-by-side inked comparison on period paper: a land dinosaur standing with straight pillar-like legs beneath its hips on the left, versus Mary Anning's flippered Ichthyosaurus and Plesiosaur swimming in the sea on the right · LABEL: SEA REPTILES, NOT DINOSAURS]
[TEACH: By cracking open "bezoar stones" inside ichthyosaur ribs, Anning and William Buckland showed they were fossilised droppings (coprolites), revealing the first prehistoric food chain. Ichthyosaurs and plesiosaurs were marine reptiles, not dinosaurs.]

For years, Mary Anning noticed twisted grey stones resting inside the ribcages of her ichthyosaurs.

In eighteen twenty-four, she cracked them open with her hammer. Suddenly, the mystery was solved: packed inside were glittering fish scales, squid hooks, and the digested bones of smaller ichthyosaurs.

Working with Oxford geologist William Buckland, who named them coprolites in eighteen twenty-nine, Mary proved those stones were fossilised droppings. For the first time, scientists could reconstruct an ancient food chain and see what a creature ate two hundred million years ago.

She even dissected fossilised squid relatives called belemnites and found their dried ink sacs. Ground with water, that two-hundred-million-year-old ink was still dark enough to draw portraits of the very fossils it came from.

But here is one question people still get wrong today. Were Mary Anning's sea dragons swimming dinosaurs?

No. Dinosaurs were land reptiles whose legs stood straight beneath their hips. Ichthyosaurs and plesiosaurs were a completely separate branch of marine reptiles that ruled the oceans while dinosaurs walked the land.

When Mary Anning died on the ninth of March, eighteen forty-seven, aged forty-seven, the Geological Society — which still barred women until nineteen nineteen — took the rare step of publishing a tribute to her in its president's annual address.

And once her cliffs proved beyond doubt that whole worlds of giant reptiles could vanish forever, a bigger mystery opened up: what kind of catastrophe could wipe out the dinosaurs across the entire planet in a single day?

A small hammer, a crumbling cliff and a twelve-year-old girl's eye. Together, they proved that whole worlds can vanish into stone.
