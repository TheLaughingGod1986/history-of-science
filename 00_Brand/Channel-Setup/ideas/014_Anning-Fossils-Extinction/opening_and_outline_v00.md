## Outline (Parts 02–05)

Each part opens cold on a scene, quote or question, and ends on a question the next part answers (*Reference lessons* §1). Music shifts between ambient wonder (`awe`) and light period plucked/arpeggiated explain cues (`explain`) at the part cards (*Polish pass* §2).

### PART 02: Curiosities on the Beach (1:25–3:05) — `awe` → `explain`
- **Cold open:** Paris, **1796**. Young French anatomist **Georges Cuvier** compares the jaws of living Indian and African elephants with giant fossil bones from Siberia and America, and declares they belong to lost species. Yet critics reply: maybe mammoths still roam uncharted lands, and sea monsters still hide in the deep ocean!
- Cut to **Lyme Regis, Dorset**, on **19 August 1800**: a sudden thunderstorm strikes an elm tree where a woman is holding fifteen-month-old **Mary Anning**. The three women under the tree are killed instantly; baby Mary is revived in a basin of warm water.
- Her father **Richard Anning** (1766–1810), a cabinetmaker, takes Mary along the slippery **Blue Lias** shale cliffs after winter storms to chip out coiled shells locals call "snake-stones" (ammonites) and tapered "devil's fingers" (belemnites), selling them on a small table outside their cottage to wealthy tourists kept in Britain by the Napoleonic Wars.
- In **November 1810**, Richard falls off a cliff, catches tuberculosis, and dies leaving the family **£120 in debt**. Eleven-year-old Mary goes back to the crumbling cliffs in winter storms to keep her mother and brother off parish charity.
- *Markers:* `· WHO: Georges Cuvier (1769–1832) — Paris anatomist · WHEN: Paris, 1796 · WHEN: Lyme Regis, 19 August 1800 · WHO: Richard Anning (1766–1810) — cabinetmaker and collector · WHEN: November 1810 · FACT: Survived a lightning strike · DATA: animated timeline building 1796 · 1800 · 1810 · 1811–1812 · SCALE: £23 for the 1812 skeleton — about half a year's labourer wages`
- **Ends on:** One winter morning after a storm in 1811, Joseph spotted a four-foot skull in the shale. What was waiting inside the cliff behind it?

### PART 03: The Seventeen-Foot Fish-Lizard (3:05–4:55) — `explain`
- **Cold open:** Late **1811**. Fourteen-year-old **Joseph Anning** uncovers a four-foot skull with jaws like a crocodile and eyes as big as dinner plates, armoured with a ring of overlapping bony plates.
- Over the next months into **1812**, twelve-year-old **Mary Anning** traces the spine back into the Blue Lias clay with her hammer and chisels until quarrymen help lift out a **17-foot (5.2-metre)** skeleton.
- Why was it so bewildering? An explainer diagram on period paper shows that it combined pieces never seen together in any living animal: the snout of a dolphin, the teeth of a crocodile, the vertebrae of a fish, and four swimming paddles like a whale's flippers. It was later named ***Ichthyosaurus*** — the "fish-lizard".
- Local manor lord **Henry Hoste Henley** buys it for **£23** — about half a year's wages for a farm labourer — and sells it to Bullock's Museum in London. In **1814**, Royal Society anatomist **Sir Everard Home** publishes the first scientific paper on the skeleton (followed by five more up to 1820) without once printing Mary or Joseph Anning's name!
- By **1820**, after a barren year on the cliffs, the Annings are about to lose their furniture for rent until family friend **Colonel Thomas Birch** auctions his own fossil collection in London on **15 May 1820**, raising **£400** to save them.
- *Markers:* `· WHO: Henry Hoste Henley — lord of the manor · WHO: Sir Everard Home (1756–1832) — London surgeon · WHEN: London, 1814 · WHO: Thomas Birch — fossil collector · WHEN: 15 May 1820 · DATA: inked anatomy chart of the Ichthyosaurus (dolphin snout, bony eye ring, fish spine, 4 paddles) + £23 vs £400 auction scale · SCALE: eye rings as wide as dinner plates · FACT: Not named in the paper`
- **Ends on:** Then, in December 1823, Mary uncovered a skeleton so impossible that Georges Cuvier himself thought she had faked it.

### PART 04: The Fake That Proved Extinction — Midpoint Twist (4:55–6:40) — `awe`
- **Cold open:** **10 December 1823**. Twenty-four-year-old Mary Anning chips out a complete **9-foot** skeleton of ***Plesiosaurus*** ("near to lizard") — four broad paddles, a stubby tail, a tiny head, and a neck coiled like a serpent with **35 vertebrae**.
- **Midpoint twist ("Case closed? Wrong."):** When a drawing of the skeleton reaches **Georges Cuvier** in Paris at the end of **1823**, he suspects the 35-vertebra neck is a fraud — a sea-serpent spine cemented onto another fossil to trick wealthy collectors!
- Rivalry / verification beat (back-and-forth portrait cuts between Cuvier in Paris and **William Conybeare** & **William Buckland** in England): Conybeare and Buckland send detailed anatomical drawings proving every vertebra was locked in undisturbed shale.
- On **20 February 1824**, Conybeare reads the landmark paper on *Plesiosaurus* to a packed room at the **Geological Society of London**. Cuvier admits his mistake in writing, praises the discovery, and begins buying fossils from Mary Anning himself!
- The bittersweet irony: on that triumphant night of **20 February 1824**, Mary Anning was not allowed inside the Geological Society of London — women were barred even as guests (and could not become Fellows until **1919**).
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: 10 December 1823 · WHO: William Conybeare (1787–1857) — geologist · WHO: William Buckland (1784–1856) — Oxford geologist · WHEN: London, 20 February 1824 · FACT: Cuvier suspected a forgery`
- **Ends on:** Skeletons showed what these creatures looked like when they died. How did Mary Anning work out what they ate when they were alive?

### PART 05: Stones That Were Ancient Meals (6:40–8:20) — `explain` → `awe`
- **Cold open:** Inside the ribs of her ichthyosaurs, Mary keeps noticing strange, spiral-ribbed grey stones locals call "bezoar stones".
- In **1824**, she cracks them open with her hammer: inside are fossilised fish scales, squid hooks, and the crushed bones of smaller ichthyosaurs. By **1829**, working with **William Buckland** (who names them **coprolites**), she proves they are fossilised droppings — letting scientists draw the world's first prehistoric food chain!
- Alongside them, she finds fossilised **belemnite ink sacs**; when ground with water, the **200-million-year-old** dried ink is still dark enough for her friends to sketch the very fossils it came from.
- **The misconception explained away:** Were Mary Anning's ichthyosaurs and plesiosaurs swimming dinosaurs? No — an inked explainer diagram shows that dinosaurs walked on land with legs straight beneath their hips, while ichthyosaurs and plesiosaurs were distinct marine reptiles that ruled the seas at the same time.
- When Mary Anning dies on **9 March 1847** at age forty-seven, the Geological Society of London — which still barred women — publishes a eulogy in its President's annual address, and in **2010** the Royal Society names her among the ten most influential women in British science history.
- **After-act hand-off:** Once Mary Anning's cliffs proved whole worlds of giant reptiles could vanish forever, two new mysteries opened up: how new species arise to take their place (hand-off to `013` *Darwin & Wallace*), and what kind of catastrophe could wipe out the dinosaurs across the entire planet at once (hand-off to `010` *Alvarez Clay Line*).
