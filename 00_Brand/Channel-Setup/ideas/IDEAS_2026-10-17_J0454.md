- **Person & room:** Twenty-seven-year-old mathematician **Ada Lovelace** (1815–1852) and inventor **Charles Babbage** (1791–1871) in **London in August 1843**, poring over brass gearwheels and Jacquard-loom punched cards as Lovelace writes **Note G** (the world's first published computer algorithm, calculating Bernoulli numbers on a machine that hasn't even been built yet!); cut to 23-year-old Cambridge fellow **Alan Turing** (1912–1954) at **King's College, Cambridge, in May 1936** imagining an infinite paper tape reading `0` and `1`, and in **Hut 8 at Bletchley Park on 18 March 1940** as the first electromechanical **Bombe ("Victory")** ticks through **158,962,555,217,826,360,000** (`159 quintillion`) Nazi Enigma settings by hunting for a single logical contradiction!
- **Date & proof:** For centuries, a "computer" wasn't a machine — it was a human clerk sitting at a desk calculating tables of navigation and artillery numbers by hand, full of fatal arithmetic typos (prompting Charles Babbage to cry out in **1821**, *"I wish to God these calculations had been executed by steam!"*). Between **1834 and 1843**, Babbage designed a steam-powered brass **Analytical Engine** separated into a **"Mill"** (the processor/CPU) and a **"Store"** (memory), fed by **Jacquard silk-loom punched cards** with conditional branching (`if/then` jumps and loops). In **August 1843**, **Ada Lovelace** (Lord Byron's daughter, whose mother schooled her in mathematics to keep her from becoming a poet!) translated Italian engineer Luigi Menabrea's French paper on the engine and added seven **Notes (A–G) nearly three times longer than the paper itself**. In **Note G**, she wrote out a step-by-step table showing how the engine would compute **Bernoulli numbers ($B_7$)** using nested loops and variables — the **world's first published computer program**, written a full century before the first electronic computer was switched on — and made the prophetic leap Babbage himself missed: because punched cards represent abstract patterns, the engine could manipulate **musical notes, letters, and logic**, not just numbers!
- **Twist (three-part):**

Turing wasn't trying to build a practical office machine at all — he was trying to answer German mathematician **David Hilbert's** pure-logic question ("Can a mechanical rule decide whether any mathematical statement is provable?"). To prove the answer was **No** (the **Halting Problem**: there are mathematical truths no algorithm can ever decide), Turing imagined a simple machine reading symbols off an infinite strip of paper tape — and proved that **one single "Universal Computing Machine"** reading instructions stored as data on its own tape can simulate *every* other machine ever conceived!

Building on the heroic pre-war breakthrough of Polish mathematician **Marian Rejewski** (1905–1980, who used permutation group theory and the mechanical *bomba* in **1932–1938** to crack early Enigma before Germany added extra rotors and plugboard cables), Turing and **Gordon Welchman** (1906–1985) at **Bletchley Park** exploited two fatal flaws: German operators sent predictable weather/header phrases (**"cribs"**, like `WETTERVORHERSAGE`), and **Enigma's reflector meant a letter could NEVER encrypt to itself** (`A` could never become `A`)! Using electric closed loops that rippled through 36 Enigma-equivalents at once, Turing's electromechanical **Bombe** (first installed **18 March 1940**, upgraded with Welchman's **diagonal board**) eliminated quadrillions of contradictory settings in a flash, finding the daily key in **under 20 minutes**!

### 2. Blood Types & Safe Transfusions: Why Blood Clumps — Karl Landsteiner's 1900 Vienna Blood-Mixing Experiment & Richard Lewisohn's 1915 Sodium Citrate (recommended for 028; drafted in `028_Landsteiner-Blood-Types/`)
- **Familiar thing:** a blood-donor bag labelled `O−`, `A+`, or `AB+`, and the routine hospital blood transfusions that save millions of surgery and trauma patients every year.
- **Person & room:** Thirty-one-year-old Austrian physician and pathologist **Karl Landsteiner** (1868–1943) at the **Institute of Pathological Anatomy, University of Vienna**, in **February 1900 – November 1901**, drawing small vials of blood from himself and five laboratory colleagues, separating the clear golden serum from the red cells in a hand-cranked centrifuge, and mixing drops in a **6 × 6 grid on glass slides**; cut earlier to French physician **Jean-Baptiste Denys** (1643–1704) in **Paris on 15 June 1667** and **December 1667** transfusing sheep and calf blood through silver quills into human veins; and German-born surgeon **Richard Lewisohn** (1875–1961) at **Mount Sinai Hospital in New York in January 1915** adding a pinch of **0.2% sodium citrate** so donated blood could be stored in bottles in an icebox!
- **Date & proof:** After **William Harvey** (`005`) proved in **1628** that blood circulates in a closed loop, seventeenth-century doctors in London and Paris immediately tried to replace lost blood. On **15 June 1667**, King Louis XIV's physician **Jean-Baptiste Denys** transfused **9 ounces of lamb's blood** into a feverish 15-year-old boy (who miraculously survived because the amount was tiny). Yet in **December 1667**, when Denys transfused calf's blood into **Antoine Mauroy**, the patient suffered violent back pain, black urine (shattered red blood cells!), and died — leading France, England, and the Pope to **ban blood transfusions for over 150 years**! Even when British obstetrician **James Blundell** (1790–1878) insisted in **1818–1829** on using only *human* blood for haemorrhaging mothers, **nearly half of all human-to-human transfusions still killed the patient**, turning their blood into fatal sticky clumps inside their veins! Doctors assumed the clumping (**agglutination**) was caused by a disease or bacterial infection (`001`). Then, in a **seven-line footnote in February 1900** and a landmark paper on **14 November 1901** in Vienna, **Karl Landsteiner** tested a simple question: what happens if you mix the blood of **six completely healthy scientists** (including his own blood) in every combination? Instead of mixing smoothly, healthy human blood clumped into brick-red clots in specific rows and columns — revealing **three distinct human blood groups: A, B, and C (later renamed O)** (with **AB** discovered the next year, **1902**, by his students **Alfred von Decastello** and **Adriano Sturli**)!
- **Twist (three-part):**
  1. **Healthy blood carries built-in "lock and key" weapons against other healthy blood:** Landsteiner proved that clumping wasn't a disease at all: red blood cells carry surface markers (**A or B antigens**, while **O** has neither), and your plasma naturally carries **antibodies** against whichever marker you lack! Give Type A blood to a Type B patient, and the patient's antibodies lock onto the donor cells and shatter them inside the bloodstream — whereas **Type O ("universal donor" red cells)** carries no A or B antigens to trigger an attack!
  2. **Why nobody used Landsteiner's discovery for a decade — blood clotted in the tube in 6 minutes! (1901–1915):** Even after Landsteiner solved why mismatched blood clumped, hospitals *still* couldn't keep blood banks! Why?
