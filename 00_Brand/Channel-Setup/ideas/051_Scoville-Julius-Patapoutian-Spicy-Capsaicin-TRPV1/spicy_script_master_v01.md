Your tongue's taste buds detect five basic tastes: sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and savoury umami. Why isn't "spicy" one of them?

In **eighteen seventy-six**, British pharmaceutical chemist **John Clough Thresh** extracted the fiery compound from cayenne peppers as pure colourless crystals and named it **capsaicin** — noting that if even a microscopic speck vaporised in his lab, everyone started coughing and his tongue burned for hours! As chemists proved in **nineteen nineteen**, capsaicin has a **long greasy hydrocarbon tail**, so drinking cold water after eating a chili only smears the oil around your mouth like motor oil on wet hands — whereas **casein** protein in milk wraps around the greasy tail like soap and washes it away!

In **May nineteen twelve**, American pharmacist **Wilbur Lincoln Scoville** at the Parke-Davis company in Detroit needed a way to standardise chili extracts for muscle-warming liniments. Because chemical dye tests weren't sensitive enough, Scoville used the **human tongue**! He diluted drops of alcohol pepper extract into larger and larger flasks of **sweetened sugar water** until a panel of **five tasters** could barely feel a tingle — testing only one sample at a time so their mouths wouldn't go numb!

On Scoville's scale, a bell pepper scores **zero**, a jalapeño about **five thousand**, a habanero **three hundred thousand**, and pure capsaicin a staggering **sixteen million Scoville Heat Units**!

Why could one part in sixteen million set human nerves on fire — and how did two scientists in California catch the mystery door on camera?

From **nineteen forty-seven to nineteen sixty** in Hungary, pharmacologist **Miklós Jancsó** proved that capsaicin doesn't affect touch or pressure nerves at all — it specifically fires, and then exhausts, the pain nerves that sense **burning heat**.

Case closed — was capsaicin simply burning cells like acid? Wrong! For fifty years, no one could find the molecular lock on our pain nerves. Then, at the University of California, San Francisco, physiologist **David Julius** and postdoctoral fellow **Michael Caterina** built a genetic dragnet!

Knowing that pain neurons react to capsaicin while embryonic **kidney cells** do not, they extracted **fourteen thousand genes** from pain nerves, inserted them into dishes of kidney cells loaded with a **calcium-glowing dye**, and added a drop of capsaicin under a microscope. Dish after dish stayed pitch dark — until **Pool Number Eleven** lit up like a Christmas tree as calcium ions flooded in!

Isolating that single gene on the **twenty-third of October nineteen ninety-seven**, Julius and Caterina unveiled the **`TRPV1` ion channel**. Then came the real twist: why would humans evolve a receptor for a chili pepper? They took a fresh dish of `TRPV1` cells, added **zero chili pepper**, and warmed the water bath. At forty degrees, nothing happened. The instant the water crossed **forty-three degrees Celsius** — the exact threshold where heat starts damaging human skin — **the `TRPV1` door snapped wide open**!

Capsaicin simply wedges that forty-three-degree heat door open at normal body temperature! What about the icy chill of **peppermint** — and why don't **birds** feel chili burn at all?

Normally, **`TRPM8`** stays shut at body temperature and snaps open when your tongue cools below **twenty-six degrees Celsius**. When **peppermint menthol** binds to `TRPM8`, it shifts that threshold warmer — tricking your nerves into telling your brain your mouth is **freezing cold**!
