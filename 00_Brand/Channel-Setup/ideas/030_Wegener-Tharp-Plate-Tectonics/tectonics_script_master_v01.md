At Christmas in December nineteen ten, thirty-year-old German meteorologist and record-setting balloonist Alfred Wegener was paging through a friend's new world atlas when he noticed something striking: if you look below the waves at the two-hundred-metre continental shelves, South America and Africa lock together across four thousand five hundred kilometres with almost no gap at all!

Wegener asked a simple question: what if you hold two pieces of torn newspaper edge to edge, and every single printed line of words reads straight across the tear? You cannot call that a coincidence!

On the sixth of January, nineteen twelve, in Frankfurt — and in his nineteen fifteen book coining the name **Pangaea**, meaning "All-Earth" — Wegener showed that Earth's geological lines read straight across the Atlantic Ocean. The Appalachian Mountains of North America end at the cliffs of Newfoundland and pick right back up in the mountains of Scotland and Norway. Worse for the textbook orthodoxy, skeletons of *Mesosaurus* — a one-metre freshwater reptile that could never swim across five thousand kilometres of salty ocean — turn up only in matching black shales in southern Brazil and South Africa, alongside fossil leaves of the fern *Glossopteris* and tropical coal beds frozen under Arctic ice!

However, Wegener made one fatal mechanical mistake. He proposed that lighter granite continents ploughed directly through the solid basalt rock of the ocean floor like icebreakers through sea ice, pushed by Earth's spin and the Moon's gravitational tides.

In nineteen twenty-four, Cambridge geophysicist Harold Jeffreys calculated that tidal forces were ten billion times too weak to push a continent through solid basalt — like trying to blow a brick wall through concrete! At a fiery symposium in New York on the fifteenth of November, nineteen twenty-six, geologist Rollin Chamberlin scoffed that believing Wegener meant forgetting seventy years of geology.

Four years later, in November nineteen thirty, just days after his fiftieth birthday, Wegener led a desperate ski rescue across the Greenland ice cap in minus fifty-four degrees Celsius cold to bring food to his stranded weather station. He saved his men, but collapsed and died on the ice on the return trek — buried beneath his skis while his theory was written off as dead.

So how *do* continents move if they cannot plough through the seabed — and why did the answer stay hidden three miles underwater until a woman barred from ships drew the bottom of the Atlantic?

Until the nineteen fifties, scientists knew more about the face of the Moon than the bottom of Earth's oceans, which textbooks pictured as a flat, muddy plain.

Starting in nineteen forty-seven, Columbia University oceanographer Bruce Heezen criss-crossed the Atlantic aboard research ships, bouncing sonar sound pings off the seabed and recording the echo times on miles of paper tape.

Because superstitious maritime rules barred women from working aboard research ships, geologist and mathematician Marie Tharp stayed behind in a basement drafting room in New York. For weeks on end, she translated tens of thousands of sonar numbers by hand into six sweeping east-to-west depth profiles across the North Atlantic floor.

In Autumn nineteen fifty-two, as Tharp lined up all six profiles, you can imagine what she felt when she spotted a unmistakable pattern: running straight down the crest of the sixteen-thousand-kilometre Mid-Atlantic Ridge was a deep, steep-walled V-shaped canyon — one and a half to three kilometres deep and up to forty-eight kilometres wide — a giant tension crack where the ocean floor was being ripped apart from below!

When she showed the V-shaped rift valley to Bruce Heezen, he groaned and dismissed it for a whole year as **"girl talk"**, protesting: *"It cannot be. It looks too much like continental drift!"* He even ordered her to erase and redraw the map!

Then, in nineteen fifty-three, at the very next desk, Heezen asked a young draftsman named Howard Foster to plot the epicentres of thousands of underwater earthquakes on a transparent sheet of plastic. When they laid Foster's transparent earthquake map over Marie Tharp's redrawn rift valley, the line of earthquake dots ran dead-centre down her V-shaped canyon — tracing a sixty-five-thousand-kilometre volcanic seam around the entire planet like the stitching on a baseball!

Published on their famous nineteen fifty-seven ocean-floor map, Tharp's rift was real. Case closed? Wrong. If brand-new rock is erupting along a sixty-five-thousand-kilometre crack and Earth isn't getting any bigger, why doesn't the planet swell up like a balloon — and what proved the seabed is a moving conveyor belt?

In November nineteen sixty-two, Princeton geologist and former World War Two naval captain Harry Hess revived British geologist Arthur Holmes's nineteen twenty-nine idea of churning heat in Earth's mantle to solve Wegener's mechanical riddle once and for all.

Continents do *not* plough through the ocean floor! Instead, Hess realised, hot rock rises from deep in the mantle underneath Marie Tharp's mid-ocean rift, erupts as fresh basalt lava, and moves outward to both sides like two giant conveyor belts — carrying the continents along inside the plates as passive passengers! And why doesn't Earth expand? Because at deep ocean trenches around the Pacific, cold, heavy old ocean floor dives back down into the mantle to be melted and recycled!

But how could anyone prove solid seabed rock was really moving?

During the nineteen fifties, ships towing magnetic sensors designed to hunt submarines found baffling zebra-like stripes of normal and reversed magnetism across the ocean floor. On the seventh of September, nineteen sixty-three, in *Nature*, twenty-four-year-old Cambridge student Fred Vine and his supervisor Drummond Matthews — alongside Canadian geophysicist Lawrence Morley — put two facts together.

First, Earth's magnetic field flips north and south every few hundred thousand years. Second, when molten volcanic basalt erupts along Marie Tharp's rift and cools below five hundred and eighty degrees Celsius, its tiny iron crystals freeze in the direction of Earth's magnetic field like millions of tiny compass needles!

As the seafloor splits and spreads to both sides, each magnetic flip prints a mirror-image pair of magnetic stripes on the left and right of the ridge — a fossil tape recording that proved the oceans are spreading and showed that while continents are up to four billion years old, no ocean floor on Earth is older than two hundred million years!

Vine and Matthews's magnetic barcode vindicated Alfred Wegener thirty-three years after he froze to death in Greenland. So does that mean the continents float on an underground sea of liquid red-hot magma?

Many people picture Earth's tectonic plates floating on a underground ocean of liquid red-hot magma. Is that true?

No! Earthquake waves prove that Earth's mantle is nearly ninety-nine percent solid rock. Under immense heat and pressure deep underground, solid rock creeps slowly over millions of years the way glacier ice flows downhill — while rigid plates carrying both continents and seabed ride on top, tracked to the millimetre today by GPS satellites!

And why do lighter granite continents — at two point seven grams per cubic centimetre — ride high above the sea for four billion years without sinking, while denser basalt ocean plates at three grams per cubic centimetre sink back into the mantle?

Because continents literally float in denser rock by the exact same physical law that keeps a four-thousand-tonne ship afloat in water: **buoyancy**! And how did we discover the law of buoyancy in the first place — and why did twenty-two-year-old Galileo Galilei prove in fifteen eighty-six that the famous story of Archimedes catching a crooked goldsmith by measuring spilled bathwater from a brimming pot was physically impossible?

Two halves of a torn newspaper, a pair of skis in the Greenland snow, and a woman with an ink pen who wasn't allowed on a ship. Together, they proved the ground beneath our feet is alive.
