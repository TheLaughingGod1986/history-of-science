### 1. Invisible Light: William Herschel's thermometer past the rainbow & Johann Wilhelm Ritter's silver chloride (recommended for 018)
- **Familiar thing:** the warmth of sunlight on your skin through a window, a TV remote or thermal camera, and sunscreen blocking invisible ultraviolet rays at the beach.
- **Person & room:** Hanoverian-born musician turned astronomer **William Herschel** (1738–1822) and his sister and astronomical partner **Caroline Herschel** (1750–1848) in a darkened ground-floor room in **Slough, England**, on **11 February 1800**, with a glass prism mounted in a window shutter casting a solar rainbow across a wooden board holding three mercury thermometers with blackened bulbs; paired with 24-year-old romantic physicist **Johann Wilhelm Ritter** (1776–1810) in **Jena, Germany**, on **22 February 1801**, and polymath **Thomas Young** (1773–1829) at the Royal Institution (**1801–1803**).
- **Date & proof:** For over a century after **Isaac Newton** split sunlight with a prism in **1666**, everyone assumed the seven visible colours of the rainbow *were* the entire spectrum of light. In early **1800**, **William Herschel** wasn't looking for invisible rays at all: he was testing differently coloured dark glass filters on his telescopes to see which colour transmitted the least solar heat while he observed sunspots! Curious why red filters felt scorching hot while green filters stayed cool, on **11 February 1800** Herschel passed a beam of sunlight through a prism onto a table, cut a slit in a cardboard screen to isolate one colour at a time, and measured the temperature rise on mercury thermometers: violet rose **2°F**, green **3.25°F**, and deep red **6.875°F (nearly 7°F)** in ten minutes. Then Herschel moved his thermometer into the **pitch-dark shadow half an inch beyond the red end of the rainbow** — where no visible light fell at all. Instead of cooling back to room temperature, the thermometer in the dark soared **9°F (5°C) in 16 minutes** (peaking **half an inch past red**)! He had discovered **infrared radiation** ("calorific rays").
- **Twist (three-part):**
  1. **The opposite end of the dark (22 February 1801):** News of Herschel's invisible heat rays reached 24-year-old **Johann Wilhelm Ritter** in Jena, who believed nature always works in polarities: if an invisible *heating* ray sits past red, an invisible *chemical* ("de-oxidising") ray must sit in the dark past violet! On **22 February 1801**, using Swedish chemist **Carl Wilhelm Scheele's** observation (`1777`) that violet light darkens white **silver chloride ($\text{AgCl}$)** paper faster than red light, Ritter placed strips of silver chloride paper in the dark shadow *beyond the violet end* of a prism's rainbow — and watched the paper turn jet-black faster than anywhere in the visible spectrum, discovering **ultraviolet light**!
  2. **The midpoint twist — Herschel convinces himself his own rays are NOT light (April – November 1800):** In his first papers (**March–April 1800**), Herschel proved that his invisible infrared rays reflect off mirrors and refract through glass lenses just like visible light. Case closed? **Wrong!** Between **May and November 1800**, Herschel ran over 200 experiments measuring how clear glass, coloured glass, water and rock salt block heat rays vs visible light. Because clear glass blocked most of the heat from a candle or hot iron poker while letting 100% of the visible light through, Herschel concluded in **November 1800** that radiant heat and light were **two completely different kinds of rays**!
  3. **The final resolution & everyday scale:** It took **Thomas Young's** two-slit wave interference experiment (**1801–1803**, measuring red light at `~700 nm` and violet at `~400 nm`), **Macedonio Melloni's** rock-salt thermopile (**1831–1835**), and **James Clerk Maxwell's** electromagnetic equations (**1865**) to prove that infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, radio waves and X-rays are all the exact same electromagnetic wave — and that every human eye is blind to **more than 99.99% of the electromagnetic spectrum** (seeing only the tiny one-octave sliver from **380 to 750 nanometres**)!

### 2. Sparks of Life & the First Battery: Luigi Galvani's twitching frog legs & Alessandro Volta's pile of coins
- **Familiar thing:** the battery inside your phone, and the electrical nerve impulses firing through your brain and muscles right now.
- **Person & room:** Anatomist **Luigi Galvani** (1737–1798) and his wife and laboratory collaborator **Lucia Galeazzi Galvani** (1743–1788) in their anatomy laboratory in **Bologna, Italy**, on **6 November 1780** and on their iron balcony railing on **20 September 1786**; cut back-and-forth against physicist **Alessandro Volta** (1745–1827) at the University of Pavia (**1792–20 March 1800**), putting bimetallic strips on his own **tongue** and stacking zinc and silver discs soaked in brine.
- **Date & twist:** On **6 November 1780**, Galvani and Lucia Galeazzi noticed that a dissected frog's leg on their table kicked violently when touched by a steel scalpel while an electrostatic machine sparked across the room. Six years later (**20 September 1786**), Galvani hung frog legs from **brass hooks** on an **iron balcony railing** on a calm, cloudless day — and watched them twitch whenever the brass hook touched the iron bar! Galvani published in **1791** that living creatures run on innate **"animal electricity."** Fellow Italian physicist **Alessandro Volta** thought that was nonsense: he insisted the frog leg was just a wet detector and the electricity came from **two different metals** (brass and iron) touching through a salty liquid! To prove Galvani wrong without using a single frog, Volta tested pairs of coins on the tip of his own **tongue**, and in **March 1800** stacked alternating discs of **silver and zinc** separated by brine-soaked cardboard — inventing the **electric battery (the Voltaic pile)** and the first continuous electric current in history! **The double twist:** both rivals were right and both were wrong — Volta invented the battery by proving dissimilar metals generate current, while Galvani (via his nephew Giovanni Aldini and **Emil du Bois-Reymond** in **1848**) had genuinely discovered the **nerve action potential** (`-70 mV to +40 mV`) after proving in **1794** that a frog leg twitches when touched by another severed nerve with *zero metal at all*!

### 3. Penicillin & Antibiotics: Fleming's mouldy dish & the Oxford bedpan factory

- **Date & twist:** Everyone knows Fleming returned from holiday on **3 September 1928** and saw a stray blob of *Penicillium notatum* mould dissolving *Staphylococcus* bacteria on an unwashed Petri dish (`"That's funny"`). The twist: Fleming couldn't purify the unstable broth and **gave up on penicillin as a medicine by 1931**! ### 4. Radioactivity: Henri Becquerel's cloudy-day drawer & Marie Curie's freezing shed
- **Familiar thing:** a Geiger counter, nuclear power, or carbon-dating ancient bones and rocks.
- **Person & room:** **Henri Becquerel** (1852–1908) at the Muséum National d'Histoire Naturelle in Paris during a week of overcast skies (**26 February – 1 March 1896**), and **Marie Skłodowska Curie** (1867–1934) and **Pierre Curie** (1859–1906) in a leaky former medical-school dissecting shed on Rue Lhomond in Paris (**1898–1902**).
- **Date & twist:** Inspired by Röntgen's X-rays (`003`), Becquerel thought uranium salts needed **bright sunlight** to fluoresce and emit penetrating rays. When heavy clouds covered Paris on **26 February 1896**, he tucked his uranium rock and a paper-wrapped photographic plate into a **pitch-dark desk drawer** with a copper Maltese cross between them. On **1 March 1896**, expecting a blank plate because there had been no sun, he developed it anyway — and found the silhouette of the cross burned black at full strength! The energy wasn't reflected sunlight; it was coming from *inside the atom itself*. Then doctoral student **Marie Curie** measured uranium rays with Pierre's piezoelectric electrometer, discovered that raw **pitchblende ore was four times more radioactive than pure uranium**, and boiled down **several tonnes** of brown ore residue by hand in a freezing shed to isolate **polonium (July 1898)** and **radium (December 1898)** — elements so fiercely radioactive that her notebooks still require a lead-lined box today!

### 5. Electromagnetism: Ørsted's twitching compass & Michael Faraday's cup of mercury

- **Date & twist:** On **21 April 1820**, Ørsted connected a Voltaic battery during a lecture and watched a compass needle twitch sideways. There they go!"* His famous mentor **Sir Humphry Davy** grew so jealous that he accused Faraday of plagiarism and cast the **sole blackball vote against** Faraday's election to the Royal Society in **1824** — only for Faraday, ten years later (**29 August 1831**), to push a magnet through a coil of wire and invent the electric generator!
