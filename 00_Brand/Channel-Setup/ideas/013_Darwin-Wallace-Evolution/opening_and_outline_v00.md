### PART 02: The Birds He Forgot to Label (1:20–2:40) — `awe` → `explain`
- **Cold open (quirky anecdote before the hard idea):** **September 1835**, the Galápagos Islands. Whenever you hear about Darwin's finches, you probably picture a sudden flash of genius on the beach. In reality, twenty-six-year-old Charles Darwin thought his birds were blackbirds, wrens and gross-beaks — and he didn't even bother to write down which island most of them came from!
  - Even worse, the crew of **HMS *Beagle*** stacked dozens of giant Galápagos tortoises on deck for food and threw their shells overboard, before the islands' vice-governor **Nicholas Lawson** mentioned he could tell which island any tortoise came from just by looking at the shape of its shell.
  - Back in London in **March 1837**, bird expert **John Gould** examines Darwin's skins and drops a bombshell: they aren't blackbirds or wrens at all. They are **13 distinct species of finch** found nowhere else on Earth, each with a beak shaped like a different tool — heavy pliers for cracking hard seeds, long forceps for probing cactus flowers.
  - Darwin has to beg shipmates (including Captain **Robert FitzRoy** and his servant **Syms Covington**) for their properly labelled birds to reconstruct which beak lived on which island!
  - In **July 1837**, Darwin opens his red-leather ***Notebook B***, writes two words at the top of page 36 — **"I think"** — and draws a rough stick-tree showing one ancestral species branching into thirteen.
  - *Markers:* `· WHEN: Galápagos Islands, September 1835 · WHO: Nicholas Lawson (1790–1851) — Galápagos vice-governor · WHEN: London, March 1837 · WHO: John Gould (1804–1881) — London ornithologist · WHO: Robert FitzRoy (1805–1865) — Beagle captain · DATA: inked comparison of 13 finch species across 4 beak tool shapes (seed-crusher, bud-biter, insect-grasper, cactus-prober) · SCALE: 13 species from 1 ancestor — like 13 workshop tools forged from one pair of pliers · FACT: Forgot to label his finches`
- **Ends on:** He could see *that* one species branched into many — but what was the invisible knife that carved each beak to fit its island?

### PART 03: The Engine and the Barnacles (2:40–4:05) — `explain`
- **Cold open:** **28 September 1838**, London. Darwin picks up a book about human population by economist **Thomas Robert Malthus** "for amusement" — and the missing mechanism clicks into place.
  - **The mechanism (Explainer diagram on period paper):** Every plant and animal produces far more offspring than food can support. Even a slow-breeding pair of elephants, Darwin calculates, would leave **15 million descendants** in five hundred years if every calf survived.
  - Because siblings vary slightly by chance — a slightly deeper beak in a drought year when only hard, thick-shelled seeds remain — the birds with the deeper beaks survive to breed, and pass that beak on. Over thousands of generations, **natural selection** reshapes the species without anyone steering it.
  - **Why he hid it:** Darwin sees what happened when an anonymous 1844 book on evolution (*Vestiges of the Natural History of Creation*) was savaged by British scientists, and worries about hurting his deeply religious wife **Emma Darwin**.
  - In **July 1844**, he writes a **230-page essay**, seals it with a letter telling Emma to spend **£400** publishing it if he dies, and instead spends **eight full years (October 1846 – September 1854)** at Down House dissecting roughly **10,000 barnacles** until his own children ask a neighbour's child, *"Where does your father do his barnacles?"*
  - *Markers:* `· WHEN: 28 September 1838 · WHO: Thomas Robert Malthus (1766–1834) — British political economist · WHO: Emma Darwin (1808–1896) — Darwin's wife · WHEN: July 1844 · WHEN: Down House, 1846–1854 · DATA: inked Malthusian growth curve + seed-hardness vs beak-depth diagram + 8-year barnacle counter (10,000 specimens) · SCALE: 8 years on barnacles — nearly 3,000 days at one brass microscope · FACT: Spent 8 years on barnacles`
- **Ends on:** His friend **Charles Lyell** warns him in **May 1856** that someone else will beat him to print if he keeps waiting. Who was that someone — and where was he?

### PART 04: Fever on Ternate — Midpoint Twist (4:05–5:35) — `awe`
- **Cold open:** **February 1858**, the volcanic island of **Ternate** in the Maluku Islands (modern Indonesia). Thirty-five-year-old **Alfred Russel Wallace** — a self-taught surveyor who makes his living selling beetle and bird skins — lies wrapped in blankets in a bamboo hut, shaking with a malarial fever.
  - Unlike wealthy Darwin, Wallace had already lost four years of Amazon specimens when his ship ***Helen* burned and sank in the Atlantic in August 1852** — ten days in an open boat before setting out again for Southeast Asia, where he collected **125,660 specimens** over eight years.
  - Lying in his fever on Ternate, Wallace remembers the exact same book by **Malthus** — and before the chill passes, the entire mechanism of natural selection comes to him in two hours. Over the next two evenings he writes a 20-page paper (*On the Tendency of Varieties to Depart Indefinitely from the Original Type*) and posts it on the March 1858 mail steamer to the one famous naturalist in England who had been kind to him in letters: **Charles Darwin**.
  - **Midpoint Twist ("Case closed? Wrong."):** On **18 June 1858**, Darwin opens the packet at Down House and writes in anguish to **Charles Lyell**: *"All my originality, whatever it may amount to, will be smashed."*
  - Worse still, scarlet fever sweeps through Down House that very week, and on **28 June 1858** Darwin's eighteen-month-old son **Charles Waring Darwin** dies. Torn by grief and honour, Darwin refuses to suppress Wallace's paper and offers to send it to any journal Wallace chooses, even if he loses all priority.
  - *Markers:* `· WHO: Charles Lyell (1797–1875) — British geologist · WHEN: May 1856 · WHEN: August 1852 · WHEN: Malay Archipelago, 1854–1862 · WHEN: 18 June 1858 · WHEN: 28 June 1858 · DATA: animated timeline building 1835 (Galápagos) → 1837 ("I think" tree) → 1838 (Malthus) → 1844 (230-p essay) → 18 June 1858 (Wallace letter) · SCALE: 125,660 specimens collected by Wallace — enough to fill 100 museum cabinets · FACT: First specimens sank at sea`
- **Ends on:** Lyell and botanist **Joseph Hooker** refuse to let twenty years of Darwin's work vanish. When they read both men's papers to the Linnean Society on **1 July 1858**, how does the scientific world react?

### PART 05: The Night Nobody Noticed (5:35–6:55) — `awe` → `explain`
- **Cold open:** **1 July 1858**, Burlington House, Piccadilly, London. A hot summer evening at the **Linnean Society**.
  - **Joseph Dalton Hooker** and **Charles Lyell** present Wallace's Ternate essay alongside extracts from Darwin's **1844 essay** and an **October 1857 letter** to American botanist **Asa Gray** proving Darwin had reached the idea first.
  - Neither author is in the room: Darwin is in Kent burying his baby son; Wallace is in New Guinea and doesn't even know his paper has been read until months later!
  - And the reaction in the hall? **Dead silence.** No shouting, no debate. Ten months later, in **May 1859**, the Society's president **Thomas Bell** sums up the past year by announcing that **1858 had not been marked by any striking discovery that at all revolutionised science!**
  - Stung into action, Darwin drops his giant multi-volume draft and writes a readable "abstract" in thirteen months: ***On the Origin of Species***, published on **24 November 1859**. All **1,250 copies** printed by John Murray sell out to booksellers on the very first day.
  - And when Wallace reads the book in Indonesia, there is no bitter priority war — he writes home that Darwin has done the work far better than he ever could, and later even coins the book's title for the whole subject: ***Darwinism*** (**1889**).
  - *Markers:* `· WHEN: London, 1 July 1858 · WHO: Joseph Dalton Hooker (1817–1911) — Kew botanist · WHO: Asa Gray (1810–1888) — Harvard botanist · WHO: Thomas Bell (1792–1880) — Linnean Society president · WHEN: May 1859 · WHEN: 24 November 1859 · WHEN: 1889 (timeline completes) · DATA: inked comparison of 30 silent listeners at the 1 July 1858 reading vs 1,250 copies of Origin of Species sold out on day 1 (24 Nov 1859) · SCALE: 1,250 hardcover books — a stack taller than a five-storey London townhouse · FACT: 1858 called "no big discovery"`
- **Ends on:** Yet for the rest of his life, one flaw in his own theory haunted Darwin — and why was the answer already sitting unread on a shelf in Europe?

### PART 06: The Missing Piece & After-Act (6:55–8:00) — `explain` → `awe`
- **Cold open (the scariest objection, explained away):** In **June 1867**, Scottish engineer **Fleeming Jenkin** points out a fatal mathematical trap in Darwin's book. If traits blend like two pots of paint — as everyone in 1859 believed — then a single bird born with a better beak will mate with normal birds, halving the advantage in its children, quartering it in its grandchildren, and washing it out completely before natural selection can act!
  - Darwin agonises over this problem until his death in **1882**, never knowing that just one year earlier (**1866**), an Augustinian friar named **Gregor Mendel** had proved with **28,000 pea plants** that traits don't blend like paint at all — they travel as whole, unbroken packets that never wash out.
  - **The everyday misconception explained away:** And what about the phrase **"survival of the fittest"**? Darwin didn't even coin it (**Herbert Spencer** did in **1864**, and Wallace persuaded Darwin to add it in the 5th edition in **1869**) — and "fittest" never meant the strongest or fiercest animal, only the one that *fits* its environment best, like a key fitting a lock.
  - *Markers:* `· WHEN: June 1867 · WHO: Fleeming Jenkin (1833–1885) — Scottish professor of engineering · WHO: Gregor Mendel (1822–1884) — Augustinian friar · WHEN: 1866 · WHO: Herbert Spencer (1820–1903) — British philosopher · WHEN: 1864`
