Even after French chemists **Anselme Payen and Jean-François Persoz in eighteen thirty-three** extracted malt `diastase`, **Louis Pasteur in eighteen fifty-seven** proved that living yeast cells ferment sugar into alcohol — and insisted that reactions inside a cell required a mysterious **"living vital force"** that died the instant you broke the cell open (`in eighteen seventy-seven, Wilhelm Kühne coined the word "enzyme" — Greek for "in yeast"`).

Then in **eighteen ninety-four** in Berlin, organic chemist **Emil Fischer** tested artificial mirror-image sugars against yeast enzymes. An enzyme digested a right-handed sugar effortlessly, yet couldn't touch its left-handed mirror twin! Fischer wrote the most famous sentence in biochemistry: *"Enzyme and glucoside must fit each other **like lock and key** (*wie Schloss und Schlüssel*), in order to exert a chemical effect on each other."*

Two years later in Munich, **Eduard Buchner** and **Martin Hahn** ground **one kilogram of brewer's yeast** with **one kilogram of sharp quartz sand** and **two hundred grams of diatomaceous earth**, then squeezed the paste under **three hundred atmospheres** to get completely **cell-free juice**. To stop bacteria spoiling the dead juice, they added thick **cane sugar** — just like preserving fruit in jam. To Buchner's astonishment, streams of **carbon dioxide bubbles and ethyl alcohol** erupted from the dead liquid! On the **eleventh of January eighteen ninety-seven**, Buchner named the soluble catalyst **`zymase`** (`winning the nineteen oh-seven Nobel Prize`): life's chemistry needed **zero vital force**!

Buchner proved that invisible chemical locks inside cells drive the chemistry of life. But for the next thirty years, nobody knew **what an enzyme was actually made of**! Why did Germany's most powerful Nobel laureate mock a **one-armed American professor at Cornell in August nineteen twenty-six** when he crystallised an enzyme — and found it was **one hundred percent pure protein**?

Case closed — proteins are just passive structural jelly like egg white, so enzymes must be mysterious non-protein chemicals! Wrong! In **August nineteen twenty-six**, a one-armed chemist who had been told he could never work in a laboratory crystallised the first enzyme in history — and proved it was **one hundred percent pure protein**!

At age seventeen, **James Sumner** lost his left arm in a hunting accident; his professor told him a one-armed man could never do experimental chemistry. Undeterred, Sumner taught himself to pipette and filter with one hand, and in **nineteen seventeen** at Cornell set out to isolate a pure enzyme: **`urease`** from the **jack bean** (`which speeds up the breakdown of urea a hundred trillion times`). One morning in **August nineteen twenty-six**, after chilling jack-bean meal in aqueous acetone overnight, Sumner peered into his microscope and saw **glittering octahedral crystals**! Every test showed those crystals had massive `urease` activity — and were **pure globulin protein**!

Instead of applause, Sumner was attacked by German Nobel laureate **Richard Willstätter**, who had diluted enzyme solutions until his chemical tests couldn't detect any protein (`because enzymes are so potent that trace amounts still work long after old protein tests go blind!`). Sumner fought back until **John Howard Northrop** at the Rockefeller Institute crystallised stomach **`pepsin` on the twentieth of July nineteen thirty** and pancreatic **`trypsin` in nineteen thirty-two** — proving every single one was pure protein and winning **Sumner and Northrop the nineteen forty-six Nobel Prize**!

Wait — if an enzyme is just a rigid protein keyhole (`Fischer 1894`), why doesn't the target molecule just sit comfortably inside the keyhole forever? How does an enzyme actually **break a chemical bond**?

Why would an enzyme that fits an unstrained molecule too snugly be a terrible catalyst? Because in **nineteen forty-six**, **Linus Pauling** realised that to speed up a reaction, an enzyme's **`active site`** must not fit the relaxed starting molecule — it must fit the strained, halfway-broken **`transition state`**, pulling the molecule over the energy hill!

Then in **February nineteen fifty-eight** in *PNAS*, **Daniel Koshland** unveiled the **`Induced-Fit Theory`**: an enzyme isn't a rigid metal keyhole — it is a **flexible glove** that changes shape as the molecule enters, clamping down to swing its chemical claws into exact position!

On the **twenty-second of May nineteen sixty-five** in *Nature*, **David Phillips**, **Colin Blake**, and **Louise Johnson** at the Royal Institution in London used X-ray crystallography to map all **one hundred and twenty-nine amino acids** of **`lysozyme`** (`the antibacterial enzyme in human tears discovered by Alexander Fleming in nineteen twenty-two`) at **`two-angstrom` resolution** — the first three-dimensional atomic structure of an enzyme in history! Across the middle of the enzyme sat a deep cleft (`subsites A to F`) that grabs a bacterium's six-sugar cell-wall chain. As the cleft closes slightly (**`induced fit`**), it physically **bends the fourth sugar ring (`ring D`) out of its relaxed chair shape into a strained sofa shape**, while two amino-acid claws — **`glutamic acid thirty-five`** and **`aspartic acid fifty-two`** — snip the strained bond in two!

Enzymes explain how thousands of reactions race inside your cells every millisecond. But what keeps all those enzymes, sugars, and ions **inside** the cell instead of washing away — and how does a **soap-bubble skin just two molecules (`five nanometres`) thick (`the cell membrane`)** keep your cells alive?

Once **David Phillips** revealed what an enzyme's **active site** looks like in three dimensions in **May nineteen sixty-five**, he unlocked modern **drug design**: almost every medicine in a pharmacy works as a custom-cut key (**`an enzyme inhibitor`**) that slips into a target enzyme's keyhole and jams it shut — from **aspirin** plugging the pain enzyme `cyclooxygenase`, to **penicillin** jamming the enzyme bacteria use to weld their cell walls!

Inside every cell, thousands of enzymes work together in a bustling chemical city. Yet the wall that separates that living city from the outside world — the **cell membrane** — is a liquid fat film **just two molecules (`five nanometres`) thick**, twenty thousand times thinner than a sheet of paper! How did **Ernest Overton in eighteen ninety-five** and Dutch scientists **Evert Gorter and François Grendel on the first of April nineteen twenty-five** spread the fats from red blood cells onto a tray of water with a silk thread and discover the **`two-to-one` phospholipid bilayer**? A hydraulic press in Munich, glittering bean crystals in New York, and an X-ray beam in London. Together, they revealed the molecular locks that make life fast enough to live.
