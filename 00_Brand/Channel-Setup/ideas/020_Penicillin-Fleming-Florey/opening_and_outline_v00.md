### PART 02: "That's Funny" and the Ten-Year Shelf (1:25–3:05)
- **Cold open (quirky anecdote & period quote):** *"That's funny."*
- **London, 3 September 1928**. Forty-seven-year-old Scottish bacteriologist **Alexander Fleming** (1881–1955) returns to his tiny turret laboratory at **St Mary's Hospital, Paddington**, after a month-long August holiday with his family in Suffolk.
- Before leaving in August, Fleming had been studying **Staphylococcus aureus** — the golden round bacteria that cause boils, wound sepsis and fatal blood poisoning — and had stacked dozens of inoculated agar Petri dishes on a corner of his bench out of direct sunlight.
- While chatting with his former assistant **Daniel Merlin Pryce** (1902–1976), Fleming picks up several discarded dishes to submerge them in a shallow tray of **Lysol disinfectant**. Because the stack is taller than the disinfectant is deep, the top dishes stay dry!
- Looking at one dry plate, Fleming stops and says, *"That's funny."* Near the edge sits a velvety blue-green colony of mould — later identified as ***Penicillium notatum*** (*Penicillium rubens*). For a centimetre or two all around the mould, the thick yellow-white carpet of *Staphylococcus* colonies has become translucent and dissolved into ghosts!
- **Why Fleming had already failed once (the 1922 tears & mucus experiment):** Six years earlier (**1921–1922**), while suffering from a head cold, Fleming had dripped his own **nasal mucus** and **tears** onto a bacterial plate and discovered **lysozyme** — a natural enzyme that dissolved harmless airborne bacteria, but failed completely against deadly human pathogens like *Staphylococcus* and *Streptococcus*.
- This time is different: Fleming grows the blue-green mould in nutrient broth and finds that its golden filtrate — which he names **"penicillin"** on **7 March 1929** (publishing in the *British Journal of Experimental Pathology* in **June 1929**) — kills *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus*, pneumonia and diphtheria bacteria even when diluted **1 part in 800**, without harming human white blood cells!
- **The twist — why Fleming gave up:** Yet raw "mould juice" is mostly water and 99.9% impurities, and the active penicillin molecule decomposes within hours at room temperature or in acid. Not being a chemist, Fleming cannot concentrate or stabilise it. After a few inconclusive surface-dressing attempts, by **1931** Fleming stops trying to make penicillin into an injectable drug and uses it for the next decade merely as a lab weed-killer to keep unwanted bacteria out of his Petri dishes!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: London, 3 September 1928 · WHO: Merlin Pryce (1902–1976) — St Mary's assistant pathologist · WHEN: 1922 · WHEN: 7 March 1929 · WHEN: June 1929 · FACT: Diluted 1 in 800, still lethal · DATA: inked dilution bar chart on period paper showing Fleming's 1929 mould broth killing Staphylococcus aureus at 1:800 dilution while leaving human white blood cells unharmed (vs carbolic acid killing white blood cells faster than bacteria) · SCALE: 1 drop of mould broth in 800 drops of water — and it still stopped deadly Staphylococcus cold`
- **Ends on:** For ten years, the greatest medicine in human history sat forgotten in a dusty journal. Who finally pulled Fleming's paper off the library shelf on the eve of the Second World War — and why did they have to brew their medicine in hospital bedpans?

### PART 03: The Bedpan Factory and the Eight Mice (3:05–4:55)
- **Cold open:** **Oxford, England, autumn 1938 – May 1940**. At the Sir William Dunn School of Pathology, Australian professor **Howard Florey** (1898–1968) and German-Jewish refugee biochemist **Ernst Chain** (1906–1979) — who fled Berlin in 1933 with £10 in his pocket — are hunting through old journals for natural antibacterial substances when Chain stumbles across Fleming's **1929** paper.
- By sheer luck, a sub-culture of Fleming's original *Penicillium notatum* mould is already sitting in a glass tube right downstairs in the Oxford pathology department!
- **Norman Heatley's Rube Goldberg extraction tower & ceramic bedpans:** The chemical hurdle that defeated Fleming nearly defeats Chain too: penicillin dissolves in organic solvents only when acidic, but acid destroys it in minutes. Enter brilliant young biochemist **Norman Heatley** (1911–2004), who rigs up a chilled counter-current extraction machine out of glass pipes, doorbell buzzers, bath-overflow valves and milk churns that transfers penicillin from cold acidified broth into amyl acetate and back into neutral water before it can break down, freeze-drying it into a tiny pinch of brown powder!
- Because the mould only grows as a thin crust on the surface of shallow liquid — and wartime factories can't spare glass flasks — Heatley designs flat, rectangular **ceramic growing vessels modelled on hospital bedpans**, stacked by the hundreds across the laboratory walls tended by six **"Oxford Penicillin Girls"** (led by **Ruth Callow**).
- **Saturday, 25 May 1940 — the eight-mouse trial:** At 11:00 a.m., Florey's team injects **eight white mice** with **110 million virulent *Streptococcus* bacteria** (far above a lethal dose). Four control mice receive no treatment; four receive injections of brown Oxford penicillin powder.
- All night, Heatley stays in the lab watching the cages. By **3:28 a.m.** on Sunday **26 May 1940**, **all four untreated mice are dead**. All **four penicillin-treated mice are scampering around healthy and alive**! When Florey hears the news on the telephone, he says simply: *"It looks like a miracle."* (Published in *The Lancet*, **24 August 1940**.)
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: Oxford, 1938 · WHEN: 25 May 1940 · WHEN: 24 August 1940 · FACT: Brewed in ceramic bedpans · DATA: inked experimental outcome chart of the 25 May 1940 Oxford mouse trial (8 mice injected with 110 million Streptococcus pyogenes: 4 untreated controls 0/4 survived past 17 hours vs 4 penicillin-treated mice 4/4 survived) · SCALE: 110 million lethal bacteria injected into a mouse weighing less than an ounce — and every treated mouse lived`
- **Ends on:** It worked like magic in a twenty-gram mouse. One problem: a human weighs three thousand times more than a mouse. What happened when the Oxford team tried to save their first dying human patient — and ran out of medicine halfway through?

### PART 04: The Policeman's Urine and the Mouldy Cantaloupe — Midpoint Twist (4:55–6:40)
- **Cold open ("Case closed? Wrong."):** **Radcliffe Infirmary, Oxford, 12 February 1941**. Forty-three-year-old Oxford Reserve Constable **Albert Alexander** (1897–1941) is dying in agony: months earlier, a scratch on his face (from a rose thorn in his police-station garden) had festered into a runaway *Staphylococcus* and *Streptococcus* infection that spread through his scalp, lungs and shoulder, forcing surgeons to remove his left eye.
- On **12 February 1941**, Dr **Charles Fletcher** (1911–1995) injects Alexander with **200 milligrams** of Oxford penicillin, followed by **100 milligrams every three hours**.
- Within **24 hours**, Alexander's raging fever drops, his appetite returns, and the oozing abscesses begin to heal!
- **The desperate recycling & tragic twist:** Because the kidneys flush **80% of injected penicillin straight into the urine unchanged**, **Norman Heatley** bikes back and forth through the Oxford snow carrying bottles of Constable Alexander's **urine** back to the Dunn School to re-extract every precious milligram and inject it back into him!
- Even with recycled urine, after **five days** (`4.4 grams` total given), every single drop of penicillin in Britain is gone. The bacteria roar back, and on **15 March 1941**, Albert Alexander dies.
- **Spores in their coat linings & the Peoria cantaloupe (July 1941 – July 1943):** Determined that no patient should ever die for lack of supply again — and fearing a Nazi invasion of Britain — Florey, Chain and Heatley rub dormant *Penicillium* spores into the **woollen linings of their own coats** so the mould will survive if one of them escapes!
- In **July 1941**, Florey and Heatley fly to the United States and visit the **USDA Northern Regional Research Laboratory in Peoria, Illinois**. There, **Andrew Moyer** (1899–1959) and Heatley feed the mould **corn steep liquor** (a cheap waste by-product of Midwestern corn starch) and **lactose** inside deep, bubbling **submerged fermentation tanks**, boosting yields more than tenfold.
- Still, Fleming's surface-loving London mould (*Penicillium notatum*) hates growing underwater. So USDA mycologist **Kenneth Raper** (1908–1987) hires laboratory assistant **Mary Hunt** — nicknamed **"Moldy Mary"** — to scour Peoria's grocery shops for rotting fruit.
- In **July 1943**, Mary Hunt brings in a **golden-mouldy cantaloupe melon** from a Peoria fruit market. Its strain — ***Penicillium chrysogenum* NRRL 1951** — thrives in deep tanks and, after X-ray and ultraviolet irradiation at Carnegie and Wisconsin (`X-1612` and `Q-176`), produces **over 1,000 times more penicillin** than Fleming's original dish! By **6 June 1944 (D-Day)**, American and British vats have brewed **2.3 million doses** for Allied soldiers!

- **Ends on:** How does a humble bread-and-melon mould actually burst a deadly bacterium open while leaving your own cells completely untouched — and what terrifying warning did Fleming give in his 1945 Nobel lecture?

### PART 05: Why Penicillin Pops Bacteria Like Balloons & Fleming's 1945 Warning (6:40–8:15)
- **Cold open (popular misconception debunked):** People often think antibiotics work by "boosting your immune system" or fighting viruses like the common cold. Is that true?
- **No:** viruses don't even have cell walls, so penicillin does nothing to a cold or flu! And why doesn't penicillin poison *your* cells the way carbolic acid does?
- Because in **May 1945**, Oxford X-ray crystallographer **Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin** (1910–1994) solved the 3D atomic structure of penicillin and revealed its secret weapon: a strained, square four-atom ring called a **beta-lactam ring**.
- Every bacterium is pumped up to high internal osmotic pressure (up to **5 to 20 atmospheres**, higher than a racing bicycle tyre!) and held together by a mesh cage of **peptidoglycan** cell wall. Penicillin's beta-lactam ring jams the bacterial enzyme (**transpeptidase**) that cross-links that cage: as the bacterium tries to grow and divide, its weakened wall tears open and the cell **bursts like an over-inflated balloon** — while human cells, which have **no cell walls at all**, are left completely unharmed!
- **After-act hand-off (7:45–8:15):**
  - On **11 December 1945**, sharing the Nobel Prize with **Florey** and **Chain**, **Alexander Fleming** issued a chilling prophecy: if people take too small a dose of penicillin, the surviving bacteria will evolve **antibiotic resistance** (`013` Darwinian natural selection in fast-forward!).
  - And how did Dorothy Hodgkin see the exact positions of invisible atoms inside a penicillin crystal in 1945? By bouncing **X-rays** (`003`) off crystals — the exact technique that grew out of **Henri Becquerel's** dark desk drawer and **Marie Curie's** freezing Paris shed when they discovered **radioactivity** (`022` candidate *Radioactivity: Becquerel's Drawer & Marie Curie's Shed*, or `021` *Faraday's Electromagnetism*).
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: May 1945 · WHO: Dorothy Hodgkin (1910–1994) — Oxford X-ray crystallographer · WHEN: 11 December 1945 · DATA: animated timeline building 3 Sep 1928 (Fleming's St Mary's Petri dish) · Jun 1929 (Fleming publishes penicillin paper) · 25 May 1940 (Florey, Chain & Heatley's 8-mouse Oxford trial) · 12 Feb 1941 (Albert Alexander first human sepsis treatment) · Jul 1943 (Mary Hunt's Peoria cantaloupe strain NRRL 1951) · 6 Jun 1944 (2.3 million doses for D-Day) · May 1945 (Dorothy Hodgkin's beta-lactam X-ray structure) · SCALE: 20 atmospheres of internal pressure inside a Staphylococcus cell — three times the pressure of a racing bike tyre, popped when penicillin unlocks its cell wall`
