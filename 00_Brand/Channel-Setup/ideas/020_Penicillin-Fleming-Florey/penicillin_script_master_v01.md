## PART 02: "That's Funny" and the Ten-Year Shelf (1:25–3:05)

[CHAPTER CARD: "That's Funny" and the Ten-Year Shelf · 1:25 · ~1.5 s]

[VISUAL MUST: 2:28 — Explainer diagram on period paper: an inked dilution bar chart shows Fleming's 1929 mould broth killing Staphylococcus aureus even when diluted 1 part in 800 while leaving human white blood cells completely unharmed, alongside the unstable penicillin molecule breaking down in water by 1931 · WHEN: 1931 · DATA: inked dilution bar chart showing Fleming's 1929 mould broth killing Staphylococcus aureus at 1:800 dilution while leaving human white blood cells unharmed (vs carbolic acid killing white blood cells faster than bacteria) · SCALE: 1 drop of mould broth in 800 drops of water — and it still stopped deadly Staphylococcus cold]
[TEACH: On 3 September 1928, Alexander Fleming discovered that Penicillium notatum mould dissolved Staphylococcus bacteria even when diluted 1 in 800, but by 1931 he abandoned trying to purify the unstable broth as a medicine.]

*"That's funny."*

On the third of September, nineteen twenty-eight, forty-seven-year-old bacteriologist Alexander Fleming returned to his tiny turret room at St Mary's Hospital in London after a month-long August holiday in Suffolk.

Before leaving in August, Fleming had been studying *Staphylococcus* — the golden bacteria that cause boils, wound infections and fatal blood poisoning — and had stacked dozens of glass Petri dishes in a corner of his bench.

While chatting with his former assistant Merlin Pryce, Fleming began dropping the old dishes into a shallow tray of Lysol disinfectant. Because the stack was taller than the tray was deep, the top dishes stayed completely dry.

Suddenly picking up one unwashed dish, Fleming stopped and muttered, *"That's funny."* Near the edge sat a fuzzy blue-green blob of mould. All around that mould, for a centimetre or two, the thick yellow carpet of *Staphylococcus* colonies had melted into a crystal-clear halo.

Six years earlier, in nineteen twenty-two, Fleming had dripped his own tears onto a plate during a head cold and discovered a natural enzyme called lysozyme — but lysozyme only killed harmless bacteria.

This blue-green mould went far deeper. On the seventh of March, nineteen twenty-nine — when he tested and published his evidence in June — Fleming named the mould's golden broth *penicillin*. Even when diluted one drop in eight hundred drops of water, his measurements proved it stopped *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus* and pneumonia bacteria cold, without harming a single human white blood cell.

But raw "mould juice" was ninety-nine point nine percent water and impurities, and worse still, the active penicillin vanished within hours at room temperature. Not being a chemist, Fleming couldn't purify or stabilise it. By nineteen thirty-one, he stopped trying to turn penicillin into a medicine.

For nearly ten years, the greatest discovery in medical history sat neglected on a library shelf. Who finally pulled Fleming's published paper out of the dust on the eve of the Second World War — and why did they have to brew their medicine inside hospital bedpans?

## PART 03: The Bedpan Factory and the Eight Mice (3:05–4:55)

[TEACH: Between 1938 and May 1940 at Oxford, Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and Norman Heatley purified penicillin using chilled solvent back-extraction and ceramic bedpan vessels, saving all four treated mice in their landmark 25 May 1940 trial.]

In nineteen thirty-eight at the University of Oxford, pathology professor Howard Florey and refugee biochemist Ernst Chain were hunting through old library journals for natural microbe-killers when Chain stumbled across Fleming's forgotten nineteen twenty-nine paper.

Even better, a culture of Fleming's original mould was already sitting in a cupboard right downstairs in the Oxford pathology building.

But the chemical trap that defeated Fleming nearly defeated Chain too. Penicillin dissolved in organic solvents only when acid was added — and acid destroyed penicillin in minutes.

Then young biochemist Norman Heatley solved the puzzle. Using glass pipes, doorbell buzzers and milk churns, Heatley built a chilled extraction rig that washed penicillin from cold acid broth into solvent and back into neutral water before it could break down, until he freeze-dried it into a tiny pinch of brown powder.

Because the mould only grew as a thin crust on the surface of shallow liquid, and wartime glass factories were busy, Heatley designed hundreds of flat rectangular ceramic growing vessels modelled on hospital bedpans.

On Saturday, the twenty-fifth of May, nineteen forty, came the experiment that changed medicine. Florey's team injected eight white mice with one hundred and ten million deadly *Streptococcus* bacteria — a dose guaranteed to kill a mouse weighing less than an ounce. Four control mice got nothing. Four mice received injections of brown Oxford penicillin.

Heatley stayed up all night watching the cages. By half past three on Sunday morning, the twenty-sixth of May, all four untreated mice were dead. Now all four penicillin-treated mice were scampering around their cage, healthy and alive. When they published the proof in August nineteen forty, Florey called it a miracle.

Penicillin worked like magic in a twenty-gram mouse. One problem: a human weighs three thousand times more than a mouse. What happened when the Oxford team tried to save their first dying human patient — and ran out of medicine halfway through?

## PART 04: The Policeman's Urine and the Mouldy Cantaloupe — Midpoint Twist (4:55–6:40)

The Oxford team had proved penicillin cured lethal bacterial infections. Case closed? Wrong.

On the twelfth of February, nineteen forty-one, at the Radcliffe Infirmary in Oxford, forty-three-year-old policeman Albert Alexander lay dying. Months earlier, a rose-thorn scratch on his face had festered into runaway blood poisoning.

Doctor Charles Fletcher injected Alexander with two hundred milligrams of penicillin, then a hundred milligrams every three hours. Within twenty-four hours, his raging fever dropped and his wounds began to heal.

However, because human kidneys flush eighty percent of injected penicillin straight out in the urine, Norman Heatley had to bicycle through the winter snow carrying bottles of the policeman's urine back to the lab to extract every precious milligram and inject it back into him.

Suddenly, after five days, every drop of penicillin in Britain was gone. The bacteria roared back, and on the fifteenth of March, nineteen forty-one, Albert Alexander died.

Determined never to run out again — and fearing a Nazi invasion — Florey and Heatley smeared dormant mould spores into the linings of their own coats and flew to the United States in July nineteen forty-one. At the government laboratory in Peoria, Illinois, Andrew Moyer and Heatley brewed the mould in deep bubbling tanks fed with corn steep liquor.

Yet Fleming's London mould hated growing underwater. So mycologist Kenneth Raper sent assistant Mary Hunt to scour Peoria's grocery shops for rotting fruit.

Finally, in July nineteen forty-three, Mary Hunt brought in a golden-mouldy cantaloupe melon. Its new strain thrived in deep tanks and produced over a thousand times more penicillin than Fleming's dish — brewing two point three million doses in time for D-Day on the sixth of June, nineteen forty-four, and six point eight trillion units in nineteen forty-five.

How does a melon mould actually burst a deadly bacterium open without hurting a single human cell — and what chilling warning did Fleming give in nineteen forty-five?

## PART 05: Why Penicillin Pops Bacteria Like Balloons and Fleming's Warning (6:40–8:15)

[CHAPTER CARD: Why Penicillin Pops Bacteria Like Balloons and Fleming's Warning · 6:40 · ~1.5 s]
[VISUAL MUST: 6:43 — an animated timeline on period paper builds across the frame: 3 Sep 1928 (Fleming's St Mary's Petri dish) · Jun 1929 (Fleming publishes penicillin paper) · 25 May 1940 (Oxford 8-mouse trial) · 12 Feb 1941 (Albert Alexander first human treatment) · Jul 1943 (Mary Hunt's Peoria cantaloupe strain) · 6 Jun 1944 (2.3 million D-Day doses) · May 1945 (Dorothy Hodgkin's X-ray structure), alongside an inked diagram of a 20-atmosphere bacterium bursting open · WHO: Dorothy Hodgkin (1910–1994) — Oxford X-ray crystallographer · WHEN: May 1945 · WHEN: 1928–1945 (timeline marker) · DATA: animated timeline building 3 Sep 1928 · Jun 1929 · 25 May 1940 · 12 Feb 1941 · Jul 1943 · 6 Jun 1944 · May 1945 alongside an inked beta-lactam cell-wall diagram showing a Staphylococcus bacterium at 20 atmospheres internal pressure bursting when penicillin blocks peptidoglycan cross-linking · SCALE: 20 atmospheres of pressure inside a bacterium — three times the pressure of a racing bicycle tyre, popped when penicillin unlocks its cell wall]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:18 — Stockholm, 11 December 1945: Alexander Fleming, Howard Florey and Ernst Chain receive the Nobel Prize as Fleming warns that under-dosing educates microbes to resist penicillin · WHEN: 11 December 1945 · LABEL: FLEMING'S 1945 WARNING]
[VISUAL MUST: 7:45 — Oxford & London, 1831–1945: Dorothy Hodgkin's X-ray crystallography machine traces back to a spinning copper wire inside a cup of liquid mercury and a 6-inch iron ring wrapped in copper wire in Michael Faraday's basement laboratory · LABEL: THE POWER BEHIND THE LAB]
[TEACH: In May 1945, Dorothy Hodgkin's X-ray structure showed how penicillin's beta-lactam ring snaps bacterial cell walls under 20 atmospheres of pressure while sparing wall-less human cells, just before Fleming's 11 December 1945 Nobel warning on antibiotic resistance.]

Many people think antibiotics work by boosting your immune system, or take them to fight a cold or flu. Why doesn't penicillin touch a virus — and why doesn't it poison your own cells?

In May nineteen forty-five, Oxford X-ray crystallographer Dorothy Hodgkin mapped the exact atoms of penicillin and revealed its secret weapon: a strained, square four-atom ring called a beta-lactam ring.

Every bacterium is pumped up to an internal pressure of up to twenty atmospheres — three times the pressure of a racing bicycle tyre — held together by a mesh cage of cell wall. Penicillin's four-atom ring jams the enzyme that weaves that cage. As the bacterium tries to divide, its wall tears open and it bursts like an over-inflated balloon. Viruses have no cells at all, and human cells have no cell walls — so penicillin leaves your body untouched.

On the eleventh of December, nineteen forty-five, accepting the Nobel Prize alongside Florey and Chain, Fleming issued a prophetic warning: use too little penicillin, and surviving bacteria will evolve resistance.

And what powered the giant electrical stirrers, pumps and X-ray tubes that made wartime penicillin possible? Over a century earlier, a self-taught bookbinder's apprentice dipped a copper wire into a cup of mercury and invented the electric motor.

An unwashed glass dish, a stack of hospital bedpans and a mouldy melon from Illinois. Together, they turned a stray speck of mould into two hundred million human lives.
