### 1. Is Light a Particle or a Wave? Thomas Young's playing card in a sunbeam, Poisson's bright spot & Einstein's photoelectric quanta (recommended for 025, or 023; drafted in `025_Young-Fresnel-Einstein-Light/`)
- **Familiar thing:** a sunbeam streaming through a gap in your curtains, the rainbow shimmer on a soap bubble, and a solar panel or phone camera sensor turning light directly into electricity.
- **Person & room:** Physician and polymath **Thomas Young** (1773–1829) standing in a darkened London room before the **Royal Society on 24 November 1801**, holding the edge of a thin **playing card (`1/30th of an inch` thick)** inside a pinhole sunbeam; cut to young French civil engineer **Augustin-Jean Fresnel** (1788–1827), skeptical mathematician **Siméon Denis Poisson** (1781–1840), and astronomer **François Arago** (1786–1853) at the **French Academy of Sciences in Paris in March 1819** gazing at the shadow of a **2-millimetre metal disc**; and 26-year-old patent clerk **Albert Einstein** (1879–1955) in **Bern, Switzerland, on 17 March 1905** explaining why dim violet light knocks sparks off metal when bright red light cannot.
- **Date & proof:** For a hundred years after **Isaac Newton** (`009`) published his *Opticks* (**1704**), almost every scientist in Europe obeyed Newton's authority that light is a stream of tiny bullets ("corpuscles") flying in straight lines. Then, in his Bakerian Lecture to the Royal Society on **24 November 1801**, London physician **Thomas Young** — the same polymath who helped decipher the Rosetta Stone — made a tiny hole in a window shutter, covered it with a pinhole in thick paper, and split the narrow sunbeam in two with the edge of a thin **slip of card (`1/30th of an inch` thick)** (and later two parallel slits cut beside each other). If light were tiny bullets flying through two gaps, the wall behind should show two bright bands. Instead, the shadow was striped with **five or six alternating bright and dark bands** — and the **very centre of the card's shadow was always a bright white stripe**! Stranger still, when Young blocked the light on just *one* side of the card with a small screen, all the bright fringes in the shadow **vanished into darkness**! Adding a second beam of light had created **darkness** where there used to be light — proving that light waves crest and trough like ripples on a pond (**wave interference**) and letting Young measure the wavelength of red light (`1/36,000th of an inch`, ~`700 nanometres`) and violet light (`1/60,000th of an inch`, ~`420 nanometres`) for the first time in history!
- **Twist (three-part):**
  1. **Ridiculed into silence (1802–1804):** Instead of celebrating Young, Lord Henry Brougham wrote vicious attacks in the *Edinburgh Review* mocking Young's wave experiments as *"destitute of every species of merit"* because they dared to question Isaac Newton. When Young published a pamphlet defending himself, **only a single copy sold**!
  2. **The 1818 French Academy trap that backfired — Poisson's Bright Spot:** Fourteen years later (**1818**), French road engineer **Augustin-Jean Fresnel** independently rediscovered wave diffraction and entered the French Academy of Sciences prize competition. Judge **Siméon Denis Poisson**, a fierce supporter of Newton's particle theory, used Fresnel's own wave integrals to derive what he thought was an absurd, fatal contradiction: if light really were a wave, then right in the dead centre of the circular shadow cast by a solid round obstacle, all the waves bending around the rim would travel the exact same distance, meet in step, and form a **bright spot of light** in the middle of the dark shadow! Committee head **François Arago** decided to test Poisson's "absurd" prediction in the laboratory: he glued a **2-millimetre metallic disc** to a glass plate with wax and illuminated it with a point of light — and **shining right in the dead centre of the dark shadow was the bright spot** (**Arago's spot / Poisson's spot**)! Poisson's own trap had proved that light is a wave, and Fresnel won the **Grand Prix on 15 March 1819**!
  3. **Einstein's 1905 counter-twist — Light is BOTH:** Just when waves had won completely, experiments by **Heinrich Hertz** (**1887**) and **Philipp Lenard** (**1902**) uncovered a baffling anomaly (**the photoelectric effect**): shining ultraviolet light (`018`) onto a clean metal plate knocked electrons out of the metal, yet cranking a bright red wave up to blinding intensity knocked out **zero electrons**, whereas even the dimmest ultraviolet glimmer kicked electrons out instantly! On **17 March 1905**, 26-year-old **Albert Einstein** showed that light travels through space as a wave (producing Young's stripes and Arago's spot), yet exchanges energy in indivisible packets (**light quanta / photons**, $E = hf$) — winning the **1921 Nobel Prize in Physics** not for relativity, but for the photoelectric law that powers every solar panel and digital camera sensor today!

### 2. Radioactivity: Henri Becquerel's cloudy-day drawer & Marie Curie's freezing shed (recommended for 023; drafted in `023_Becquerel-Curie-Radioactivity/`)
- **Familiar thing:** a clicking Geiger counter, carbon and uranium dating of ancient rocks, hospital cancer radiotherapy, and the warm heat deep inside the Earth.
- **Person & room:** French physicist **Henri Becquerel** (1852–1908) at the Muséum National d'Histoire Naturelle in **Paris** during a week of grey winter clouds (**26 February – 1 March 1896**), opening a pitch-dark wooden desk drawer holding a lump of uranium salt and a copper Maltese cross on a paper-wrapped photographic plate; cut to 30-year-old Polish-born physicist **Marie Skłodowska Curie** (1867–1934) and **Pierre Curie** (1859–1906) in a leaky, unheated former medical-school dissecting shed on **Rue Lhomond, Paris** (**1898–1902**), stirring boiling cauldrons of brown pitchblende ore residue with a heavy iron rod.
- **Date & proof:** In **January 1896**, weeks after **Wilhelm Röntgen** (`003`) stunned the world with X-rays, **Henri Becquerel** suspected that glowing, fluorescent **uranium salts** had to absorb **bright sunlight** in order to re-emit penetrating rays through black paper. On Wednesday **26 February 1896**, Paris was covered in thick winter clouds. Unable to expose his uranium crystals to the sun, Becquerel locked the unexposed uranium salt, a **copper Maltese cross**, and a photographic plate wrapped in two sheets of thick black paper inside a **pitch-dark desk drawer**. Four days later, on Sunday **1 March 1896**, although the sun had never come out, Becquerel developed the plate anyway expecting a faint smudge — and gasped: the silhouette of the Maltese cross was burned **jet-black at full strength in total darkness**! The uranium needed no sunlight, no battery, and no heat: the invisible rays were pouring spontaneously out of the **uranium atoms themselves**!
- **Twist (three-part):**
  1. **The electrometer surprise — the waste rock is 4× stronger than pure uranium (April 1898):** While physicists flocked back to flashier X-rays, 30-year-old doctoral student **Marie Skłodowska Curie** used Pierre and Jacques Curie's ultra-sensitive **piezoelectric quartz electrometer** to measure the tiny electrical current (`10⁻¹¹ amperes`) created as uranium rays ionized the air. Testing every element she could find, she proved that the radiation depended *only* on the number of uranium (or thorium) atoms present — coining the word **"radioactivity"** in **1898**. Then came the shock: when she tested raw **pitchblende** (the black mineral ore from Joachimsthal out of which uranium is extracted), the raw ore was **four times more radioactive than pure uranium itself**! Hidden inside the rock had to be a new, unknown element far more radioactive than uranium!
  2. **Tonnes of waste slag in a freezing dissecting shed (July 1898 – July 1902):** Using chemical separation where the electrometer acted as an "invisible eye", Marie and Pierre discovered **polonium** in **July 1898** (named after Marie's occupied homeland, Poland) and **radium** in **December 1898** — an element **more than a million times more radioactive than uranium**! Yet chemist **Eugène-Anatole Demarçay** saw a new spectral line, while hard-headed chemists refused to believe radium existed until the Curies could show its actual **atomic weight**. Working for **four years (1898–1902)** in a leaky, freezing wooden shed with no fume hoods, Marie boiled and crystallized **several tonnes of pitchblende waste residue** (`20-kilogram` batches at a time) until in **July 1902** she isolated **one-tenth of a gram (`0.1 g`) of pure radium chloride** (determining radium's atomic weight as `225`, modern `226`) — glowing with a eerie blue-green light in the dark and staying **1.5°C (`3°F`) warmer than the room around it** without burning anything!
  3. **The Nobel exclusion that Pierre stopped & the 1,500-year notebooks (1903–today):** In **1903**, a group of French Academy members nominated only **Henri Becquerel** and **Pierre Curie** for the Nobel Prize in Physics — leaving Marie off the letter! Tipped off by Swedish mathematician **Gösta Mittag-Leffler**, Pierre declared he would refuse any prize that excluded Marie; she became the **first woman to win a Nobel Prize (1903)** and, in **1911**, the **only person in history to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences (Physics and Chemistry)**. Yet neither she nor Pierre knew the danger of the glowing vials they carried in their pockets: Marie died of aplastic anaemia in **1934**, and because **Radium-226 has a half-life of 1,600 years**, her laboratory notebooks at the Bibliothèque Nationale in Paris are **still radioactive today** and must be kept in lead-lined boxes!

- **Familiar thing:** the calories listed on every packet of food, car brakes glowing red-hot when stopping, and why no perpetual-motion machine can ever create energy out of nothing.
- **Person & room:** American-born adventurer and military engineer **Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford** (1753–1814) at the Electoral Arsenal in **Munich, Bavaria**, in **January 1798**, watching two horses walk in circles to turn a blunt steel borer inside a brass cannon cylinder submerged in a wooden box of cold water; cut to twenty-something Manchester brewer's son **James Prescott Joule** (1818–1889) in his basement laboratory in **Salford/Manchester** (**August 1843 – June 1845**), dropping lead weights on silk strings to spin a brass **paddle wheel** inside an insulated copper tank of water — and meeting a 23-year-old **William Thomson (Lord Kelvin)** (1824–1907) in **Oxford in June 1847** and on Joule's **Swiss Alps honeymoon in August 1847**.
- **Date & proof:** At the end of the eighteenth century, the world's greatest chemists — led by **Antoine Lavoisier** (`017`) in his **1789** *Traité Élémentaire de Chimie* — believed heat was an indestructible, weightless fluid element called **"caloric"** trapped in the pores of matter and squeezed out like water from a sponge whenever you rubbed or hammered a solid. Then, in **January 1798** at the military arsenal in **Munich** (read to the Royal Society on **25 January 1798**), **Count Rumford** noticed that boring out brass cannon barrels heated the metal far hotter than boiling water. If heat were a finite fluid squeezed out as metal was shaved into powder, then pressing a **blunt steel borer** that cut almost *no* shavings should release almost no heat — and the cannon should quickly run out of caloric! Instead, Rumford encased a brass cannon cylinder in a wooden box holding **18.77 pounds (`8.5 kg`) of cold water (`60°F / 15.6°C`)**, pressed a blunt steel borer against its bottom with **10,000 pounds (`4.5 tonnes`) of force** turned by two horses, and timed it: at 1 hour the water reached `107°F`; at 1 hour 30 minutes, `142°F`; and at **2 hours and 30 minutes, the water actually boiled (`212°F / 100°C`) without a single spark of fire** — while the blunt borer had scraped off only **837 grains (`54 grams`, under 2 ounces)** of metallic dust! Because the heat supply was **inexhaustible** as long as the horses kept walking, Rumford proved that heat cannot be a material substance — **heat is motion**!
- **Twist (three-part):**
  1. **Ignored for 45 years — and he married Lavoisier's widow! (1798–1843):** Despite boiling water with a blunt cannon before astonished spectators in Munich (and even marrying Lavoisier's widow **Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier** in **1805**!), the scientific establishment refused to give up the caloric fluid for another forty-five years.
  2. **The Manchester brewer's paddle wheel & the 1/200th-degree thermometer (1843–1847):** In industrial Manchester, young brewer **James Prescott Joule** — who never held a university post and was tutored as a teenager by **John Dalton** (`004`) — set out to measure whether mechanical work, electricity (`021`), and heat are all exact, interchangeable forms of the same thing. Between **1843 and 1849**, Joule rigged up two lead weights (`29 pounds` each) falling **63 inches** (`20 times` in a run) on pulleys to spin a **brass paddle wheel** through stationary baffles inside a copper vessel of water. Using custom glass thermometers made by **John Benjamin Dancer** so precise he could read **1/200th of a degree Fahrenheit (`0.005°F`)**, Joule proved that **772 foot-pounds** of mechanical work always raises **1 pound of water by 1°F** (`4.184 joules per calorie`) — whether the work comes from falling weights, compressing air, or cranking a Faraday magneto!
  3. **Rescued from silence by a 23-year-old at Oxford — and the honeymoon waterfall thermometer (June–August 1847):** The Royal Society rejected or buried Joule's papers as the hobby of a provincial brewer (telling him at the **23 June 1847** British Association meeting in **Oxford** to limit his talk to a brief summary because the chairman had no time for discussion!). Sitting in the back row was 23-year-old **William Thomson (later Lord Kelvin)**, who stood up and asked pointed questions that electrified the hall. Two months later (**August 1847**), walking near **Chamonix in the Swiss Alps**, Thomson spotted Joule on his **honeymoon** — walking beside his bride's carriage carrying a **several-foot-long thermometer** in his hand to try to measure whether the water at the bottom of the **Sallanches waterfall** was `0.2°F` warmer than at the top! Together with German physician **Julius Robert Mayer** (1814–1878, who noticed in **1840** in Java that sailors' venous blood stayed brighter red in the tropics because they burned less food-oxygen to stay warm) and **Hermann von Helmholtz** (1821–1894, **1847**), they established the **First Law of Thermodynamics (Conservation of Energy)**: energy can never be created or destroyed, only transformed — and today every single packet of food and every physics equation measures energy in **joules (`J`)**!

### 4. Turning Thin Air Into Bread: Fritz Haber's tabletop ammonia drop & Carl Bosch's bursting steel reactors
- **Familiar thing:** the bread, pasta and vegetables on your dinner plate — and the startling fact that **half of all the nitrogen atoms inside your muscles and DNA right now** came out of a high-pressure chemical factory.
- **Person & room:** German chemist **Fritz Haber** (1868–1934) and his British engineering assistant **Robert Le Rossignol** (1884–1976) at the **Technische Hochschule in Karlsruhe, Germany**, on **2 July 1909**, crowding around a hot quartz-and-steel tabletop tube at **200 atmospheres of pressure** as clear drops of liquid ammonia condense into a glass collector; and industrial metallurgist **Carl Bosch** (1874–1940) at **BASF in Ludwigshafen** (**1909–1913**), cutting open burst artillery-steel cylinders eaten hollow from the inside by hot hydrogen.
- **Date & twist:** Four out of every five breaths you take (**78% of Earth's atmosphere**) are pure **nitrogen ($N_2$)**. Every plant and human needs nitrogen to build proteins and DNA, yet crops cannot absorb nitrogen gas from the air because its two atoms are locked together by a **triple covalent bond** (`945 kJ/mol`, one of the strongest chemical bonds in nature). In **September 1898**, British Association President **Sir William Crookes** warned that as the world's natural fertilizer beds of Chilean saltpetre ran dry by the 1930s, hundreds of millions of wheat-eaters would starve to death unless chemists learned to "fix" nitrogen from the air. On **2 July 1909** in Karlsruhe, **Fritz Haber** and **Robert Le Rossignol** squeezed nitrogen and hydrogen gas together at **200 atmospheres of pressure** (200 times atmospheric pressure) and **500°C** over a catalyst of rare **osmium**, producing a steady stream of **liquid ammonia ($NH_3$)** at **80 millilitres per hour**! Then **Carl Bosch** at BASF tested **over 20,000 experiments on 4,000 catalysts** (led by **Alwin Mittasch**) to replace rare osmium with cheap **magnetite iron** — and when 200-atmosphere hydrogen at 500°C diffused into thick carbon-steel reactor walls, reacted with the carbon into methane blisters, and **blew the steel tubes to pieces**, Bosch invented a **double-walled steel reactor** with a thin soft-iron inner liner and an outer pressure shell drilled with **tiny "Bosch holes"** that let escaping hydrogen vent harmlessly before it could weaken the steel! **The tragic twist:** Today the **Haber-Bosch process** feeds **half of humanity (~4 billion people)** — yet because ammonia also oxidizes into nitric acid for TNT and artillery shells, Haber's discovery enabled Germany to fight on after the 1914 British naval blockade cut off Chilean saltpetre; when Haber personally directed the first chlorine poison-gas attack at Ypres on **22 April 1915**, his wife **Dr Clara Immerwahr** (1870–1915, the first woman to earn a chemistry doctorate at Breslau) condemned it as a *"perversion of the ideals of science"* and took her own life on **2 May 1915**, and in **1933** the Nazi regime forced Haber himself into exile because he was born Jewish!

### 5. The First Computer & Breaking Enigma: Ada Lovelace's 1843 Note G & Alan Turing's 1936 Universal Machine and 1940 Bombe
- **Familiar thing:** the smartphone or laptop you are watching this video on — a single physical machine that can switch from playing music to editing photos to running a game simply by loading new software.
- **Person & room:** Twenty-seven-year-old mathematician **Ada Lovelace** (1815–1852) and inventor **Charles Babbage** (1791–1871) in **London in August 1843**, poring over brass gears and Jacqard-loom punched cards as Lovelace writes **Note G** (the world's first published computer algorithm, calculating Bernoulli numbers on a machine that hasn't even been built yet!); cut to 23-year-old Cambridge fellow **Alan Turing** (1912–1954) at **King's College, Cambridge, in May 1936** imagining an infinite paper tape reading `0` and `1`, and in **Hut 8 at Bletchley Park on 18 March 1940** as the first electromechanical **Bombe ("Victory")** ticks through **158,962,555,217,826,360,000** (`159 quintillion`) Nazi Enigma settings by hunting for a single logical contradiction!
- **Date & twist:** For centuries, a "computer" wasn't a machine — it was a human clerk sitting at a desk calculating tables of numbers by hand, full of fatal arithmetic typos. In **1837–1843**, **Charles Babbage** designed a steam-powered brass **Analytical Engine** with a "Mill" (CPU) and "Store" (memory), and in **August 1843** **Ada Lovelace** (Lord Byron's daughter, whose mother taught her mathematics to prevent her becoming a poet!) translated Luigi Menabrea's paper and added notes **three times longer than the paper itself** — proving in **Note G** that a machine reading punched cards could manipulate *any* symbol (including musical notes and algebra, not just numbers!) and writing out the **first step-by-step recursive computer program** a century before the first electronic computer existed! Ninety-three years later (**28 May 1936**), trying to solve German mathematician David Hilbert's pure-logic *Entscheidungsproblem* ("Can a mechanical method decide whether any mathematical statement is provable?"), 23-year-old **Alan Turing** invented a theoretical machine reading an infinite strip of paper tape — proving that **some problems can never be solved by any algorithm (the Halting Problem)**, while along the way inventing the **Universal Turing Machine**: one machine that becomes *any* machine simply by reading instructions stored as data on its own tape! **The wartime & tragic twist:** When World War II broke out, the German military trusted the **Enigma machine** because its scramblers and plugboard offered **159 quintillion (`1.59 × 10²⁰`) settings** that changed every 24 hours. Building on Polish mathematicians **Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski's** breakthrough, Turing and **Gordon Welchman** at **Bletchley Park** (`1940`) realised Enigma had one fatal flaw — **a letter could never encrypt to itself** — and built the **Bombe**, an electromechanical logical sieve that eliminated quadrillions of impossible rotor settings in **under 20 minutes**! Historians estimate Bletchley Park shortened the war in Europe by **two to four years and saved ~14 million lives** — yet because Bletchley Park remained a state secret until the **1970s** (its Colossus blueprints and hardware ordered destroyed after the war), when Turing was prosecuted in **1952** under Britain's anti-homosexuality laws, the court had no idea he had helped save the nation!
