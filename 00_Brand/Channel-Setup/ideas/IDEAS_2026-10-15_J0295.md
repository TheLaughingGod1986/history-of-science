### 1. Evolution: Darwin's 20-year secret & the letter from the jungle (recommended for 013)
- **Familiar thing:** why a bird's beak, a moth's wing or an animal's coat fits the place it lives.
- **Person & room:** Charles Darwin (1809–1882) in his cluttered Down House study in Kent (with his red leather *Notebook B* and specimen drawers of Galápagos finches and barnacles), paired in back-and-forth portrait cuts with young Welsh naturalist **Alfred Russel Wallace** (1823–1913) writing in a bamboo hut on the volcanic island of Ternate (Maluku Islands, Indonesia) between bouts of malarial fever.
- **Date & proof:** After visiting the Galápagos in **September–October 1835**, Darwin didn't even label which island his birds came from — thinking they were blackbirds, wrens and finches until London ornithologist **John Gould** examined the skins in **March 1837** and told him they were **13 distinct species of ground finch** unique to the islands. In **July 1837** Darwin drew his tiny **"I think"** branching tree of life on page 36 of *Notebook B*; in **autumn 1838** he read Thomas Malthus and worked out **natural selection**; and in **July 1844** he wrote a **230-page essay** — then locked it inside his desk with a letter instructing his wife Emma to pay **£400** to publish it only *after his death*, terrified of the uproar it would cause. He spent the next **8 years (1846–1854)** dissecting **10,000 barnacles** instead — until **18 June 1858** (or early June 1858), when a thin post packet arrived at Down House from **Alfred Russel Wallace** in Ternate containing a 20-page essay setting out the exact same theory.
- **Twist:** Rather than bury Wallace's letter, a heartbroken Darwin (whose 18-month-old son **Charles Waring Darwin** died of scarlet fever ten days later, on **28 June 1858**) handed both Wallace's essay and his own 1844/1857 extracts to geologist **Charles Lyell** and botanist **Joseph Dalton Hooker**, who had them read together at the **Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858**. Neither author was in the room (Darwin was at his baby son's funeral; Wallace was still in the Malay Archipelago), nobody in the audience argued, and at the Society's annual meeting in **May 1859**, its president **Thomas Bell** famously declared that the year 1858 had not been marked by any striking discovery that revolutionised science! Six months later (**24 November 1859**), all **1,250 copies** of *On the Origin of Species* sold out to booksellers on the very first day.

### 2. Cell Theory: Robert Hooke's cork, Robert Remak & where every living cell comes from
- **Familiar thing:** the 30 trillion microscopic cells that make up your skin, blood and bones.
- **Person & room:** **Robert Hooke** (1635–1703), Curator of Experiments at the Royal Society in London, slicing bottle cork with a keen penknife under a candle-and-water-globe leather microscope in **January 1665**; then **Matthias Schleiden** & **Theodor Schwann** in Berlin (**1838–1839**); and Polish-Jewish embryologist **Robert Remak** (1815–1865) working in an attic lab in Berlin (**1852**).
- **Date & twist:** Hooke coined the word **"cell"** in *Micrographia* (**January 1665**) because the **1,259,712,000** tiny pores in a cubic inch of cork looked like monks' bedrooms (*cella*) — and after Hooke died in **1703**, his bitter rival **Isaac Newton** became President of the Royal Society and Hooke's only portrait vanished! Nearly two centuries later (**1838–1839**), Schleiden and Schwann realised all plants and animals are made of cells, but Schleiden insisted new cells crystallised out of fluid like sugar crystals. In **1852**, **Robert Remak** proved every cell comes from an existing cell dividing in two — only for his colleague **Rudolf Virchow** to publish the famous motto *"Omnis cellula e cellula"* in **1855** without crediting Remak!

### 3. Extinction & Fossils: Mary Anning's 17-foot sea dragon at Lyme Regis
- **Familiar thing:** a spiral ammonite fossil on a beach or a dinosaur skeleton in a museum — and the fact that species can vanish forever.
- **Person & room:** Twelve-year-old **Mary Anning** (1799–1847) and her brother Joseph, chipping through storm-slipped Blue Lias shale on the cliffs of **Lyme Regis, Dorset**, in **1811–1812** (and her candlelit fossil shop on Broad Street), paired with French anatomist **Georges Cuvier** (1769–1832) at the Muséum National d'Histoire Naturelle in Paris.
- **Date & twist:** Before the early 1800s, most naturalists believed no creature made by God could ever go extinct — strange bones had to belong to animals still living in unexplored seas. In **1811–1812**, twelve-year-old Mary Anning excavated a **5.2-metre (17-foot)** marine reptile skeleton (*Ichthyosaurus*), and in **December 1823** she uncovered the first complete long-necked ***Plesiosaurus***. When drawings reached Paris, **Georges Cuvier** initially suspected the 35-vertebra neck was a forged monster stitched together from different animals — until British geologists convened a special meeting at the Geological Society of London in **February 1824**, Cuvier admitted he was mistaken and became a customer of Anning's, and her fossils clinched the proof of **extinction**. Yet the Geological Society barred women from membership until **1904**, and wealthy collectors published papers on her skeletons without once naming her in the title.

### 4. Invisible Light: William Herschel's thermometer past the rainbow & Ritter's ultraviolet
- **Familiar thing:** the warmth of sunlight on your skin, a TV remote, or sunscreen blocking ultraviolet rays.
- **Person & room:** **William Herschel** (1738–1822) and his sister **Caroline Herschel** in a darkened room in Slough, England, on **11 February 1800**, with a glass prism in a window shutter casting a rainbow across a table of mercury thermometers; and **Johann Wilhelm Ritter** (1776–1810) in Jena, Germany, in **February 1801**.
- **Date & twist:** Herschel wasn't looking for a new kind of light — he was trying to find which colour filter would block heat when he observed the Sun through his telescope. On **11 February 1800**, he placed thermometers in the violet, green and red bands, and set a control thermometer in the dark shadow *just beyond the red end* of the spectrum. To his astonishment, the thermometer in the dark rose **higher than any visible colour** (about **9°F / 5°C above room temperature** in 16 minutes) — proving for the first time that the Sun shines in colours human eyes cannot see (**infrared**). One year later (**February 1801**), 24-year-old **Johann Wilhelm Ritter** read Herschel's paper, reasoned that if there is invisible heat past the red there must be an invisible opposite past the violet, placed paper soaked in white **silver chloride** in the dark beyond violet, and watched it blacken faster than anywhere in the visible rainbow — discovering **ultraviolet**!

### 5. Electromagnetism: Ørsted's twitching compass & Michael Faraday's cup of mercury
- **Familiar thing:** every electric motor in your phone, fan and train, and every power-station generator on Earth.
- **Person & room:** **Hans Christian Ørsted** (1777–1851) in a Copenhagen lecture theatre on **21 April 1820**, and former bookbinder's apprentice **Michael Faraday** (1791–1867) in the basement laboratory of the Royal Institution in London on **3–4 September 1821** and **29 August 1831**.
- **Date & twist:** For centuries, electricity and magnetism were thought to be two completely separate forces. On **21 April 1820**, Ørsted switched on a battery during an evening lecture and saw a nearby compass needle twitch sideways. In London, **Michael Faraday** — who had left school at 13 and knew almost no algebra — rigged a hanging wire over a bar magnet fixed with wax in a small cup of liquid mercury on **3–4 September 1821**, connected a battery, and danced around the table shouting *"There they go! There they go!"* as the wire spun continuously in circles: the world's first electric motor. The twist: his famous mentor **Sir Humphry Davy** grew so jealous that he accused Faraday of taking the idea from William Hyde Wollaston and cast the **lone blackball vote against** Faraday's election to the Royal Society in **1824** — only for Faraday, ten years later (**29 August 1831**), to run the trick in reverse by pushing a magnet through a coil of wire and inventing the electric generator!
