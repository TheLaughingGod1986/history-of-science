## PART 02: The 32-Foot Wall in Florence & Torricelli's 1644 Mercury Tube (1:25–3:05)

When you sip iced water through a drinking straw, do you *suck* the liquid up?

No — you cannot pull a liquid with a gas! When you expand your chest, you lower the air pressure *inside* the straw, and then the weight of the room's air pressing down on the surface of your glass **pushes the water up the straw** into your mouth!

However, for nearly two thousand years after the Greek philosopher Aristotle, scholars taught a sacred rule: **"Nature abhors a vacuum."** Empty space, they claimed, is a physical paradox that Nature refuses to allow — so when you pull up the piston of a water pump, water rushes up the pipe only to prevent an empty void from opening!

Then, in sixteen thirty-eight, well-diggers in Florence brought an elderly Galileo Galilei a baffling mystery. Whenever a well was deeper than **eighteen Florentine braccia** — about **thirty-two feet**, or ten point three metres — even the most tightly sealed suction pump failed completely! Above thirty-two feet, the water column simply snapped and refused to rise an inch higher!

Why would Nature abhor a vacuum up to thirty-two feet, and then suddenly stop caring?

After Galileo died, his thirty-five-year-old successor Evangelista Torricelli realised the secret: a pump doesn't pull water up from inside at all! Instead, the weight of the outside air pressing down on the well pushes the water up the pipe — and at sea level, the atmosphere is only heavy enough to balance a thirty-two-foot tower of water!

To test his idea inside a room without a thirty-five-foot pipe, Torricelli and his twenty-one-year-old assistant Vincenzo Viviani switched in sixteen forty-three and sixteen forty-four to liquid **mercury**, which is **thirteen point six times denser** than water. Dividing thirty-two feet by thirteen point six, Torricelli predicted the air would hold up only **thirty inches** of mercury!

When Viviani filled a four-foot glass tube with mercury, sealed it with his finger, and tipped it upside down into a dish of mercury, the silver column dropped and stopped dead at **thirty inches** — leaving the **world's first sustained vacuum** at the top of the glass! On the eleventh of June sixteen forty-four, Torricelli wrote a sentence for the ages: *"We live submerged at the bottom of an ocean of the element air."*

But Aristotelian critics still refused to believe the space at the top of Torricelli's glass tube was truly empty! Why did a twenty-three-year-old French mathematician build a forty-six-foot glass tube of red wine — and then send a barometer up a volcano?

When news of Torricelli's tube reached France, critics claimed that invisible "spirits" and vapours trapped inside the top of the glass were pushing the mercury down!

In late sixteen forty-six, twenty-three-year-old French prodigy Blaise Pascal put those critics to a brilliant test at a glassworks in Rouen. He had glassblowers make **forty-six-foot glass tubes**, lashed them to a ship's mast, and inverted one filled with water and one filled with **red wine**!

The critics predicted that because wine is "more spirituous" and evaporates more easily, wine vapours at the top of the tube would push the red wine *lower* than the water. Instead, because alcohol is slightly lighter than water, outside air pressure pushed the column of red wine **higher than the water**!

Even then, skeptics refused to yield. Case closed? Not until Pascal thought of the ultimate experiment!

If the mercury in a barometer is held up by the weight of the ocean of air above our heads, what if you carry that barometer **up a high mountain**, where there is *less* air overhead?

Too frail to make a steep climb himself in Paris, Pascal wrote on the fifteenth of November sixteen forty-seven to his brother-in-law Florin Périer in Clermont-Ferrand, at the foot of the volcanic mountain **Puy-de-Dôme**.

At eight in the morning on Saturday, the nineteenth of September sixteen forty-eight, Périer filled two identical mercury barometers in the monastery garden in the valley, where both stood at just over **twenty-six French inches** — seven hundred and eleven millimetres. Leaving a monk to watch one tube all day, Périer and a team of witnesses carried the second barometer **one thousand metres** up to the windy summit!

When Périer set up the tube at the top of the mountain, the mercury had fallen by **more than three inches** — eighty-four millimetres! Stunned, they tested it five times in wind, rain, sun, and inside the summit chapel, then watched the mercury steadily climb back up as they walked down the mountain!

Now nobody could deny that air has crushing weight. What if you pump the air out of a hollow copper sphere — and hitch **sixteen warhorses** to pull it open?

In Magdeburg, Germany, engineer and mayor Otto von Guericke wasn't satisfied with a tiny vacuum at the top of a glass tube — he wanted to pump the air out of a large container directly!

Around sixteen fifty, modifying a brass fire-engine water pump, Guericke invented the **world's first mechanical vacuum pump**. When he first tried pumping the air out of a sealed wooden beer barrel, the outside atmosphere pressed inward so hard that it **smashed the thick oak staves to pieces** with a thunderous bang!

So Guericke cast two thick, hollow **copper hemispheres**, fifty centimetres across, that met along a flat rim sealed only with a ring of grease-soaked leather — **with zero bolts and zero latches**.

On the eighth of May sixteen fifty-four, before Emperor Ferdinand III at Regensburg, Guericke pumped the air out of the copper sphere, closed the valve, and hitched **two teams of eight horses — sixteen horses in all** — pulling in opposite directions!

Whipped at full strength, the sixteen horses strained and reared, but could not pull the unbolted bowls apart — because the outside air clamped those two copper halves together with **two tonnes of force**! Then Guericke walked up, turned the tiny brass valve with two fingers to let the air hiss back in, and the two heavy bowls **fell apart of their own weight**!

Inspired by Guericke's spheres, Anglo-Irish chemist Robert Boyle and his young assistant Robert Hooke built a glass-globe vacuum pump in Oxford in sixteen sixty. When Boyle hung a ticking pocket watch inside the glass globe and pumped the air out, the hands kept turning, **but the ticking sound vanished into total silence** — proving that sound waves cannot cross a vacuum! And in sixteen sixty-two, pouring mercury into a J-shaped glass tube, Boyle discovered **Boyle's Law**: squeeze a gas to half its volume, and its pressure doubles!

So how did engineers turn that invisible two-tonne crush of the atmosphere into a machine that pumped flooded coal mines dry and launched the **Industrial Revolution**?

Most people think the first steam engines worked by using high-pressure steam to *push* a heavy piston. Is that true?

No — the first steam engines ran on a **vacuum**! Following Denis Papin's sixteen ninety design, English ironmonger Thomas Newcomen in seventeen twelve filled a giant brass cylinder under a piston with low-pressure steam, and then sprayed a jet of cold water inside. Instantly, the steam condensed back into a few drops of water, leaving an empty **vacuum** beneath the piston — and the **weight of the outside atmosphere slammed the piston down with more than five tonnes of force** to pump deep coal mines dry and launch the Industrial Revolution!

And Torricelli's ocean of air connects every breath we take across the entire planet. Three centuries later, following Ernest Rutherford's twentieth of May nineteen oh four discovery that radioactive uranium ticks into lead like an atomic clock, American geochemist Clair Patterson tried in nineteen fifty-three to measure microscopic traces of lead inside a fallen meteorite to find the **true age of the Earth** — only to discover that every breath of air entering his laboratory was poisoned with **two hundred times too much industrial lead** from leaded petrol, a mystery he proved in nineteen sixty-five by coring ancient ice in Greenland!

A thirty-two-foot well in Florence, a hike up a French volcano, and sixteen horses in a German courtyard. Together, they proved we live at the bottom of a mighty ocean of air.
