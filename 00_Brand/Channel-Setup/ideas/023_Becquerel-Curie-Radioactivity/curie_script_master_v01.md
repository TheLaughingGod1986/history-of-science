## PART 02: The Cloudy Week in Paris and the Maltese Cross (1:25–3:05)

[CHAPTER CARD: The Cloudy Week in Paris and the Maltese Cross · 1:25 · ~1.5 s]

[TEACH: After cloudy weather in Paris from 26 February to 1 March 1896 ruined his sunlight experiment, Henri Becquerel developed his stored photographic plate anyway and discovered that uranium emits penetrating rays spontaneously in total darkness.]

On the twentieth of January, eighteen ninety-six, mathematician Henri Poincaré showed the French Academy of Sciences the first X-ray photographs of human bones, newly discovered in Germany by Wilhelm Röntgen.

Sitting in the audience was forty-three-year-old physicist Henri Becquerel — whose father and grandfather had both studied glowing phosphorescent minerals in the exact same Paris museum laboratory.

When Poincaré noted that Röntgen's X-rays came from the glowing fluorescent spot on his glass vacuum tube, Becquerel jumped to a neat — and completely wrong — conclusion. What if ordinary glowing crystals, like yellow-green uranium salts, also give off penetrating rays whenever bright sunlight makes them glow?

On the twenty-fourth of February, eighteen ninety-six, Becquerel placed uranium crystals on top of a photographic plate wrapped in two sheets of thick black paper, set a coin between them, and left the packet out in the bright sun. When he developed the plate, the silhouette of the coin appeared. Sunlight, he thought, was powering the rays.

However, on Wednesday the twenty-sixth of February, eighteen ninety-six, when Becquerel prepared a new packet with a thin copper Maltese cross, heavy winter clouds covered Paris for four straight days. Frustrated, he locked the unexposed plate, the cross and the uranium salt inside a pitch-dark wooden desk drawer.

On Sunday the first of March, eighteen ninety-six, although the sun had never come out, Becquerel developed the plate anyway as a baseline check — expecting at most a faint smudge.

Suddenly, in the darkroom tray, the silhouette of the Maltese cross burned jet-black at full strength. Reporting his discovery the next day, the second of March, Becquerel tested uranium crystals kept in total darkness for months. Every single one poured out the invisible rays nonstop, without sunlight, heat or electricity.

Why did most physicists walk away from Becquerel's rays — and how did a thirty-year-old Polish student in Paris measure those invisible rays without using a single photographic plate?

## PART 03: The Quartz Balance and the Pitchblende Shock (3:05–4:55)

[TEACH: Using Pierre and Jacques Curie's piezoelectric quartz electrometer in 1897–1898, Marie Curie proved that radiation is an atomic property ("radioactivity") and discovered on 12 April 1898 that raw pitchblende ore is nearly four times more active than pure uranium.]

Born Maria Skłodowska in Russian-occupied Warsaw — where women were banned from the university — she studied at night in Warsaw's secret "Floating University" and worked as a governess before boarding a train to Paris in eighteen ninety-one.

In late eighteen ninety-seven, hunting for a doctoral thesis topic in Paris, thirty-year-old Marie Curie chose Becquerel's neglected uranium rays.

Instead of blurry photographic plates, Marie used a precision instrument built by her husband Pierre Curie and his brother Jacques: the piezoelectric quartz electrometer.

As uranium rays passed through air between two metal plates, they knocked electrons off air molecules, letting a tiny current — just trillions of an ampere — leak across the gap. By hanging weights on a thin quartz crystal that generated a matching electric charge when stretched, Marie timed that minuscule current with a stopwatch.

Testing dozens of compounds — wet, dry, powdered or heated — she proved the radiation depended *only* on the number of uranium or thorium atoms present, coining the word *radioactivity*.

Then, on the twelfth of April, eighteen ninety-eight, came the shock. Marie tested raw pitchblende, the black mineral ore from Joachimsthal out of which uranium is mined.

If uranium were the only active substance in the rock, raw ore had to be weaker than pure uranium. Instead, her electrometer needle raced across the scale nearly four times faster for raw pitchblende than for pure uranium.

Even lab-made uranium crystals tested normal, while the natural rock beat them every time. Hidden inside the pitchblende rock, in trace amounts too tiny for any chemical test to see, had to be an unknown element far more radioactive than uranium.

Pierre Curie immediately dropped his own crystal research to join her hunt. When they dissolved the black rock in acid, why did they find not one new element, but two — and why did Europe's chemists still refuse to believe them?

Tracking the radiation with their electrometer after each chemical step, Marie and Pierre announced *polonium* on the eighteenth of July, eighteen ninety-eight — named after Marie's homeland, Poland — and on the twenty-sixth of December, eighteen ninety-eight, with Gustave Bémont and Eugène-Anatole Demarçay, they announced *radium*, over a million times more radioactive than uranium.

Two new elements in six months. Case closed? Wrong.

Chemists refused to believe radium existed until the Curies could weigh a pure sample and measure its atomic weight.

With no money for ore, the Curies obtained tonnes of brown waste slag left over after uranium extraction at Joachimsthal. Working in an unheated, leaky wooden dissecting shed on Rue Lhomond, they boiled twenty-kilogram cauldrons of waste by hand.

*"Sometimes I had to spend a whole day stirring a boiling mass with a heavy iron rod nearly as big as myself,"* Marie recalled. At night, the tiny dishes glowed like *"faint, fairy lights."*

Finally, in July nineteen hundred and two — after forty-five months — Marie isolated one-tenth of a gram of pure radium chloride and fixed its atomic weight at two hundred and twenty-five. In March nineteen hundred and three, Pierre measured radium pouring out a hundred calories of heat per gram every hour.

Then came a secret betrayal. In nineteen hundred and three, four French Academy members nominated Henri Becquerel and Pierre Curie for the Nobel Prize in Physics — leaving Marie off the letter.

Warned by Swedish mathematician Gösta Mittag-Leffler, Pierre declared he would refuse any prize that excluded Marie. In December nineteen hundred and three she became the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, and in nineteen eleven she won a second Nobel Prize in Chemistry alone.

Why did radium pour out heat for years without burning — and why must researchers in Paris today open a lead box just to read Marie Curie's notebooks?

## PART 05: Why Radium Glows for 1,600 Years and the Lead-Lined Notebooks (6:40–8:15)

[TEACH: Radioactivity is the spontaneous transmutation of unstable atomic nuclei (Rutherford and Soddy, 1902–1903), and because Radium-226 has a half-life of 1,600 years, Marie Curie's 1899–1902 laboratory notebooks remain stored in lead-lined boxes in Paris today.]

Many people picture pure radium glowing bright cartoon green, or wonder how a cold rock can pour out energy for centuries without breaking the laws of physics. Is that true?

In fact, pure radium salts in air glow a pale blue-violet by ionizing the nitrogen gas around them; the green glow on old watch dials came from zinc-sulfide paint struck by radiation. And as Ernest Rutherford and Frederick Soddy proved in nineteen hundred and two and nineteen hundred and three, radioactivity is the spontaneous transmutation of unstable atomic nuclei — heavy uranium and radium atoms ejecting subatomic particles as they decay into lighter elements.

Because Radium-two-two-six has a half-life of sixteen hundred years, half of the radium atoms spilled onto Marie Curie's notebooks in eighteen ninety-nine will still be decaying in the year thirty-five hundred. Marie died on the fourth of July, nineteen thirty-four, from radiation exposure, and today her notebooks at the National Library in Paris sit inside lead-lined boxes.

And when Pierre Curie measured a hundred calories of heat pouring out of a gram of radium every hour, why did physicists think it threatened the Conservation of Energy — the law discovered when Count Rumford boiled water with a blunt cannon barrel in Munich in January seventeen ninety-eight and James Prescott Joule spun a paddle wheel with falling weights in June eighteen forty-five?

A cloudy week in Paris, a copper cross in a dark drawer, and four years stirring boiling rock in a freezing shed. Together, they opened the door to the atomic age.
