### PART 02: The Cloudy Week in Paris and the Maltese Cross (1:25–3:05)
- **Cold open (scene & false hypothesis):** **Paris, 20 January – 1 March 1896**. At a meeting of the French Academy of Sciences on **20 January 1896**, mathematician **Henri Poincaré** shows the first X-ray photographs of human bones sent from Germany by **Wilhelm Röntgen** (`003`).
- Sitting in the audience is 43-year-old **Henri Becquerel** (1852–1908), whose father and grandfather had both studied **phosphorescence** (minerals that glow in the dark after being exposed to sunlight) in the exact same Paris museum laboratory.
- **Becquerel's wrong guess:** Becquerel notices that Röntgen's X-rays come from the glowing fluorescent spot on the glass vacuum tube. He jumps to a neat — and completely wrong — conclusion: what if ordinary fluorescent crystals, like **potassium uranyl sulfate** (a yellow-green uranium salt), also give off penetrating X-rays whenever **bright sunlight** makes them glow?
- On **24 February 1896**, after setting his uranium crystals in bright sunshine on top of a photographic plate wrapped in two sheets of thick black paper with a coin between them, Becquerel sees a faint shadow of the coin and reports to the Academy that sunlight triggers the rays!
- **The cloudy-day accident (26 February – 1 March 1896):** On Wednesday **26 February 1896**, Becquerel prepares a new packet — placing a thin **copper Maltese cross** between the black paper and the uranium crust — and waits for the sun. But heavy winter clouds blanket Paris for four straight days.
- Giving up on the weather, Becquerel puts the unexposed plate, the copper cross and the uranium salt away inside a **pitch-dark wooden desk drawer**.
- On Sunday **1 March 1896**, even though the uranium had never seen the sun, Becquerel decides to develop the plate anyway as a baseline check — expecting it to be almost completely blank.
- Instead, the silhouette of the Maltese cross develops **darker and sharper than ever before**! He repeats the test in total darkness with uranium salts that have *never* been exposed to light, and even non-fluorescent uranium compounds: every single one pours out the invisible rays nonstop, day and night, summer and winter!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: Paris, 20 January 1896 · WHO: Henri Poincaré (1854–1912) — French mathematician and physicist · WHEN: 24 February 1896 · WHEN: 26 February 1896 · WHEN: 1 March 1896 · FACT: No sunlight needed at all · DATA: inked exposure comparison on period paper showing Becquerel's expectation (0 hours of sunlight = 0 exposure in the dark drawer) vs the 1 March 1896 result (96 hours in a pitch-dark drawer = full-strength blackening across the Maltese cross) · SCALE: four days locked in a pitch-black drawer with zero sunlight — and the rock burned an image as strong as midday sun`
- **Ends on:** Once physicists realized uranium rays couldn't be switched off or focused like Röntgen's sharp medical X-rays, most of them lost interest and walked away. Why did a thirty-year-old Polish student in Paris pick up the forgotten mystery — and how did she measure an invisible ray without using a single photographic plate?

### PART 03: The Piezoelectric Quartz Balance and the Pitchblende Shock (3:05–4:55)
- **Cold open:** **Paris, autumn 1897 – April 1898**. Born **Maria Skłodowska** in Russian-occupied Warsaw — where women were barred from the university, forcing her to study at Warsaw's secret underground **"Floating University"** before working as a governess to fund her sister's medical studies and moving to Paris in **1891** — **Marie Curie** (1867–1934) needs a topic for her physics doctoral thesis in late **1897**.
- Instead of subjective photographic plates that only show "light" or "dark", Marie turns to a precision instrument invented by her husband **Pierre Curie** (1859–1906) and his brother **Jacques Curie** (1856–1941): the **piezoelectric quartz electrometer**.
- **How the electrometer works (mechanism):** Uranium rays knock electrons off air molecules between two metal plates (**ionization**), allowing a tiny electric current — just **a few trillionths of an ampere (`10⁻¹¹ A`)** — to leak across the air gap. To measure that minuscule current, Marie hangs weights on a thin strip of **quartz crystal** (which generates a matching electric charge when stretched) and balances the needle to a fraction of a second with a stopwatch!
- **Discovery 1 — It's an atomic property:** Testing dozens of compounds, Marie discovers that the strength of the rays has nothing to do with whether uranium is wet, dry, powdered, heated, or chemically combined with other elements: it depends **solely on the exact number of uranium atoms present** (and she finds that **thorium** emits rays too!). In her **1898** paper, she coins a brand-new word for this atomic power: **"radioactivity"**.
- **Discovery 2 — The pitchblende anomaly (April 1898):** Then Marie tests raw geological minerals from the museum collection — including **pitchblende** (uraninite, a heavy black rock from **Joachimsthal** in Bohemia from which uranium is mined).
- If uranium were the only radioactive element in the rock, raw pitchblende ore (which is only partly uranium) *must* be weaker than pure uranium. Instead, Pierre's electrometer needle races across the scale **four times faster** for raw pitchblende than for pure uranium!
- Even after she makes synthetic chalcolite in the lab (which tests normal), natural chalcolite rock tests **twice as active** as uranium. Marie's inescapable deduction (**12 April 1898**): hidden inside the tonnes of pitchblende rock in tiny trace amounts must be a **brand-new chemical element** far more radioactive than uranium!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: Paris, 1891 · WHEN: Late 1897 · WHO: Jacques Curie (1856–1941) — French mineralogist and physicist · WHEN: 12 April 1898 · FACT: Studied at secret university · DATA: inked electrometer current bar chart on period paper from Marie Curie's 12 April 1898 Academy note: Metallic Uranium = 24 picocoulombs/s (`24 × 10⁻¹² A`), Natural Chalcolite = 52 picocoulombs/s (~2x uranium), Joachimsthal Pitchblende = 83 picocoulombs/s (~3.5–4x uranium) · SCALE: 83 trillionths of an ampere — an electric current a hundred billion times smaller than a household lightbulb, measured with a stretched crystal of quartz and a stopwatch`
- **Ends on:** Pierre Curie immediately dropped his own crystal research to join Marie in the hunt for the hidden element. When they dissolved the black rock in acid, why did they find not one new element, but two — and why did the chemistry professors refuse to believe them?

### PART 04: The Freezing Dissecting Shed, 0.1 Gram of Radium, and the 1903 Nobel Letter — Midpoint Twist (4:55–6:40)
- **Cold open ("Case closed? Wrong."):** **Rue Lhomond, Paris, July 1898 – December 1903**. Tracking the radiation with their electrometer after each chemical separation, Marie and Pierre announce two new elements in **1898**: first **polonium** on **18 July 1898** (400 times more active than uranium, named after Marie's partitioned homeland **Poland**), and then, with **Gustave Bémont** (1857–1932) on **26 December 1898**, **radium** — hidden in the barium fraction and **over a million times more radioactive than uranium**!
- **Why chemists didn't believe them:** Although spectroscopist **Eugène-Anatole Demarçay** (1852–1903) spotted a faint new violet spectral line, orthodox chemists scoffed: *"Show us the element on a weighing scale and measure its atomic weight, or it's just a phantom!"*
- **Four years in the freezing shed (1898–1902):** With no money to buy expensive uranium ore, the Curies realise the radium stays behind in the useless **brown waste slag** thrown away after uranium is extracted at the Joachimsthal glassworks! The Austrian government gives them **several tonnes** of waste residue, and the Paris School of Physics lets them work in an abandoned, leaky wooden **medical-school dissecting shed** on Rue Lhomond — freezing in winter, stifling in summer, with a glass roof that leaked rain and **no fume hoods**.
- Stirring boiling **20-kilogram cauldrons** with an iron bar nearly as tall as herself (*"Sometimes I had to spend a whole day stirring a boiling mass with a heavy iron rod nearly as big as myself,"* Marie wrote) and performing thousands of fractional crystallizations, in **July 1902** — after **45 months** — Marie finally weighs out **one-tenth of a gram (`0.1 g`) of pure radium chloride**, fixing radium's atomic weight at **`225`** (modern **`226.025`**)!
- In the dark shed at night, the tiny glass tubes of radium salt glow like ***"faint, fairy lights"*** — and in **March 1903**, Pierre Curie and **Albert Laborde** discover that radium continuously gives off **100 calories of heat per gram every single hour** without ever cooling down or losing visible weight!
- **The midpoint twist — the 1903 Nobel betrayal stopped in time:** In **1903**, four members of the French Academy of Sciences (including Gabriel Lippmann) send a secret letter to Stockholm nominating **Henri Becquerel** and **Pierre Curie** for the Nobel Prize in Physics — **completely omitting Marie's name**!
- Swedish mathematician **Gösta Mittag-Leffler** (1846–1927), a champion of women in science, quietly writes to Pierre to warn him. Pierre writes back firmly that any Nobel Prize on radioactivity that leaves out Marie Curie would be a travesty and he cannot accept it alone. The committee adds Marie's name (using her 1898 priority publication), and in **December 1903** she becomes the **first woman in history to win a Nobel Prize** — before winning a **second Nobel Prize in Chemistry alone in 1911**!
- *Markers:* `· WHEN: 18 July 1898 · WHO: Gustave Bémont (1857–1932) — Paris laboratory chief · WHEN: 26 December 1898 · WHO: Eugène-Anatole Demarçay (1852–1903) — French spectroscopist · WHEN: July 1902 · WHEN: March 1903 · WHO: Gösta Mittag-Leffler (1846–1927) — Swedish mathematician · WHEN: December 1903 · WHEN: 1911 · FACT: 2 Nobels in 2 sciences`
- **Ends on:** Marie and Pierre kept glowing vials of radium by their bedside and refused to patent the extraction process so hospitals everywhere could treat cancer for free. Yet why did radium pour out heat year after year without burning — and why must visitors to Paris today sign a radiation waiver and open a lead box just to read Marie Curie's notebooks?

### PART 05: Why Radium Glows for 1,600 Years & the Lead-Lined Notebooks (6:40–8:15)
- **Cold open (popular misconception debunked):** Many people think Marie Curie's radium itself glows bright neon green like in cartoons, or that radioactivity violates the conservation of energy. Is that true?
- **No:** pure radium salts in air actually glow a **pale blue-violet** (mostly from ionizing the nitrogen in the air around the crystal; green glow only happens when alpha particles hit a zinc-sulfide phosphor painted on watch dials!). And radium doesn't create energy out of nothing: as **Ernest Rutherford** (`004`) and **Frederick Soddy** proved in **1902–1903**, radioactivity is **spontaneous nuclear transmutation** — unstable heavy nuclei (`Uranium-238` → `Radium-226` → `Radon-222` → `Polonium-210` → `Lead-206`) ejecting alpha and beta particles at thousands of kilometres per second as they decay into lighter elements!
- Because **Radium-226 has a half-life of 1,600 years**, half of the radium atoms spilled onto Marie Curie's clothes, furniture and paper laboratory notebooks in **1899–1902** will still be decaying in the year **3500**!
- Marie Curie died on **4 July 1934** at age 66 of aplastic anaemia from decades of radiation exposure (and building mobile X-ray vans — *"petites Curies"* — that examined over a million wounded soldiers in World War I). Today, her original 1899–1902 laboratory notebooks at the **Bibliothèque Nationale de France** still set off Geiger counters and are stored inside **lead-lined boxes**.
- **After-act hand-off (7:45–8:15):**
  - And when Pierre Curie measured 100 calories of heat pouring out of a single gram of radium every hour in 1903, physicists thought it broke the most sacred law in science: the **Conservation of Energy**.
  - How did humanity discover that heat is really mechanical motion in the first place — when **Count Rumford** boiled water with a blunt cannon barrel in Munich in **1798** and **James Prescott Joule** spun a brass paddle wheel with falling weights in **1845** (`024` candidate *Thermodynamics: Rumford's Cannon & Joule's Paddle Wheel*)?
- *Markers:* `· WHO: Frederick Soddy (1877–1956) — English radiochemist · WHEN: 4 July 1934 · FACT: Notebooks need lead for 1,600 yr · WHEN: 1798 · WHEN: 1845 · DATA: animated timeline building 26 Feb–1 Mar 1896 (Becquerel's cloudy-day drawer & Maltese cross) · 12 Apr 1898 (Marie Curie's pitchblende electrometer anomaly, 4x uranium) · 18 Jul & 26 Dec 1898 (Polonium and Radium announced) · Jul 1902 (0.1 g pure radium chloride isolated from tonnes of ore, atomic weight 225/226) · Dec 1903 (Becquerel, Pierre & Marie Curie share Physics Nobel) · 1911 (Marie Curie wins Chemistry Nobel alone) · 3500 AD (one Radium-226 half-life of 1,600 years later) · SCALE: a half-life of 1,600 years — if a Roman emperor had spilled radium in 400 AD, half of those exact atoms would still be firing radiation today`
