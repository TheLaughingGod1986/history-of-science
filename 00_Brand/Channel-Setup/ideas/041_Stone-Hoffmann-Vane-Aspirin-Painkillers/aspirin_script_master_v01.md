Why would anyone chew on the bark of a riverbank tree to stop a fever?

For more than three thousand five hundred years — from the ancient Egyptian **Ebers Papyrus** around fifteen fifty BC to the Greek physician Hippocrates — healers knew that brewing tea from **willow leaves and bark** cooled burning fevers and dulled pain.

Yet in Europe, that ancient secret was almost forgotten — until a summer day in **seventeen fifty-eight** near Chipping Norton in Oxfordshire. Walking along a damp riverbank, English clergyman **Reverend Edward Stone** peeled a strip of bark from a **white willow tree** and touched it to his tongue. Suddenly, he was stunned by its fierce, biting bitterness — the exact same bitter taste as expensive **Peruvian cinchona bark**, the rare South American quinine tree used to treat malaria fevers, then called **"agues"**!

Suspecting that a tree growing in wet English marshes might cure the fevers caught in those very marshes, Stone stripped a pound of willow bark, dried it inside a bag on the outside of a **baker's bread oven** for three months, and pounded it into powder. Over the next **five years**, he gave small twenty-grain doses — about one point three grams — every four hours to **fifty fever-stricken patients**. Every single patient recovered! On the **twenty-fifth of April seventeen sixty-three**, Stone's landmark letter was read before the **Royal Society in London**.

Sixty-five years later, in eighteen twenty-eight, European chemists isolated the active yellow crystal from willow bark and named it **salicin**, after *Salix*, the Latin word for willow. In **eighteen thirty-eight**, Italian chemist Raffaele Piria converted salicin into a far stronger compound called **salicylic acid** — also found inside wild **meadowsweet flowers**, *Spiraea*. By **eighteen seventy-six**, Scottish doctor Thomas Maclagan proved these willow compounds dramatically calmed swollen, painful joints!

There was just one agonising problem: patients had to swallow **four to six grams of raw salicylic acid every day** — and the acid was so caustic it burned the mouth, caused violent ringing in the ears, and made patients' stomachs bleed!

How did a twenty-nine-year-old chemist in a German dye factory — desperate to spare his own father from that stomach-burning acid — tame the molecule on the tenth of August eighteen ninety-seven?

In the eighteen nineties, the **Friedrich Bayer** dye works in Elberfeld, Germany, opened a new pharmaceutical laboratory directed by chemist Arthur Eichengrün and pharmacology boss Heinrich Dreser.

At one of the benches stood twenty-nine-year-old chemist **Felix Hoffmann**. Every day, Hoffmann watched his elderly father suffer twice: first from crippling arthritis, and second from the corrosive salicylic acid potions that made his father vomit in pain.

Building on an impure reaction first tried by French chemist Charles Gerhardt in eighteen fifty-three, Hoffmann set out to disguise the acid's biting edge. On the **tenth of August eighteen ninety-seven**, Hoffmann heated salicylic acid with **acetic anhydride**, snipping off one harsh hydrogen atom and replacing it with a protective chemical cap called an **acetyl group**. When the solution cooled, it formed **one hundred percent pure, sparkling white needles of acetylsalicylic acid**!

Case closed? Wrong! When Hoffmann and Arthur Eichengrün handed their gentle new painkiller to pharmacology chief Heinrich Dreser, Dreser **refused to test or sell it**!

Why would a drug company boss reject the greatest painkiller in history? First, Dreser insisted without proof that salicylic compounds **"enfeebled and poisoned the heart."** Second, Dreser was completely obsessed with *another* molecule that Felix Hoffmann had acetylated just **eleven days later, on the twenty-first of August eighteen ninety-seven**: acetylated morphine, which Dreser proudly launched as a "non-addictive" children's cough syrup named **Heroin**!

Refusing to let Hoffmann's painkiller die on the shelf, Arthur Eichengrün secretly swallowed large doses of acetylsalicylic acid himself — feeling zero stomach pain — and smuggled samples to doctors in Berlin! When dentist patients and fever wards reported miraculous relief without a trace of nausea, Dreser was forced to relent. On the **sixth of March eighteen ninety-nine**, Bayer registered the name **Aspirin** — **"A"** for acetyl, **"-spir-"** for the *Spiraea* meadowsweet flower, and **"-in"**!

Yet here is the strangest riddle in medical history: how did doctors prescribe **hundreds of billions of aspirin tablets** over the next **seventy-four years** without anyone knowing how an aspirin pill actually stops pain?

When you swallow an aspirin tablet for a stubbed toe or a toothache, how does the pill in your stomach "know" where the pain is?

The secret is: **it doesn't!** Once dissolved into your bloodstream, aspirin molecules travel **everywhere** in your body at once. So why do you only feel relief in your injured toe?

For seventy-four years after eighteen ninety-seven, nobody knew. Then, at the Royal College of Surgeons in London, forty-four-year-old British pharmacologist **John Vane** solved the mystery — publishing his discovery in *Nature New Biology* on the **twenty-third of June nineteen seventy-one** and winning the **nineteen eighty-two Nobel Prize**!

Vane discovered that whenever cells in your body are damaged or infected, a tunnel-shaped enzyme inside those cells called **cyclooxygenase** — or **COX** — grabs fatty acids from the cell wall and turns them into tiny local distress flares called **prostaglandins**.

Unlike hormones that travel through the whole body, prostaglandins act right where they are made: they make local blood vessels swell up, command your brain's thermostat to trigger a **fever**, and sensitise nearby nerve endings so the slightest touch shouts **"PAIN!"**

As Gerald Roth and Philip Majerus proved in **nineteen seventy-five**, when an aspirin molecule enters that COX enzyme tunnel, it does something no other painkiller does: it **permanently snaps off its acetyl group** — the exact cap Felix Hoffmann attached in eighteen ninety-seven — and welds it onto a single amino acid inside the tunnel, blocking the enzyme by more than ninety-five percent! Because only your injured cells are frantically churning out pain prostaglandins, plugging the enzyme silences the alarm **right where you hurt**!

Because John Vane and his colleagues discovered that tiny **blood platelets** use that exact same COX enzyme to manufacture a sticky chemical called **thromboxane**, which makes platelets clump together into arterial blood clots that trigger **heart attacks and strokes**!

Unlike ordinary body cells, blood platelets have no nucleus — so once a tiny **seventy-five-milligram low-dose aspirin** snaps its acetyl lock inside a platelet's enzyme tunnel, that platelet cannot build a new enzyme for its entire **eight-to-ten-day lifespan**!
