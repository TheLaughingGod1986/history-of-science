In the nineteen sixties, biology textbooks stated as settled fact that no bacterium could ever live above **fifty-five or sixty degrees Celsius**, because heat unravels living proteins the way boiling water cooks a raw egg.

Yet when Indiana University microbiologist **Thomas Brock** visited **Yellowstone National Park**, he noticed vibrant pink, orange, and yellow ribbons waving right inside the scalding outflow streams of geothermal geysers!

On the **nineteenth of September nineteen sixty-six**, Brock and his twenty-year-old undergraduate student **Hudson Freeze** dipped microscope slides into the **seventy-degree Celsius** waters of **Mushroom Spring**. Thriving happily at temperatures up to **seventy-nine degrees** — hot enough to scald human skin in a single second — was a remarkable rod-shaped microbe they named ***Thermus aquaticus***, published in **August nineteen sixty-nine**!

Crucially for all of humanity, Brock placed a living sample of his Yellowstone microbe — **strain `YT-1`** — into the public **American Type Culture Collection**, free for any scientist on Earth to study. And in **nineteen seventy-six** at the University of Cincinnati, graduate student **Alice Chien**, **David Edgar**, and **John Trela** isolated the secret weapon that let the microbe copy its DNA without cooking: a heat-proof enzyme called **`Taq` DNA polymerase**, which works fastest at a blistering **seventy-five to eighty degrees Celsius**!

For seven years, `Taq` polymerase sat quietly in laboratory freezers as a charming curiosity of nature. How did a late-night drive through the California redwoods in **April nineteen eighty-three** turn that Yellowstone enzyme into the most powerful copying machine in biology?

By the early nineteen eighties, geneticists knew how to cut and read DNA, yet finding one human gene in a drop of blood was an agonising nightmare — because **three billion letters** of background DNA drowned that single gene out.

On a Friday night in **April nineteen eighty-three**, Cetus Corporation chemist **Kary Mullis** was driving along **California Highway 128** through the moonlit redwoods when a dazzlingly simple mathematical trick hit him. Instead of hunting for one tiny sentence inside a three-billion-letter library, what if you bracket just the start and end of that sentence with two short synthetic DNA bookmarks called **primers**?

First, heat the test tube to **ninety-five degrees Celsius** so the DNA double helix melts apart into two single strands. Second, cool the tube to **fifty-four degrees** so your two primers lock onto the exact target gene. Third, warm it to **seventy-two degrees** and let **DNA polymerase** copy both strands — turning one DNA molecule into **two**, then **four**, **eight**, **sixteen**, and after **thirty cycles**, **one billion seventy-three million seven hundred and forty-one thousand eight hundred and twenty-four copies**!

Case closed — did Mullis's chain reaction immediately automate DNA testing when he first ran it on the **sixteenth of December nineteen eighty-three** and published it with **Randall Saiki** and **Henry Erlich** on the **twentieth of December nineteen eighty-five**? Wrong! Every single time they heated the tube to **ninety-five degrees** to unzip the DNA, the heat **boiled their ordinary *E. coli* enzyme to death**! Technicians had to sit beside water baths for hours, opening the cap after every cycle to pipette in fresh enzyme thirty times in a row!

Then Mullis and Saiki remembered **Thomas Brock's Yellowstone bacterium** sitting in the public culture collection! When they put **`Taq` polymerase** into the tube — published on the **twenty-ninth of January nineteen eighty-eight** and winning Mullis the **nineteen ninety-three** Nobel Prize — the geyser enzyme laughed at ninety-five-degree heat, letting a machine copy DNA a billion-fold inside one sealed droplet!

Meanwhile, in **Leicester, England**, what did geneticist **Alec Jeffreys** see on a dripping X-ray film at **nine-oh-five a.m. on Monday the tenth of September nineteen eighty-four** that turned DNA into the ultimate witness in every courtroom on Earth?

Any two human beings on Earth share **ninety-nine point nine per cent** of the exact same DNA sequence. So how could a scientist ever tell two people apart from a microscopic trace of blood or saliva?

At the University of Leicester, thirty-four-year-old geneticist **Alec Jeffreys** was studying strange, stuttering stretches of human DNA called **minisatellites** — places where a short genetic phrase repeats itself over and over along the chromosome. Everyone carries the same repeating phrase, yet the **exact number of stutters** varies wildly from person to person!

At **nine-oh-five a.m. on Monday the tenth of September nineteen eighty-four**, Jeffreys pulled a wet X-ray film out of his darkroom developing tank showing DNA from his lab technician and her parents. Staring at the dark horizontal bars — like a **personal supermarket barcode, half inherited from mother and half from father** — Jeffreys saw that every person on Earth (except identical twins) carries an unmistakable **DNA fingerprint**, published on the **seventh of March nineteen eighty-five** in *Nature*!

When forensic scientists married **Alec Jeffreys's repeating DNA markers** to **Kary Mullis and Thomas Brock's `PCR` photocopier**, detectives no longer needed a large bloodstain: they could build a one-in-a-billion DNA profile from a **single human cell**, a licked postage stamp, or — in the hands of **Svante Pääbo** — a **forty-thousand-year-old Neanderthal bone**!

Yet when police first brought Alec Jeffreys a murder case in **nineteen eighty-six to nineteen eighty-seven**, why did his test shock every detective in the room?

Many people still believe the very first criminal DNA test in **nineteen eighty-six** was used to lock up the police's prime suspect. Why is the true story the exact opposite?

Because in nineteen eighty-six, a terrified seventeen-year-old kitchen porter named **Richard Buckland** had falsely confessed under questioning to a murder in Leicestershire! Police were certain he was guilty — until Alec Jeffreys compared the crime-scene DNA with Buckland's blood and proved on the spot that **the boy was one hundred per cent innocent**, making the very first triumph of forensic DNA the **saving of an innocent teenager** before a community screen caught the real killer, Colin Pitchfork!

`PCR` and DNA fingerprinting gave humanity a molecular photocopier to **read** any page in the three-billion-letter book of life. But what if a child is born with a deadly single-letter **typo** inside their DNA? How could you reach inside a living cell, find that one misspelled word among three billion letters, and **snip it out with surgical precision**? Why did Japanese biologist **Yoshizumi Ishino in December nineteen eighty-seven** and Spanish microbiologist **Francisco Mojica in February two thousand and five** find strange repeating palindromes in bacteria (`CRISPR`) that turned out to be a **three-billion-year-old mugshot album of enemy viruses** — how did dairy researchers **Rodolphe Barrangou and Philippe Horvath on the twenty-third of March two thousand and seven** prove **yogurt bacteria** use those mugshots to slice viruses in half — and how did **Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna on the twenty-eighth of June twenty twelve** turn that yogurt shield into programmable **`CRISPR-Cas9` gene scissors**?

A glass slide in a boiling Yellowstone puddle, a moonlight drive through the redwoods, and a nine-oh-five morning X-ray. Together, they turned a single invisible strand of DNA into a billion unbreakable witnesses for the truth.
