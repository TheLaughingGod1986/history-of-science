For over three hundred years, anatomists stared at two tiny triangular glands sitting like little hats on top of your kidneys — the **adrenal glands** — and asked a baffling question: why do we have them, and what on Earth are they for?

In **eighteen fifty-five**, London physician **Thomas Addison** discovered that patients whose adrenal glands were damaged wasted away and died. Then, in **March eighteen ninety-four**, a Yorkshire physician named **George Oliver** — who had been testing sheep adrenal extracts and measuring radial arteries with a spring gauge — walked into the University College London laboratory of Professor **Edward Schäfer** holding a tiny glass vial.

Schäfer thought it was nonsense, until he injected a few drops of Oliver's adrenal extract into a vein to humour his guest. What happened next stunned them both: within seconds, **every artery clamped tight**, and the mercury float in Schäfer's blood-pressure gauge **shot almost out the top of the glass tube**!

A global race broke out to purify the mystery chemical inside the gland. After **John Jacob Abel** at Johns Hopkins isolated an impure derivative he called *"epinephrine"*, Japanese chemist **Jokichi Takamine** and his twenty-four-year-old assistant **Keizo Uenaka** working in a New York laboratory on the **twenty-first of July nineteen hundred** (`and patented in nineteen oh-one`) precipitated sparkling white needle crystals of the **very first pure hormone in human history — `adrenaline`**!

Adrenaline proved a gland could dump an emergency chemical into the blood. Yet for everyday digestion and organ control, the great Russian physiologist **Ivan Pavlov** insisted that **electrical nerve wires** did all the talking. What happened on the afternoon of the **sixteenth of January nineteen oh-two** when two brothers-in-law in London cut every single nerve?

Case closed — when acidic food leaves your stomach, electrical nerve wires telegraph your pancreas to pour out digestive juice! Wrong! Even when you cut **every single nerve wire** in the gut, the pancreas still fires right on schedule!

Whenever acidic food squirts out of your stomach into the upper small intestine, your **pancreas** immediately pours out alkaline juice to neutralise the acid and digest your meal. In St. Petersburg, **Ivan Pavlov** — who won the nineteen oh-four Nobel Prize — taught that acid in the gut triggers nerve endings that wire straight to the brain and back to the pancreas.

On the afternoon of **Thursday, the sixteenth of January nineteen oh-two**, at University College London, physiologists and brothers-in-law **William Bayliss** and **Ernest Starling** decided to test that nerve reflex. Under anaesthesia, they cut the vagus nerves, removed the nerve ganglia, and **sliced away every single microscopic nerve fibre** around a loop of upper intestine — leaving the loop attached to the body **only by its blood vessels**!

Then they dripped a few drops of **zero-point-four percent hydrochloric acid** into the nerve-free loop. Two minutes later, **the pancreas began pouring out digestive juice just as fast as before**!

*"Then it must be a chemical reflex!"* Starling exclaimed. Why couldn't it just be the acid itself riding in the blood? Because injecting acid straight into a vein did nothing! Right there at the bench, Starling scraped the lining off the intestine, **ground it in a mortar with sand and weak acid**, filtered it, and injected the liquid into a vein. Instantly, the pancreas gushed out a torrent of juice! They named the chemical messenger **`secretin`**!

Bayliss and Starling had uncovered a brand-new communication system in the body. What should science call these blood-borne chemical letters — and how did a dinner in **Cambridge in nineteen oh-five** give us the word **"hormone"**?

How do you name a discovery that rewrites all of physiology? Needing a single word for chemicals made in one organ and carried by the blood to awaken another, **Ernest Starling** sat down to dinner at Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, with biologist **William Hardy** and classical scholar **William Vesey**.

Vesey suggested the Greek participle **`hormōn`**, meaning **"to excite"** or **"to set in motion"** — and on the **twentieth of June nineteen oh-five**, in his Croonian Lecture at the Royal College of Physicians in London, Starling gave the world the word **"hormone"**!

Once scientists knew to hunt for chemical letters in the blood, a golden age of medicine exploded: on **Christmas Day, the twenty-fifth of December nineteen fourteen**, biochemist **Edward Kendall** crystallised the thyroid hormone **`thyroxine`**, which sets the metabolic speed of every cell (`and later isolated adrenal cortisone`); in **nineteen twenty-one**, **Banting and Best** isolated **`insulin`**; and in **nineteen fifty-nine**, medical physicist **Rosalyn Yalow** and **Solomon Berson** invented **radioimmunoassay** (`winning the nineteen seventy-seven Nobel Prize`), a detector so sensitive it could measure hormones down to **one trillionth of a gram** — like spotting a single lump of sugar dissolved in a sixty-mile lake!

Many people still picture the **nervous system** and the **hormone system** as two separate worlds — just as **Ivan Pavlov** and **Ernest Starling** once clashed over which one ran the body. Why did both turn out to be half of the same masterpiece?

Because your body needs **both**: electrical nerves are like **high-speed telegraph wires** that flick a single muscle in milliseconds, while hormones are like **wireless radio broadcasts** through your blood that coordinate trillions of cells across hours, days, and decades — and right at the base of your brain, the **hypothalamus** links the two together!

That raises a fascinating question: every morning before you even open your eyes, your glands release a surge of **cortisol** and your body temperature rises to get you ready for the day. How do your glands and cells know **what time it is**? Back in **seventeen twenty-nine**, French astronomer **Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan** locked a sensitive plant inside a **pitch-black cupboard** in Paris — and discovered its leaves still opened at sunrise and folded at sunset without ever seeing the Sun! How did **Jürgen Aschoff's nineteen sixty-two underground bunker**, **Seymour Benzer and Ronald Konopka's September nineteen seventy-one mutant fruit flies**, and **Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash, and Michael Young** prove that **almost every single cell in your body carries its own self-winding twenty-four-hour molecular clock**?

A vial of adrenal extract, a loop of nerve-cut intestine in London, and a Greek word over dinner in Cambridge. Together, they revealed the secret chemical letters flowing through your blood.
