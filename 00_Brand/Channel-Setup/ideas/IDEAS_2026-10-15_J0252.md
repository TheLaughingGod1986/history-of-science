## The five candidates

### 1. Genetics: Gregor Mendel's 28,000 pea plants (recommended for 012)
- **Familiar thing:** why a child has their mother's eyes or their grandfather's red hair when neither parent has it.
- **Person & room:** Gregor Johann Mendel (1822–1884), an Augustinian friar at St Thomas's Abbey in Brünn (Brno) who failed his teacher's certification exam twice, working in a 35-by-7-metre walled monastery garden and a warm candlelit abbey study.
- **Date & proof:** Between **1856 and 1863**, Mendel grew and hand-pollinated about **28,000 pea plants** with a fine paintbrush and tiny calico bags, counting **7,324 seeds** in one trial alone (**5,474 round to 1,850 wrinkled** — a **2.96 to 1** ratio). He read his paper on **8 February and 8 March 1865** to about 40 people at the Natural Science Society in Brünn.
- **Twist:** Nobody in the room understood what he had proved. When he sent his paper to the famous Munich botanist **Carl von Nägeli**, Nägeli urged him to test his laws on **hawkweed (*Hieracium*)** — not knowing hawkweed reproduces asexually without fertilization (apomixis), so every cross failed and Mendel's own 3:1 law seemed to break! Mendel died in 1884, his successor burned his papers, and **35 years after his 1865 talk**, in the spring of **1900**, three botanists across Europe (Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns, Erich von Tschermak) independently worked out the 3:1 ratio — only to discover the dead monk had published it in 1866.
- **Neighbours (`n_mendel.md` — gate PASS, 8 big on-topic):**

### 2. Evolution: Darwin's secret notebooks & the letter that forced his hand
- **Familiar thing:** why a bird's beak or an animal's coat fits the place it lives.
- **Story:** After visiting the Galápagos in **September–October 1835**, young Charles Darwin didn't even label which island his birds came from — thinking they were blackbirds, wrens and gross-beaks until London ornithologist **John Gould** told him in **March 1837** they were 13 species of finch. Darwin sketched his "I think" tree in Notebook B (July 1837), wrote a 230-page essay in **1844**, and locked it away for 14 more years — until **18 June 1858**, when a post packet arrived from **Alfred Russel Wallace**, shivering with malaria on the island of Ternate, setting out the exact same theory.
- **Twist:** Darwin and Wallace's papers were read together at the Linnean Society in London on **1 July 1858**; neither man was there (Darwin was burying his baby son Charles Waring), and at the annual meeting in May 1859 the Society's president, **Thomas Bell**, remarked that the year 1858 had not been marked by any striking discovery that revolutionized science!

- **Search (`public_search.md`):** `"how darwin discovered evolution"` and `"charles darwin natural selection"` both autocomplete (`yes`).

### 3. Radioactivity: Becquerel's dark drawer & Marie Curie's shed
- **Familiar thing:** why certain rocks give off invisible energy without any power source.
- **Story:** In late **February 1896**, Paris was overcast for days, so **Henri Becquerel** locked his uranium salt crystal and a black-wrapped photographic plate inside a dark desk drawer. On **1 March 1896** he developed the plate anyway, expecting a blank — and found the crystal's shadow burned black without sunlight. Two years later (**July & December 1898**), **Marie and Pierre Curie** boiled tons of waste pitchblende in a leaky former medical-school shed in Paris to isolate polonium and radium.
- **Twist:** The Curies spent four years stirring cauldrons with an iron rod nearly as tall as Marie to isolate a single decigram (0.1 g) of radium chloride — and their 1890s notebooks remain so radioactive today (radium-226 half-life: 1,600 years) that readers at the Bibliothèque Nationale in Paris must sign a waiver and fetch them from lead-lined boxes.
- **Neighbours (`n_radioactivity.md` — gate PASS, 3 strictly on-topic ≥ 1M):** TED-Ed *The genius of Marie Curie* (4.14M), FuseSchool *Stable and Unstable Nuclei | Radioactivity* (1.04M), TED-Ed *Radioactivity: Expect the unexpected* (1.01M).
- **Why #3:** Close neighbour to 003 (*X-rays*, 1895) and 004 (*Inside an Atom*, Rutherford). Better after a gap from 003/004.

### 4. Insulin: Banting, Best & the miracle on Ward 38
- **Familiar thing:** blood sugar and the tiny vial that turned a fatal diagnosis into a manageable condition.
- **Story:** At 2 a.m. on **31 October 1920**, debt-ridden Ontario surgeon **Frederick Banting** woke from sleep and scribbled 25 words in his notebook. In **May 1921**, he and 22-year-old student **Charles Best** (who won a coin toss for the first shift) began working in a stifling borrowed room at the University of Toronto; with biochemist **James Collip** purifying the extract, they gave a purified dose to 14-year-old **Leonard Thompson** on **23 January 1922**.
- **Twist:** When the 1923 Nobel Prize was awarded to Banting and lab director **J. J. R. Macleod** (who had been on holiday in Scotland during the summer of 1921), Banting was so outraged Best was left out that he immediately gave Best half his prize money — prompting Macleod to give half of his to Collip — and Banting, Best and Collip sold the patent to the University of Toronto for **$1 each**.
- **Neighbours (`n_insulin.md` — gate PASS, 3 big on diabetes/insulin):** TEDx 4.35M and 3.57M, TED Peter Attia 2.73M; Khan Academy *Glucose Insulin and Diabetes* 756k; TED-Ed *What did dogs teach humans about diabetes?* 391k.
- **Why #4:** Strong search (`"how insulin was discovered"` autocompletes), but its 1M+ neighbours are modern clinical/diet talks rather than TED-Ed animations, and it sits in the same hospital lane as 005, 007 and 011.

### 5. Cholera: John Snow, Henry Whitehead & the Broad Street pump handle
- **Familiar thing:** clean tap water.
- **Story:** In the Soho cholera outbreak of **31 August – 8 September 1854**, **John Snow** walked street by street timing how far each victim lived from the **Broad Street water pump** and persuaded the parish board to remove the pump handle on **8 September 1854**.
- **Twist:** The famous dot map didn't cause the handle to come off (Snow drew it months later, in December 1854), and the sceptics were only convinced when local curate **Reverend Henry Whitehead** — who set out to prove Snow *wrong* — discovered that the baby at **40 Broad Street** whose soiled nappies had leaked into the well 3 feet away was the index case, and a widow miles away in Hampstead died because she had a bottle of Broad Street water delivered by cart every day for its taste!
- **Neighbours (`n_cholera.md` — gate PASS on paper, 5 big, no TED-Ed):** Extra History's 4-part Broad Street Pump series (2.88M, 2.04M, 1.95M, 1.23M); no 1M+ TED-Ed neighbour.
- **Why #5:** John Snow already appears in 011 (*Surgery before anaesthesia*, Part 06) and waterborne germs sit close to 001 (*Germs*).
