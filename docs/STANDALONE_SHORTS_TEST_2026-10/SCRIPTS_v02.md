## Locked spoken lines (v02)

1. In 1800, a thermometer found light no one could see. William Herschel split sunlight with a prism and held thermometers in each colour. Red was warmer than violet. Then he moved one past the red, into the dark. It got hotter still. He'd found infrared: invisible light, just past the red.
2. In 1669, a man boiled urine to make gold. Hennig Brand, in Hamburg, hoped to find gold hiding inside it. He boiled it down to a black sludge. No gold. Instead, a white substance that glowed in the dark. He'd found phosphorus, the first element with a known discoverer.
3. In 1854, a killer was hiding in a water pump. Cholera was killing hundreds in Soho, London, and many still blamed bad air. John Snow tracked where the dead had lived. They crowded round one pump on Broad Street. He had its handle taken off. The killer was in the water.
4. Marie Curie's notebooks are still radioactive. In 1898, she and Pierre found two new elements: polonium, then radium. Radium glowed in the dark, and she handled it for years with no protection. Today her papers sit in lead-lined boxes in Paris. Radium takes sixteen hundred years to lose half its strength.
5. In 1964, the afterglow of the Big Bang was blamed on pigeons. Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson picked up a strange noise in their giant antenna. Pigeons were nesting inside, leaving droppings. But the noise wasn't the pigeons. It came from every direction in the sky: the afterglow of the Big Bang.
6. In 1928, a mouldy dish gave us penicillin. Alexander Fleming came back from holiday to old dishes of bacteria. On one, a fuzzy mould had landed. All around it, the bacteria were dead. He called it mould juice: penicillin. Others later turned it into a medicine that has saved millions.
7. In 1771, a sprig of mint brought dead air back to life. Joseph Priestley let a candle burn out in a closed jar. The air was spent. He slipped in a sprig of mint and waited ten days. Then a fresh candle burned perfectly well. The plant had remade the air.
8. A horse's blood climbed eight feet up a glass tube. Stephen Hales, a vicar, wanted to measure the push of a heartbeat. He fixed a tall tube into a mare's artery. The blood rose taller than a man, bobbing with every beat. In 1733 he published the first blood pressure reading.
