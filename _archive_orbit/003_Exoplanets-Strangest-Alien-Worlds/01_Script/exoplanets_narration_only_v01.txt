SCENE 01 — Cold open: Orbit's question

If alien worlds are real… what are they actually like? Diamond crusts? Glass rain? Three suns in the sky?
Today, Orbit is going on a tour.
Not a fantasy tour. A tour of exoplanets — real planets around other stars — and the strangest ones our instruments have found so far.
This is exoplanets explained the Orbit way: one world at a time, with the science we actually have… and the wonder we cannot help feeling.
Every point of light in that sky might be a sun. And around many of those suns, we now know, there are worlds. Not guesses on a poster. Measurements in a database.
Orbit is about to visit the strangest entries on that list — carefully, honestly, and only as far as the evidence allows.

SCENE 02 — What is an exoplanet?

An exoplanet is simply a planet that orbits a star other than our Sun.
For most of human history, every world we could point to lived in one address: the Solar System. Earth. Mars. Jupiter. Neighbours we could see, or at least visit with probes.
Then the map changed.
In nineteen ninety-two, astronomers confirmed planets around a pulsar — a dead, spinning star — a discovery so strange it almost did not count as “normal” planets in people’s minds. Then, in nineteen ninety-five, came fifty-one Pegasi b: a world around a Sun-like star. The first of a new era.
Astronomers started finding planets by the shadows they cast and the tiny tugs they give their stars. Not photographs of cities. Not messages. Tiny, careful clues — repeated, measured, argued over, and confirmed.
Today, thousands of exoplanets are known. Many more candidates wait in the data. The count climbs every year as telescopes sharpen and catalogues merge.
And the catalogue is not a list of Earth copies.
It is a museum of extremes.
Some are smaller than Earth. Some are many times larger than Jupiter. Some hug their stars so closely they complete an orbit in hours. Some wander alone in the dark, with no sun at all — rogue planets ejected from their birth systems.
Alien worlds are not one species. They are a whole vocabulary of what “planet” can mean when you stop assuming our Solar System is the template.
That is the first lesson Orbit wants you to carry: the universe did not read our textbooks before it built worlds.

SCENE 03 — How we find alien worlds (without flying there)

We do not visit these places. Not yet.
The nearest stars are still trillions of kilometres away. Our fastest probes would need tens of thousands of years to reach even the closest. So we catch exoplanets the way you catch a moth’s shadow on a lamp.
When a planet crosses in front of its star, the starlight dips — a transit. Measure the dip carefully, and you learn size. Watch many dips, and you learn the year of that world — how long it takes to orbit. Watch the depth of the dip over years, and you can even learn whether the orbit is tilted, eccentric, or slowly shifting.
Or we feel a star’s tiny wobble. A planet’s gravity pulls. The star shifts. The light’s colour fingerprint slides by a whisper — a Doppler shift. That whisper can reveal mass. Combine mass and size, and you can estimate density. Density is where geology starts whispering back.
Sometimes atmospheres leave fingerprints too — gases that steal particular colours of starlight as it filters through the rim of a world during a transit. Water vapour. Carbon dioxide. Sodium. Silicates. Each molecule has a favourite colour to absorb.
That is how alien worlds become science, not speculation: patterns that refuse to go away when you check again.
And missions like Kepler and TESS turned transit hunting into a census — thousands of candidates from patches of sky, many confirmed, many still waiting for follow-up. Ground-based observatories chase the wobble. Space telescopes like Hubble and JWST stare at atmospheres when a world passes in front of its star.
Three tricks. One goal: turn a point of light into a world with a size, a mass, a year, and sometimes a smell.
And once you can measure a world… you can ask the dangerous, wonderful question:
What would it be like to stand there?
Orbit is about to try — carefully, honestly, and only as far as the evidence allows.

SCENE 04 — Diamond world

First stop: a world that may be built differently from Earth at the deepest level — richer in carbon, under pressures that can favour exotic forms of carbon… including diamond-like material in models of some carbon-rich planets.
Say that slowly.
Not a jewellery advert. A planet where the chemistry of rock itself may lean toward carbon, and where extreme pressure can rearrange that carbon into structures we associate with gemstones.
On Earth, carbon mostly lives in softer forms — graphite in pencils, organic chains in life. Oxygen steals much of the bonding game in our crust. But if a planet forms with less oxygen and more carbon in its building blocks, the interior maths changes. Under enough pressure, carbon’s favourite shape can become a rigid crystal lattice — the same geometry we call diamond.
We have not walked there. We infer from star composition, planet mass, planet radius, and physics under crushing force. Some famous candidates and thought-experiments — worlds like the carbon-planet idea tied to systems such as fifty-five Cancri — made the public imagination race ahead of the photographs we do not have.
Is every glittering exoplanet a diamond beach? No. Models disagree. Some worlds may be carbon-rich but still hide their carbon in other minerals. Some may be gas-dominated giants with exotic chemistry we barely name yet.
That is fine… if we keep the honesty.
Orbit’s cream eyes drink the glitter anyway. Because even a careful model of a diamond-leaning crust is enough to rewrite what “ground” can mean.
Standing here would not be standing on granite. It would be standing on a chemistry lesson written in facets — hard, bright, and utterly alien to a world like Earth.
Alien worlds do not have to be alive to be astonishing. Sometimes the astonishment is geology wearing a tuxedo.

SCENE 05 — The planet where it rains glass sideways

Next: one of the strangest weather forecasts in astronomy.
There is a famous blue-ish hot world — often discussed with HD 189733 b — where models and observations paint a brutal picture: scorching heat, winds that can scream at thousands of kilometres per hour… and silicate particles that may condense and fall as a kind of glass rain.
Not gentle drizzle.
Sideways.
On Earth, rain falls down because gravity wins against soft air. On a world like this, the atmosphere is a runaway engine. Heat from a nearby star drives furious circulation. The dayside can be hot enough that minerals behave like vapour. Cooler regions — or shocked flows in the wind — can let those minerals condense into tiny solid grains.
Those grains are not water. They can be silicates — the same family of stuff that makes sand and glass on Earth — lofted, swept, and driven nearly horizontally in the jet-stream violence of a hot atmosphere.
Picture a storm where the droplets are microscopic shards, and the wind is a highway. That is the HD 189733 b story as many papers tell it: a hot Jupiter-class world so close to its star that “weather” becomes a chemistry experiment in motion.
Is every detail certain? No. Exoplanet atmospheres are hard. Instruments argue. Models improve. Headlines sometimes run faster than papers.
But the core wonder is solid enough to teach: alien weather is not Earth weather with a filter. It can be chemistry and dynamics pushed into a regime our umbrellas never prepared for.
Orbit files a mental note: do not open a picnic blanket here.
He also files a quieter note: worlds like this are why astronomers chase spectra. A blue planet is not automatically an ocean. Sometimes blue is hot air, scattering, and exotic clouds.

SCENE 06 — Three suns in the sky

Now a sky that breaks childhood drawings.
Some planets orbit in multi-star systems. Not one sun — a family. In the right architecture, a world could see more than one bright stellar disk in its sky… and cast more than one shadow.
Sunset becomes a negotiation. Noon becomes a chessboard of light. Your sense of “day” depends on which star dominates, and when.
If one star is close and bright, it may rule the heat. If another is distant and cool, it may add a second dawn while the first sun is still high. Shadows can overlap, split, or slide apart as the stars dance in their own wide orbits.
We have found planets in binary and multiple-star systems. Not every configuration is stable. Gravity is a jealous choreographer — stars can fling each other, strip planets, or squeeze orbits until a world cannot hold on.
But nature has already shown it can keep a planet dancing near more than one star for a long time.
Science fiction loved double suns long before the catalogue proved them ordinary. Tatooine’s twin sunset is not fantasy anymore. It is a category.
For Orbit, this is the moment the word “alien” stops meaning green and starts meaning geometry: the simple fact that your sky can have more than one furnace.
He hovers in the overlapping gold. Cream eyes soft. Antenna bright.
Home has one Sun. The catalogue has other arrangements.
And somewhere in those arrangements, a child — if life ever found a foothold — might draw three circles in crayon and call them all “day.”

SCENE 07 — Hot Jupiter: the hottest nights

Some exoplanets are gas giants parked shockingly close to their stars — hot Jupiters.
They can complete an orbit in days — sometimes less than two Earth days. Their daysides can be heated to furnace temperatures — thousands of degrees, hot enough to melt many solids. And because many are tidally influenced, they can keep one face roasted while the other faces the dark…
…except the dark is not cold comfort. Heat races through the atmosphere. Winds redistribute energy. The nightside can still glow. “Night” becomes a relative term.
These worlds helped rewrite textbooks. For a long time, our Solar System suggested giant planets live far out — Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune in the cold suburbs. Then the first hot Jupiters appeared in the data — huge, close, impossible-seeming… until they were simply true.
How did they get there? Migration is one leading idea: a giant forms farther out, then spirals inward through a disk of gas, or gets kicked by sibling planets. Another path: they form close and never leave. The debate is still live. The existence is not.
Some hot Jupiters are puffy — larger than models expect for their mass, inflated by relentless stellar heat. Some show tails of escaping atmosphere, bleeding into space. Some are so dark on the dayside they swallow light like charcoal, while their nights still shimmer.
Orbit learns a space fact the hard way: the strangest planets in the universe are sometimes strange because of address — not personality. Put a giant next to a star, and physics will invent weather we do not have words for.
Here, on the edge between glare and glow, the lesson is simple and huge: location is destiny. Same material as Jupiter. Different neighbourhood. Different story.

SCENE 08 — Eyeball planet

Then comes a world that looks, from space, like a staring eye.
A tidally locked planet near a smaller, cooler star may keep one face in permanent day and one in permanent night. The star’s gravity synchronises the spin — like our Moon always showing one face to Earth. Ice can claim the dark. Desert can claim the light. Between them: a ring of twilight — a possible sweet strip where temperatures might be less cruel.
Call it an eyeball planet. The name is informal. The geometry is serious.
On the dayside, rock can bake under endless noon. On the nightside, perpetual midnight can lock water and gases into ice that never sees a sunrise. But along the terminator — the line between — light arrives at a shallow angle, forever. Climate models suggest that strip can be the coolest, most stable band on the world.
This is where imagination and science shake hands carefully. We do not know which eyeball worlds — if any — host life. We do know tidal locking is a common fate in certain orbits, and that climate models for such worlds are some of the most interesting puzzles in modern astronomy.
Some researchers imagine oceans hugging the twilight ring — a liquid bracelet between fire and frost. Others warn that thick atmospheres or strong winds could smear heat around and blur the eyeball pattern. Data, not daydreams, will decide.
Orbit likes the metaphor too much to ignore it: a planet that never blinks… and a thin eyelid of maybe.
He stays in the ring a moment longer than the schedule allows.
Because this world is not a spectacle only. It is a question mark written in geography — a reminder that “habitable” might look nothing like Earth’s round green map.

SCENE 09 — Could any of these host life?

So… could any of these host life?
Honest answer: we do not know yet.
Diamond crusts and glass rain are not automatic homes. Hot Jupiters are brutal furnaces. Multi-star light can be beautiful and unstable for gentle climates. Eyeball twilight belts are promising in models — and still unproven as gardens.
What we can do is define the question better.
The habitable zone is the region around a star where, under the right conditions, a rocky world might keep liquid water on its surface. Not steam-only. Not ice-only. A range where water could pool.
It is not a guarantee. Venus sits near the inner edge of the Sun’s habitable zone in some diagrams — and it is a volcanic pressure cooker. Mars sits farther out — and it is a cold desert. Atmosphere matters. Magnetic fields matter. How the world formed matters.
The habitable zone is a starting line, not a finish line.
Then we look for clues: atmospheric gases out of balance… surface fingerprints… something that is hard to explain with rocks and sunlight alone.
On Earth, oxygen piled up in our air because life kept producing it faster than it was removed. Methane hangs around in our atmosphere in ways that would be odd without ongoing biology. Those are biosignature ideas — not proof templates, but conversation starters.
JWST and future observatories are already sniffing exoplanet air — measuring carbon dioxide, water vapour, methane, and more when a world transits. One day, a spectrum might show a combination that makes astronomers sit very still at their desks.
That is the quiet revolution of exoplanets explained in one sentence: we are learning to study the air of worlds we will never walk — not in this century, maybe — and still ask whether something breathes there.
Orbit’s eyes soften.
He does not need a handshake from an alien. He needs a true spectrum… and the patience to believe what it says.

SCENE 10 — What the catalogue teaches

Pull the journey into one thought.
Alien worlds are not a single idea. They are a catalogue of answers to a question Earth never asked itself loudly enough:
What else can a planet be?
A carbon-rich glittering crust.  
Sideways glass weather.  
A sky with three suns.  
Nights that still burn.  
A staring eyeball of day and ice.
None of that requires conspiracy. It requires telescopes, maths, doubt, and time.
Before nineteen ninety-five, exoplanets were a hope. Now they are a field. Kids born today may grow up with a number — thousands known — updating every year. They may never remember a time when we wondered whether other planets existed. They will wonder what those planets are like.
That shift is already happening.
And it requires a kind of courage: to let the universe be stranger than the stories we wrote before we could measure it.
The strangest planets in the universe are not waiting at the edge of imagination. Many are already in the data — waiting for better instruments to tell us their next secret.
