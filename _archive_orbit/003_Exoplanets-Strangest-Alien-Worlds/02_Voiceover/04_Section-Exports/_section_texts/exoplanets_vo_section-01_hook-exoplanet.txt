If alien worlds are real… what are they actually like? Diamond crusts? Glass rain? Three suns in the sky?
Today, Orbit is going on a tour.
Not a fantasy tour. A tour of exoplanets — real planets around other stars — and the strangest ones our instruments have found so far.
This is exoplanets explained the Orbit way: one world at a time, with the science we actually have… and the wonder we cannot help feeling.
Every point of light in that sky might be a sun. And around many of those suns, we now know, there are worlds. Not guesses on a poster. Measurements in a database.
Orbit is about to visit the strangest entries on that list — carefully, honestly, and only as far as the evidence allows.

An exoplanet is simply a planet that orbits a star other than our Sun.
For most of human history, every world we could point to lived in one address: the Solar System. Earth. Mars. Jupiter. Neighbours we could see, or at least visit with probes.
Then the map changed.
In nineteen ninety-two, astronomers confirmed planets around a pulsar — a dead, spinning star — a discovery so strange it almost did not count as “normal” planets in people’s minds. Then, in nineteen ninety-five, came fifty-one Pegasi b: a world around a Sun-like star. The first of a new era.
Astronomers started finding planets by the shadows they cast and the tiny tugs they give their stars. Not photographs of cities. Not messages. Tiny, careful clues — repeated, measured, argued over, and confirmed.
Today, thousands of exoplanets are known. Many more candidates wait in the data. The count climbs every year as telescopes sharpen and catalogues merge.
And the catalogue is not a list of Earth copies.
It is a museum of extremes.
Some are smaller than Earth. Some are many times larger than Jupiter. Some hug their stars so closely they complete an orbit in hours. Some wander alone in the dark, with no sun at all — rogue planets ejected from their birth systems.
Alien worlds are not one species. They are a whole vocabulary of what “planet” can mean when you stop assuming our Solar System is the template.
That is the first lesson Orbit wants you to carry: the universe did not read our textbooks before it built worlds.
